# Day 2. Clothed in goodness

*What is wrapped around you that you did not make?*

## The passage

> For He hath no despite of that He hath made, nor hath He any disdain to serve us at the simplest office that to our body belongeth in nature, for love of the soul that He hath made to His own likeness. … For as the body is clad in the cloth, and the flesh in the skin, and the bones in the flesh, and the heart in the whole, … so are we, soul and body, clad in the Goodness of God, and enclosed. Yea, and more homely: for all these may waste and wear away, but the Goodness of God is ever whole; and more near to us, without any likeness; for truly our Lover desireth that our soul cleave to Him with all its might, and that we be ever-more cleaving to His Goodness.
>
> — Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*, chapter 6 (ed. Grace Warrack, 1901)

## Reflection

Getting dressed on a walking morning is a small ceremony of layers. The thin one against the skin, then the shirt, then the warm one, then whatever keeps off the weather. Each layer has a job, and each one holds in the warmth of the one beneath. By the time the pack goes on you are wrapped in a sequence, and you do not think about it at all, until the day that one layer is missing.

Julian saw something very like this, and she put it into a sentence you can almost feel on your shoulders. The body is clad in cloth, the flesh in skin, the bones in flesh, the heart in the whole. Then comes the turn: so are we, soul and body, clad in the Goodness of God, and enclosed.

She does not stop at comfort, though. She says *more homely*, and she gives the reason: all those other coverings wear away. Cloth frays. Boots lose their soles. Skin cracks in the cold. The Goodness she means does not wear, because it is ever whole. Anyone who has watched a favourite jacket give out somewhere along the way will see the point. Everything you are wearing is on its way to being worn out. The covering she describes is not.

That is worth saying plainly to anyone who is wearing thin by now. A long road wears the walker, too: the knees, the patience, the mood. *Ever whole* has nothing to do with how you look or feel after days on foot. It is a statement about what is around you, not about what is in the mirror.

Before the cloth, though, there is a sentence that is easy to skim and worth a second look. God has no despite of what He has made, she writes, and no disdain *to serve us at the simplest office that to our body belongeth in nature*. The plainest needs of the body are not beneath notice. That is a surprising thing to find in a medieval book, and it is a comfort for a walker. Your feet, your hunger, your thirst, your need for a bed and a warm drink are not a distraction from the serious business. They are not too small to be held.

Now for the hard places. The word "enclosed" is the first. A person who walks for the sky, the long view and the open road may not love the word. Being enclosed can sound like being shut in. Julian lived enclosed, by her own choice, in a small room beside a church, and she uses the word for something closer to warmth than to walls: the way a body is enclosed in its own warmth, the way a child is enclosed in an arm. But if the word jars, say so, and read it as held.

The second hard place is a bigger one. To be held is not the same as being spared. Julian was gravely ill when this was shown to her. Later, in chapter 15, she writes that God *keepeth us even alike secure in woe and in weal*. She does not promise that the rain will stay off. The wrapping is not a roof. She says that you are kept in the rain, and in the sun, in equal measure, and she does not explain how. If that sounds thin, it is allowed to sound thin. She was not writing it from comfort.

And there is the plain difficulty of being held at all. Most people who walk a long road like to feel capable. You carry your own pack. You choose your own pace. To think of yourself as clad and carried, with little part in it, can feel like a loss of dignity. Notice that nothing in the passage asks you to do anything. The body does not dress itself in its skin. It is simply in it. The only thing left for you is to notice.

So what does it ask of an ordinary day? To pay attention, for a moment, to the layers. To feel the weight of a coat you did not weave, on a road other people made, past fields other people kept, towards a bed that someone made up. Almost nothing in your day was made by you alone. That is not an accusation. It is another way of saying that you are wrapped up in a good deal more than you can see.

Julian calls God *our Lover*, here and elsewhere. Read it as an old word for someone who loves, someone whose whole wish is that you stay close to the good. Take as much of that as is useful this morning. The cloth, at any rate, is yours to feel.

## For the walk

- What is wrapped around me this morning that I did not make, and what of it is wearing thin?
- At the first rest, when you put a layer on or take one off, pause with it in your hands. Name the sequence slowly, cloth, skin, flesh, bone, heart, and rest a hand on your chest on the last one. Then put the layer on, and notice that it was already warm. It takes less than a minute, and nobody need see.

## At the end of the day

Nobody at the end of a long walk wants a lecture, but a blanket is another matter. Wherever you sleep tonight, there is probably one: a rough grey wool one, a sleeping bag, a duvet that smells of someone else's washing powder. The bed does not ask whether you earned it. It takes your weight.

Before that come the evening chores, and Julian has something to say about them. God, she wrote, has no disdain to serve us at the simplest office that belongs to the body. You know the simplest offices by now. Feet washed and looked over. Socks rinsed and hung up. Boots stuffed with paper, if there is any. A drink of water, a plate of something hot. They are dull, and they are the whole upkeep of a walker. Do them slowly tonight. They are part of what holds you, and they count.

Think back over the day and ask where you were held. It need not be grand. A wall that you leaned on for five minutes. A bench someone put there. A set of waymarks that carried you through a place you could not have found. A stranger's directions. The fact that your legs went on working when you stopped asking them to. These are the cloth, and the skin, and the flesh.

Ask also where you tried to hold everything up yourself. Perhaps you spent an hour carrying a worry as if the road depended on it. Perhaps you walked too fast to prove something. There is a kind of tiredness that comes less from the kilometres than from being the only support. Nothing in Julian says you were wrong to try. She says only that the support was there, and did not wear.

Be honest, too, about the day's cold spots. Some part of today may have felt unwrapped: a long wet stretch, a loneliness at four in the afternoon, a message that landed badly. Being held, in her sense, did not make those hours easy, and that does not have to be proved tonight. If the day did not feel held, you can leave the sentence unsaid and read the prayer as a wish.

If you are sleeping in a shared room, the dark is full of other people being held in just the same way, each in a bag or under a blanket, each taking up exactly as much room as they need. You will not meet most of them. It is pleasant to know they are there.

If the room is stuffy and the word enclosed feels too close tonight, open a window if you can. Julian herself lived enclosed, and she chose the word for the goodness around her, not for walls. It is nearer to the way the air is round you, unnoticed until it is gone.

And if the day did feel safe, let it. A good day does not need a reason.

Three questions for the warm dark:

- Where today did I let something hold me, a wall, a stranger, a roof?
- What was I carrying alone that I could have set down?
- What, tonight, is simply keeping me warm?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I am wrapped in more than I made. Thank you for the cloth and the roof and the hands behind them. Hold me in the rain as in the sun. Let what wears out wear out gently. Let me be warm enough to rest.*
