# Day 6. Prayer that tastes of nothing

*Does it count when it feels like nothing?*

## The passage

> But yet oftentimes our trust is not full: for we are not sure that God heareth us, as we think because of our unworthiness, and because we feel right nought, (for we are as barren and dry oftentimes after our prayers as we were afore); and this, in our feeling our folly, is cause of our weakness. … *I am Ground of thy beseeching: first it is my will that thou have it; and after, I make thee to will it; and after, I make thee to beseech it and thou beseechest it. How should it then be that thou shouldst not have thy beseeching?* … *Pray inwardly, … though thee thinketh it savour thee not: for it is profitable, though thou feel not, though thou see nought; yea, though thou think thou canst not. For in dryness and in barrenness, in sickness and in feebleness, then is thy prayer well-pleasant to me, though thee thinketh it savour thee nought but little. And so is all thy believing prayer in my sight.*
>
> — Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*, chapter 41 (ed. Grace Warrack, 1901)

## Reflection

By the sixth day of a walk, most people have stopped pretending to themselves about at least one thing. Perhaps it is the pace. Perhaps it is the weight of the pack, or the real reason they set out. For some it is also the matter of prayer, or whatever stands in its place: the stillness at the start of the day that you meant to keep and have kept, perhaps, for two mornings. You stand at a gate, the world is quiet, and you feel nothing at all.

If that is where you are, the person who wrote today's passage has been there. Look at how she begins. Our trust is often not full, she says, because we are not sure we are heard, and because we feel *right nought*. We are as barren and dry after our prayers as we were before them. Then, in the very next sentence: *For thus have I felt in myself.* She is not talking about other people. She has been dry herself.

What comes next is the part that has made the chapter loved. She is given a sentence: *I am Ground of thy beseeching.* The wish to pray, the little pull toward stillness that you may be feeling this morning, is not a thing you manufactured. The order she is given is this: first it is God's will that you should have it, then you are made to want it, then you are made to ask for it, and you ask. The asking is the last link in a chain that began somewhere else. The wanting is already part of the answer. And so comes a question that is almost playful, and very tender: how could it be that you should not have what you ask for?

Read plainly, it says that the faintest wish counts. You do not have to feel anything. You do not have to produce a state of mind. Perhaps all you did this morning was look at the sky and want something, without a word for it. That is what she means by *beseeching*, and that is the thing she was shown.

Then the second half is a kind of permission slip. Pray inwardly, she says, *though thee thinketh it savour thee not*: though it tastes of nothing. It is profitable though you feel nothing, see nothing, and think you cannot. In dryness and barrenness, in sickness and feebleness, that is when your prayer is well-pleasing. She is not saying this to people who feel fine. She is saying it to people who are dry, ill and weak, and she means them to hear it as welcome.

For a walker there is something close to home here. A long road has its own dry places: chapped lips, a throat that wants water, a patience worn thin. A prayer that tastes of nothing is not a failure of the prayer. It is what one would expect in a tired and weathered creature on a long road. She does not say that walking is prayer. She says that dry, unfelt attention is welcome, and what counts as that, for you, is for you to say.

Now the hard places. The first is a simple one: all this assumes that someone is on the other end, and if you are not sure of that, the passage does not make it so. You can still take the human part from it. Wanting counts. A faint turning toward what you need is real, whether or not you have a name for what it turns to.

The second is harder. *How should it then be that thou shouldst not have thy beseeching?* There are people who have asked, with their whole hearts, for something that did not come: a recovery, a reconciliation, a life. For them, that question may feel like a slap. Julian saw the difficulty. In the next chapter she writes that when we have prayed a long time and think we have not been given what we asked for, we should not be in heaviness, because *either we abide a better time, or more grace, or a better gift*. It may not satisfy you. It is what she was given, and she passes it on as she received it, without dressing it up.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Less than you might think. The smallest possible prayer: a breath, a name, a word like *here*. And the willingness to leave it without feedback. The door of a locked church, a gate in a wall, a quiet bit of path: any of these is enough. You do not have to feel that it worked.

## For the walk

- If the wish to pray, or just to be still, is itself a gift, what is it asking for right now?
- At the next gate, wall or milestone, stop for one minute with no words. If you want one, use a single word, *here*, on the out-breath. If nothing comes, count the minute as done and walk on. It counts either way.

## At the end of the day

It is easy to fall asleep in the middle of a prayer. It is an old joke, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. A tired body prays badly, and the passage names feebleness as one of the times when prayer is well-pleasing. If the first thing you do tonight is yawn, you have started well.

Go back through the day and look for the moments that were closest to praying, without being called that. It may have been the first sight of a valley opening out. It may have been a half-minute of complete quiet in a doorway. It may have been an irritation turned into a plea: please, let this hill end. These are not nothing. They are the same wanting that Julian is talking about, in work clothes.

Then look at the dry stretches, the hours where you felt nothing at all. Today may have been mostly one long dry stretch. Say so, and be honest about it. The passage does not ask you to be grateful for the dryness. It says that dry prayer is welcome *in dryness and in barrenness*, not instead of it. You are allowed to wish it had been otherwise.

If you are carrying a request that has not been answered, the evening can be a very quiet time for it. You need not argue with it or give it up. Julian's own words are that the answer may be a better time, or more grace, or a better gift, and she leaves it open which. You do not have to choose tonight.

Prayer, at the end of a day like this, can be very small. The whole of it may be the word you whispered at the gate this morning, said once more with your eyes closed.

- What did I want today, underneath everything else I wanted?
- Where did I feel nothing, and could I let that be enough?
- Is there a request I am carrying that I could hold more loosely tonight?

## A short prayer

*Here I am, and I have little to say. Take the wish for the words. Take the dry hours with the rest. Be near whoever I cannot help. Let me sleep inside whatever I could not pray.*
