# Day 4. The great hurt

*What do you do with a question that has no answer yet?*

## The passage

> But in this I stood beholding things general, troublously and mourning, saying thus to our Lord in my meaning, with full great dread: *Ah! good Lord, how might all be well, for the great hurt that is come, by sin, to the creature?* And here I desired, as far as I durst, to have some more open declaring wherewith I might be eased in this matter. … Another understanding is this, that there be deeds evil done in our sight, and so great harms taken, that it seemeth to us that it were impossible that ever it should come to good end. And upon this we look, sorrowing and mourning therefor, so that we cannot resign us unto the blissful beholding of God as we should do. And the cause of this is that the use of our reason is now so blind, so low, and so simple, that we cannot know that high marvellous Wisdom, the Might and the Goodness of the blissful Trinity.
>
> — Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*, chapters 29 and 32 (ed. Grace Warrack, 1901)

## Reflection

There is a kind of morning when the sky is clear, the path is dry, the birds are going about their business, and you are carrying something that none of it touches. A message from home. A diagnosis. A name that comes into the mind and will not leave. The road is lovely and the heart is somewhere else. You may walk through country that deserves your whole attention and feel nothing, and then feel guilty for feeling nothing.

Julian knew that heaviness. This is the day to say so plainly, because if the book were to hurry past it, it would not be worth carrying.

Here is where she stands in the book. She has just been told, in words that have since become famous, that all shall be well. We will come to that tomorrow. But she does not leave the matter there, and she does not nod and move on. She stands, in her words, *beholding things general, troublously and mourning*, and she asks the question that is in the back of almost every mind: how can it be? Look at the great hurt that has come into the world. How could all be well?

Notice that she dares to ask. She says she desired an answer *as far as I durst*, which is an honest little phrase: she asked as far as she dared, and would have liked to ask further. And she puts the question in the plainest language she has.

Then she says something even more candid, in the second half of the passage. There are deeds evil done in our sight, and harms so great, that it seems impossible they could ever come to a good end. And when we look at them, we sorrow so much that we cannot settle ourselves to look at God. She makes no attempt to wave that away. She does not call the harms small, or say that they only seem bad. She calls them great and she says the sorrow they cause is real enough to block the view.

What she offers instead of an answer is an admission of limits. The cause of our not knowing, she says, is that our reason is so blind, so low and so simple that it cannot take in the Wisdom, Might and Goodness she is talking about. That is not an explanation of suffering. It is a confession that she does not have one. In her book's own terms, the answer exists, and it is hidden.

A word about "sin", since it is her word. We tend to hear it as private guilt. Julian does not mean only that. When she writes the word, she says in chapter 27, it brought to her mind *all that is not good*, and the pains of every creature, bodily and spiritual. She is looking at the whole sorrow of the world, and her own small part in it. She lived in hard times. The edition's introduction speaks of oppression, riot and pestilence in Norwich itself, and of wars further off, so her question was not an idle one.

There are harder sayings in these chapters, and it would be dishonest to pretend that there are not. She writes in chapter 27 that pain, as she sees it, serves a purpose for a time, *for it purgeth*. It is a thing to say to oneself, if one says it at all, and never to someone else in pain. In chapter 32 she turns to what her Church taught about those who are lost, and says that it seemed to her impossible that all could be well. We do not follow her down that road in this book. It is there in the text, and she does not hide it.

What does this ask of an ordinary day? First, permission. You are allowed to carry a question that has no answer, and to say so, and you are not obliged to dress it up as faith or as despair. Second, a little company for it. She says in chapter 28 that *each kind compassion that man hath on his even-Christians with charity, it is Christ in him*. Her word is fellow-Christians, and you may stretch it as far as it will go. The thing it describes is simple. When you feel for another person's hurt and do something kind about it, that is a great deal.

If you can do nothing at all today, and the question stays the size it was at breakfast, walk anyway. The question can walk with you.

## For the walk

- If I dared, as Julian dared, what would I ask this morning?
- Say that question once, aloud, under your breath if there is someone near. Do not answer it. If it is too big to say, say the first word of it, or only its shape. Then walk for ten minutes noticing only what is in front of you: the sound of your boots, the colour of a wall. If a kind thought about someone else comes, follow it a little way.

## At the end of the day

Some days have no neat shape. Today may be one of them. You may have carried something heavy from breakfast to dusk and set it down in the same place you picked it up, no lighter and no clearer. Nothing here is going to tell you that this was wrong.

If it was a heavy day, say what it was heavy with. It need not be a sentence for anyone else. A name, or a word, or the sound of a phone not ringing. It helps, sometimes, to hear it in your own voice, in the dark, with nobody to correct it. Julian did something like this. She put her question to the One she was addressing, in dread, but in plain words.

Then look for the other thing. Even on the worst days there are usually moments of kindness, and they are easy to miss when you are looking for something else. A hand held out over a stile. A woman who asked how far you had come, and waited for the answer. A sentence in a text. These do not prove that all is well, and they are not meant to. They are what a little compassion looks like when it is in the room.

On a road with few other walkers, a hurt has no one to interrupt it. It can have the whole day to itself. If today was that kind of day, the quiet was not a verdict on you or on the pain. It was only space, and space can be used for something else tomorrow.

If you have been blaming yourself for not feeling better, for walking through a lovely morning with a closed heart, you may put that down as well. Nothing in these chapters blames you. Julian mourned over the hurt of the world, and the answer she was given, as tomorrow's passage will show, came without blame.

At night a hurt tends to grow, as things do in the dark. If it wakes you at three, there is nothing to be solved at that hour. Say the name or the word once, and turn over. In the morning it will be the same size, or a little smaller, and the road will still be there. If you are tired as well as heavy, put the heaviness down for the night, as you would put down the pack. It is not a failure of love to sleep.

If there is someone who could carry it with you for an hour, a friend, a priest, a stranger at the next table, the evening is a good time to think about asking. Julian writes, in chapter 28, that *we suffer not alone but with Him*. That is her faith speaking, and you may hear it as you can. The plain human version is also true: a hurt that is shared is lighter.

- What did I carry today that I could not mend?
- Where did I see, or do, something kind, however small?
- Who might I let carry this with me, even for an hour?

## A short prayer

*I have a question, and tonight there is no answer. Hear it anyway. Be with whoever is hurting, near or far, and with me. Let me be kind where I can. Let the dark be gentle, and the morning not too sudden.*
