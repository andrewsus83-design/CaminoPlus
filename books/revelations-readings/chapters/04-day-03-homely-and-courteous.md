# Day 3. Homely and courteous

*Who has stooped to your level on this road, and who have you stooped to meet?*

## The passage

> And of all the sight it was most comfort to me that our God and Lord that is so reverend and dreadful, is so homely and courteous: and this most fulfilled me with comfort and assuredness of soul. … It is the most worship that a solemn King or a great Lord may do a poor servant if he will be homely with him, and specially if he sheweth it *himself*, of a full true meaning, and with a glad cheer, both privately and in company. Then thinketh this poor creature thus: *And what might this noble Lord do of more worship and joy to me than to shew me that am so simple this marvellous homeliness? Soothly it is more joy and pleasance to me than [if] he gave me great gifts and were himself strange in manner.* … For our courteous Lord willeth that we should be as homely with Him as heart may think or soul may desire. But [let us] beware that we take not so recklessly this homeliness as to leave courtesy. For our Lord Himself is sovereign homeliness, and as homely as He is, so courteous He is: for He is very courteous.
>
> — Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*, chapters 7 and 77 (ed. Grace Warrack, 1901)

## Reflection

Think of a door at the end of a long afternoon. It is a plain door, in a village you have never seen, and behind it is someone whose job, or whose habit, is to take in strangers. Within a minute the whole day is rearranged. You are asked your name. A glass of water appears. You are shown where the bed is, where the boots go, where the kettle lives. You came in as a tired stranger and in the space of a few sentences you are a guest.

What matters about that minute is not the water. It is the manner. Someone with a house and a say in it has decided, for no reason except kindness, to treat you as one of the household.

Julian had been looking at something dreadful, in the old sense of the word: a vision so overwhelming that she calls it both horrifying and sweet. And out of the whole sight, she says, the most comfort was this: that God, who is so *reverend and dreadful*, is also *homely and courteous*. "Homely", in her English, means at home, familiar, one of the house. "Courteous" means gracious and gently considerate.

Then she gives an example, and it is a good one. A great lord, she says, can do no higher honour to a poor servant than to be homely with him, and above all to do it himself, with a true meaning and a glad face, in private and in company. The servant's thought is the part worth stopping at. What more could this noble lord do to honour so simple a servant than to show him this marvellous homeliness? It is more joy to him than a heap of gifts would be, if the lord stayed distant in manner.

You can feel the difference between a gift and a welcome. A cold parcel left on a step is a gift. A person who sits down opposite you with the same soup is something else. On a long road you may meet both, and you will know which one you are carrying home.

The next part is easy to miss, and it is the courtesy. Julian, who has spoken of how close and easy God can be, adds a check in chapter 77: beware that we take this homeliness so recklessly as to leave courtesy. As homely as He is, she says, so courteous He is. Nearness and good manners come together. Anyone who has stayed in other people's houses knows that the best guests are the ones who feel at home and still tidy up.

Not everything here sits easily. The picture itself is the first snag. It is a lord and a servant, a medieval picture, and some readers will hear only the ranks in it and not like them. What Julian takes from it is the stooping, not the ranking. The servant is not honoured by being told where he stands, but by being met.

The second is that you may not feel it. Some days the road is not courteous at all. A shop is shut when you need it. A path has been churned to mud. A face you hoped would be kind is not. Julian is not describing the courtesy of the world. She is also careful to say that this was her sight, and she wrote *as to my sight* often enough that it is hard to miss: she is telling you what she saw, not telling you what you must feel.

And there is a question under the whole picture, which is whether you can be that kind of person yourself. It would be a poor reading of the passage that stopped at being on the receiving end. If the greatest thing a lord can do is to be homely and glad of heart, then the same is true at every level. Courtesy is one of the few things a tired walker can still give away.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? A small, steady politeness. A first greeting, before the other person has made theirs. A thank-you that is said looking at the person. A name remembered. A room left a little better than you found it. And, now and then, the nerve to be at ease: to accept the bowl, to sit at the table, to let someone be glad to have you.

## For the walk

- Who has been homely with me in the last day, treating me as one of the house, and did I notice at the time?
- Be courteous on purpose, once, to someone who will never know it was on purpose. Say good morning first, or *Buen Camino* if there is someone to say it to. Look at the person as you do. And if no one is there, say a thank you to the road, aloud, in a plain voice. Then notice how it feels to have done it for no reason.

## At the end of the day

A shared table is a great leveller. Whatever you were at breakfast, tonight you are someone with a fork and a plate, in a room with a few others who have also come a long way. If you are in a village where you are the only walker, the table may be for one, and the kindness may only be a nod across a counter. That counts. Think about the people who made the room you are sitting in, and what they must have done to have it ready for a stranger.

Run through the day and find the stoopings. Who bent down to your level? It might have been a host with a key and a smile, or a message that came at just the right time, or a fellow walker who slowed down a little so that you did not have to ask. Some of these will be so small that you have to look twice. They were still a lord being homely.

Then turn the question round. Where did you do it for someone else? And where did you hold back, from tiredness or irritation or plain forgetfulness? Courtesy is the first thing to go at the end of a hard day, and a stranger may remember it. If you were short with someone, a sentence tomorrow morning will mend more than a long apology tonight.

In a shared room, courtesy has a small repertoire, and it is all done in the dark. A head torch pointed at the floor and not at faces. A bag packed the night before, so that it does not have to crackle at half past five. A door closed softly. Nobody thanks you for any of it, which is exactly what makes it courtesy.

On a road with few walkers, a host may have been expecting you, with the lights on and a meal planned. To be expected is a courtesy before it is anything else. It is worth noticing, and worth a phone call if you are going to be late.

If you have a host to thank, tomorrow morning is the time. A name used once, a hand shaken, a word about the bed. It takes a few seconds, and it is the other half of what the host did. They stooped, and you say that you noticed.

Be fair to yourself as well. Julian's two words, homely and courteous, hold something you can turn on your own tired self. Be at home in your own skin tonight, and be gentle with it. A walker who has been sharp with their own feet all day has not been courteous to the ones that carried them.

If none of this came to mind, that is also an answer. Some days are plain, and a plain day can still end with a clean bed.

- Who made me feel at home today, and what did they actually do?
- Where was I short on courtesy, with someone else or with myself?
- Whom could I be homely with tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for every door that opened today. Thank you for the hands that made the beds. Teach me the manners of someone who is glad. Let me be at home where I am a guest, and kind where I am the stranger. Bless the house I sleep in, and whoever keeps it.*
