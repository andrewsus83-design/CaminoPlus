# How to use this book

This is a small, quiet book for eight days. It is made to go with the Camino de Invierno, the Winter Way, which the app lists as running from Ponferrada to Santiago de Compostela, about 260 km. *Invierno* is the Spanish word for winter. The name belongs to the road, and you can read the book in any season, on the road or at home.

The app's own route notes call it a road with few pilgrims, and they advise planning where you will sleep ahead. That is worth knowing here. A quiet road gives you long hours alone, and some evenings in a village where you may be the only walker at the table. This book is made for those hours. It is quiet and consoling, and it does not talk much. It keeps you company and lets you get on.

## A day

Every day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**The morning** has three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from *Revelations of Divine Love*, a book written in English in the fourteenth century by Julian of Norwich. The words are exactly as they stand in the 1901 edition we use, old forms and all. Read the passage twice, the second time aloud if you can, or under your breath. Old English slows the eye, and that is half of its use.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Julian's. It begins with something from the road, a coat, a head torch, a door, and lets the passage open it. It says plainly what the passage says. It says where the passage is hard, and it is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour, and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. You will not need a notebook. Most of them are better done on your feet.

**The evening** has two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back over the day with the morning's passage in mind. It names what may have been hard and what may have been good, and it ends with two or three gentle questions. You do not have to answer them in writing. They are for lying down with.
- **A short prayer.** Original, and short. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

Julian was a Christian of the fourteenth century, and she writes as one. She speaks of God, of Christ, of the Church and of sin in her own medieval terms, and she hopes that her readers will take them as she does. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All are welcome here. You do not need to share her belief to recognise what she noticed about being small, being held, being afraid, and being tired. Where she is hard to follow, we say so.

## The shape of the eight days

The days follow a walk. They do not follow the order of her book.

- **Day 1.** The smallness of things.
- **Day 2.** Being held.
- **Day 3.** The courtesy of God.
- **Day 4.** Sin and suffering, told honestly.
- **Day 5.** "All shall be well".
- **Day 6.** The way of prayer.
- **Day 7.** The long patience.
- **Day 8.** The end, and the beginning.

At an ordinary pace of 20 to 25 km a day, about 260 km is roughly two weeks, give or take. Eight days will not match every day of such a walk, and they do not need to. Read a day when it suits you: on a rest day, on a day of rain, on a day when the road is hard. Repeat one that helped. Read two at once if you want. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off. At home it works the same way: the morning with your tea, the evening before bed.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, sometimes with a shared kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well. Julian's own words are explained where they first come up. She says *shewing* for what we would call a vision, and *homely* for something close to "at home".

Read slowly. The book was made to be read a few lines at a time.
