# About the book and its author

## The woman

*Revelations of Divine Love* was written by Julian, an anchoress at Norwich, as the title page of our edition says. An anchoress was a woman who had chosen to live shut in a small room built against a church, given over to prayer. The edition's introduction describes such rooms as having a window into the church, so that she could follow its services, and another to the outside world, for those who came to speak with her.

Very little is known about her outer life. She does not give her name in the book. An old note quoted in the edition says that her name was Julian and that she was a recluse at Norwich. She calls herself *a simple creature unlettered*. From her own words the edition's editor works out that she was born about 1342, since Julian says she was *thirty years old and a half* in May 1373. We do not know when she died. The edition quotes an old note that has her still living in 1442, and modern accounts usually place her last record at about 1416. We have not tried to settle it.

## The book

Julian says that her showings came to her on the thirteenth of May, 1373. She was gravely ill and thought she was dying. She tells us that fifteen showings lasted from about four o'clock in the morning until after nine, and that a sixteenth followed the next night. She wrote the book in English, and she says that *fifteen years after, and more* she was answered as to what they meant. The edition's introduction reckons from her words that the book cannot have been finished before about 1393.

In this edition the book has 86 short chapters. Most of it concerns the suffering of Christ, which she describes plainly. Around that centre she writes about sin, about the Church, about prayer, and about a comfort she believed was meant for everyone: *we are all one in comfort*, she says. She also says, early on, that in all things she believes *as Holy Church believeth, preacheth, and teacheth*.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

Julian says little about roads. Yet the book is made of things a walker meets: a small round thing in the palm of the hand, the clothes you wear, a great lord and a poor servant, a child that has fallen and runs to its mother, a light in the night. It is a quiet book, and its theme, in the introduction's words, is comfort.

It is also hard in places. She writes in the terms of her Church, with sin, penance, hell and the Fiend all on the page. The passages here are the ones a general reader can follow, and the reflections say where she is difficult. We have left aside the long chapters on the Passion. She says that as truly as God is our Father, God is our Mother (chapter 59). We have not chosen a passage for that, but you will meet it if you read on.

## The edition, and what we did with it

The English in the passages is from *Revelations of Divine Love*, in the edition of Grace Warrack, published in 1901. The title page calls it *A version from the MS. in the British Museum*. The edition's notes say what that means. Julian's English book survives in two manuscripts, one in Paris from the sixteenth century and one in the British Museum, in seventeenth-century handwriting, called the Sloane manuscript. The editor transcribed the Sloane manuscript. She divided it into paragraphs, added capital letters and varied the punctuation. To make a book for general use she adopted modern spelling and put most words no longer in use into modern English. She kept a few old words and nearly all the old grammar. She says that her rule was never to omit a word of the manuscript, and that the few words she added are in square brackets.

That is what the edition says of itself. We have not seen the manuscripts. What we use is a modernised text, not Julian's fourteenth-century English, and we rely on the editor for how closely it follows the manuscript. Scholars today know a shorter account of the same showings. It was not known when this edition was made, and it is not used here.

The edition is in the public domain, and the text is on Project Gutenberg as ebook number 52958. Every quotation in this book was checked against it, word for word. We quote only Julian's own text, never the editor's introduction, notes or glossary.

In the passages we changed no words. The old spellings stay, *shewed* and *hazel-nut* and *all-thing*, and so do the editor's square brackets where she added a word. We left out the small footnote numbers. A mark like this, …, shows a gap between two stretches. In three passages, on Days 2, 5 and 6, the mark falls in the middle of a sentence. That is where the edition printed a footnote number, and nothing of Julian's is missing there. The choice of the eight passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours. The passages come from eleven of her chapters: 5, 6, 7, 27, 29, 31, 32, 41, 77, 83 and 86.
