# Day 3. The snare is broken

*What do you say after a narrow escape?*

## The passage

> If it had not been Yahweh who was on our side, let Israel now say, if it had not been Yahweh who was on our side, when men rose up against us; then they would have swallowed us up alive, when their wrath was kindled against us; then the waters would have overwhelmed us, the stream would have gone over our soul; then the proud waters would have gone over our soul. Blessed be Yahweh, who has not given us as a prey to their teeth. Our soul has escaped like a bird out of the fowler's snare. The snare is broken, and we have escaped. Our help is in the name of Yahweh, who made heaven and earth.
>
> — Psalm 124:1 to 8 (World English Bible)

> Those who trust in Yahweh are as Mount Zion, which can't be moved, but remains forever. As the mountains surround Jerusalem, so Yahweh surrounds his people from this time forth and forevermore.
>
> — Psalm 125:1 to 2 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

Sooner or later a walk has a narrow place in it. A stretch of road with a car coming too fast and no verge. A wet stone that turns under the foot. A sky that darkens faster than the forecast said it would, or a stream higher than you expected. Nothing happens, you get through, and ten minutes later your hands are still a little shaky. What do you say, after that?

Psalm 124 is a song for the other side of a narrow place. Its first words are in the past tense: *If it had not been Yahweh who was on our side.* That "if" is the grammar of people who got through. And look how it insists. The poet says it once, then says to the whole people, *let Israel now say*, and then says it again. Gratitude here is not only felt. It is said, aloud, together, and the saying is part of how the rescue is remembered.

The dangers come in four pictures, and each is a way that fear feels. Being swallowed alive. Being drowned by water that goes over the head. Being torn by teeth. Being trapped. Then the last picture is the lightest of all: *Our soul has escaped like a bird out of the fowler's snare.* And notice how the sentence is built. The bird does not break the snare. *The snare is broken*, and the bird is simply out. All the credit goes away from the one who escaped. The song closes on a line you will meet again before the book is over: *Our help is in the name of Yahweh, who made heaven and earth.*

What might that ask of an ordinary day? A small act. When you have got through a narrow place, stop, even for ten seconds, and say thank you out loud. Who it is addressed to can stay vague. The saying matters more than the grammar.

There are two hard places today. The first is the very form of the poem. "If it had not been" is the language of survivors. It cannot be said by those who were not rescued, or about them, and the psalm does not say what to say to the ones the flood took. We will not pretend that it does. If you are walking because someone did not come through something, today may be a hard day. It is fine to read only the second psalm, or to put the page down.

The second is in Psalm 125, in the verses left out of the passage. Verses 3 to 5 sort the world into the upright and the crooked, and promise that the crooked will be led away with those who do wrong. Real lives are not so tidy, and the line between the two runs through every person, you included. Read it, if you read it, as a hope that the rule of cruelty will not last for ever, and keep it away from the person snoring in the next bunk.

What stays on the page is better. After the water, the mountains. *As the mountains surround Jerusalem, so Yahweh surrounds his people.* On the first day the hills were a question, threat or hope. Here they have an answer. The poet looks round at the hills that ring the city and sees no trap, only a wall that guards. And the ones who trust are like a mountain that *can't be moved*. That does not mean never shaken. It means staying where you are, with weight in the right place.

A mountain that surrounds you can also hide the weather, and a psalm is not a forecast. This is the calm part, so read it plainly. Before a high or long day, look at the forecast the evening before. Start early, so that you are down from the high ground before the afternoon. Carry water and food and a warm layer you hope not to need. Tell someone where you are going. Turn back if the day looks wrong: there is no shame in turning back. The app's notes say that services are sparse in places on this road, so know where your next water and food are. If you are in trouble, call 112, which works across Spain and France. Trust and a forecast do not compete. Do both.

## For the walk

- What is one narrow place I have come through, on this road or in my life, that I have never thanked anyone for?
- Before the hardest or highest stretch of the day, stop and check three things: water, weather, and who knows where you are. Go on. When it is behind you, stop again and say "thank you" aloud, to whoever or whatever you like, even to the path itself. If the day brings no narrow place at all, say it for the ones that did not come.

## At the end of the day

Think of the narrowest place you passed today. It may not have been dramatic. A narrow bridge, a road with no shoulder, a descent on loose stones, an awkward conversation. Whatever it was, you came through it, or you would not be sitting here.

The psalm teaches a habit: go back over the day and say "if it had not been". If it had not been for the foot that held, the driver who moved over, the water bottle that was full, the text that arrived in time, the stranger who pointed out the turn. Most of the things that could have gone wrong did not, and they passed so quietly that you did not notice. Say them now, one by one, and let each be a small thank you.

But some things did go wrong. A blister, a wrong turn, a wasted hour, a sharp word. The psalm has nothing to say about those, and it is an honest silence. Not every day is a rescue. Some days are only a wound, and you tend it. A day of small harms is not a day without help. It may only be a day when the help was less dramatic.

Tiredness plays tricks about danger. When you are tired you can magnify a small risk into a threat, or ignore a real one because you are too weary to care. Neither is fair to you. Check the plain facts instead. What does tomorrow's forecast say? Where is the next water? Does anyone know where you will be? These are small acts of care, and they sit comfortably beside the psalm.

The psalm asks for the thanks to be said, *let Israel now say*, and not only felt. So say one thank-you aloud before you sleep, quietly if the room is asleep: to the ceiling, to the day, to whoever you have in mind. It may feel foolish for a moment. It does not feel foolish for long.

Is there a snare you are still in? A loop of worry that went round all afternoon, or a remark you keep replaying. The psalm's bird did not break the snare itself, and you cannot break yours by thinking harder. Put the loop down for the night and go to sleep without solving it. Things usually look looser in the morning.

And think of being surrounded. Tonight you are among other sleepers, in a village you do not know, with walls around you and hills beyond the walls. You are, quite simply, held by a good deal that you did not arrange.

- What went right today that could easily have gone otherwise, and have I said so to anyone?
- Where did I feel hemmed in today, and what, looked at again, was on my side?
- What do I need to know about tomorrow before I sleep?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the foot that held and the car that moved over. Thank you for the water when I needed it. For those who did not come through, I have no words, only this quiet. Keep me careful and keep me calm. Let tomorrow's weather be kind and my choices wise.*
