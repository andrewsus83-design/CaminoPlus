# About the Psalms of Ascent

## The Psalms

The Book of Psalms is a collection of 150 poems, songs and prayers in the Hebrew Bible, which Christians call the Old Testament. Its Hebrew name, *Tehillim*, means "praises", though many of the poems are complaints, and many are both. The collection falls into five books, and the songs of this book are in the last of them, which runs from Psalm 107 to Psalm 150.

Tradition gave many psalms to David, the king. Others carry the names of different singers, and many carry no name at all. Scholars differ on how old the poems are and how the collection grew. Many think the collection took its final shape in the centuries after the return from exile in Babylon (about the sixth century BC), with some poems older than that. This book claims nothing more precise.

## The Songs of Ascents

Fifteen psalms, 120 to 134, carry the same heading. In Hebrew it is *shir ha-ma'alot*, which is usually put into English as a song of ascents, or of steps, or of going up. Four of the fifteen are headed with David's name (122, 124, 131 and 133) and one with Solomon's (127). The other ten name nobody. Whether the headings are the poets' own or were added later, scholars weigh differently.

What the heading means, and how the songs were used, are also open questions. The most familiar answer, and the one this book takes as a starting point, is that they are traditionally understood as songs pilgrims sang while going up to Jerusalem for the great festivals. There are others. An early Jewish tradition, written down in the Mishnah (about AD 200), speaks of fifteen steps in the Temple, one for each song, on which the Levites sang. Some link the word to the going up from exile. Some point to the poems' own habit of climbing by repetition, one line picking up a word from the one before (Psalm 121, with its "keeps" and "keeper", is the clearest case). No one can settle it, and the book does not need to. These are short poems, all but one of them nine verses long or shorter, with a road and its weather in them.

A practical point. Some Bibles, following the old Greek and Latin numbering, call these Psalms 119 to 133. If your Bible disagrees with the numbers here by one, that is why.

## Jewish and Christian use

The psalms are Jewish poems, and Jews have sung and prayed them in synagogue and at home from ancient times. Psalm 126, for example, is sung in many communities before the blessing after meals on Sabbaths and festivals. Christians have prayed the psalms from the earliest centuries, and Latin-speaking Christians called these fifteen the gradual psalms, from the Latin for a step. They are shared ground, and this book tries to respect both houses. Where a reading belongs to one tradition (in Psalm 132, for example), the reflection says whose it is.

The psalms use *Israel* for the people who sang them, and *Zion* for the hill of Jerusalem and then for the city and its people. We read them as ancient poems and say nothing about any present-day politics.

## The name Yahweh

You will meet the word *Yahweh* in the first line. It is how the World English Bible prints the personal name of God in Hebrew, written with four consonants. Most English Bibles print "the LORD" in capital letters instead. In Jewish tradition the name is held so holy that many do not say it aloud and say *Adonai*, "my Lord", or *HaShem*, "the Name", in its place. The World English Bible also uses *Yah*, a short form of the name, and keeps the word *Lord* for *Adonai*.

We quote the translation as it stands, and we handle the name with care. If you are used to saying "the Lord", say that. If the name is not one you say, say the word you do say. The sense of the poems will not suffer.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The passages are from the World English Bible, a modern English translation made by volunteers. It was begun as an update of the American Standard Version of 1901, and it has been dedicated to the public domain. We read it from the complete text on Project Gutenberg, ebook number 8294, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

The passages are untouched, apart from the verse numbers and headings, which are left out. The translators' punctuation is kept, straight quotation marks and all. Where we skip verses inside a psalm, a mark like this shows it: …. Nine of the fifteen psalms are quoted whole. Six are quoted in part, and each reflection says what was left out. The choice of passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours and are original. The prayers are written plainly, in our own words, and do not pretend to be Scripture.
