# Day 2. Glad to go

*Can gladness at the gate and a sneer from the comfortable sit in the same day?*

## The passage

> I was glad when they said to me, "Let's go to Yahweh's house!" Our feet are standing within your gates, Jerusalem; Jerusalem, that is built as a city that is compact together; where the tribes go up, even Yah's tribes, according to an ordinance for Israel, to give thanks to the name of Yahweh. … Pray for the peace of Jerusalem. Those who love you will prosper. Peace be within your walls, and prosperity within your palaces. For my brothers' and companions' sakes, I will now say, "Peace be within you." For the sake of the house of Yahweh our God, I will seek your good.
>
> — Psalm 122:1 to 4 and 6 to 9 (World English Bible)

> To you I do lift up my eyes, you who sit in the heavens. Behold, as the eyes of servants look to the hand of their master, as the eyes of a maid to the hand of her mistress; so our eyes look to Yahweh, our God, until he has mercy on us. Have mercy on us, Yahweh, have mercy on us, for we have endured much contempt. Our soul is exceedingly filled with the scoffing of those who are at ease, with the contempt of the proud.
>
> — Psalm 123:1 to 4 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

Some mornings you are glad before you are properly awake. Someone nearby is lacing their boots and says, "Shall we go?", and the answer rises from somewhere that has nothing to do with sleep. Psalm 122 begins there. *I was glad when they said to me*, the poet says, and what they said was an invitation. The gladness does not start with the poet. It arrives from outside, from being asked, from the fact that other people are going too.

Notice who goes: *where the tribes go up*. It is a crowd, a whole people on the move, climbing together to give thanks. A road can be lonely, and some days you will want it to be. But on a long walk company also comes and goes, for an hour, for a day, for a table in the evening, and the psalm treats company as part of the gladness and not an extra.

Then the poem does what pilgrim poems do. It arrives. *Our feet are standing within your gates, Jerusalem.* The tense changes in the middle of the song. For days the city has been a word on a sign or a distance in a book, and now the feet are on its stones. If you have ever come over a rise and seen the place you have been walking towards, you know the odd quiet that follows. The psalm stays there a moment and looks at the city, *built as a city that is compact together*, close-packed and held tight.

What the traveller does with the arrival is the surprise. Having come, the poet does not boast of it. The poet prays for the place: *Pray for the peace of Jerusalem.* And the reason is given, and it is warm: *For my brothers' and companions' sakes, I will now say*, and what is said is *Peace be within you.* A guest blesses the host's town. It is a good habit for anyone passing through. A village is somebody's home and not a backdrop, and a *buenos días* to someone at a door is a small form of the same prayer.

One verse is missing from the page, and it matters. Verse 5 says that there are thrones set there for judgment, the thrones of David's house. The city is a capital with a king's court, and this is a political poem as well as a pilgrim's one. Its peace is a peace of justice and order, and of one family's rule. You can hold the prayer for peace and leave the thrones, and many readers do.

Psalm 123 does not let the gladness last the page. It takes up the lifted eyes of the first day, *To you I do lift up my eyes*, but the mood has changed. The picture is of servants watching a master's hand, and it will sit badly with some readers. It comes from a world of household service and bondage, and we do not take it as approval of that world. What it describes is a kind of attention: not watching a master's face to flatter, but watching the hand, for the smallest sign that mercy is coming. If the picture grates, set another beside it, such as a child watching a door or a walker watching a gap in the cloud. The poem asks only for the eyes, fixed on one place and waiting.

And the reason for the waiting is plain: *we have endured much contempt*, and *the scoffing of those who are at ease*. The singers have been looked down on by people comfortable enough to find them funny. You may meet a thin version on any road, a remark about why anyone would walk when there are buses, or an older sneer you have carried since you were small, and that the road has a way of waking. The psalm does not answer the sneer, and it does not fight it. It looks up, and it waits for mercy.

Gladness at the gate is real, and so is contempt, and the psalms put them side by side without apology.

Three small things, then. Be glad when someone says "Let's go", and let it be a plain gladness. Wish peace on the place you pass through, whether or not it knows. And when a sneer finds you, do not stare at it. Lift the eyes to where mercy comes from.

## For the walk

- Who has made this day possible, a host, a friend at home, someone who simply set out at the same time? Have I thanked them, even silently?
- As you come to the next village, hamlet or farm, wish it peace in your own words before you go in. Then greet the first person you see, in their language if you can. A plain *buenos días* is enough.

## At the end of the day

Gladness is easier to find at seven in the morning than at seven in the evening, so look for it now with a kinder eye. It need not have been large. A coffee that came at the right moment, a downhill after a long up, a stranger who laughed at the same thing you did. Count whatever there was.

The psalm's gladness came from being asked in. Was there an asking today? A good morning offered first, a door held, a seat made room for, a loaf passed along a table. These are easy to miss when you are watching your feet, and they are the whole of what a road gives you apart from the road.

And look at where you arrived. The psalm's feet were *standing within your gates*. Yours are standing inside today's gate, wherever it was: a village square, a hostel door, a bench at the end of the stage. Notice them. They did the whole thing. Take your boots off and thank the feet, aloud if the room allows.

The poet's reason for blessing the city was other people: *my brothers' and companions' sakes*, the song says, so the arrival was for them too. Who were your companions today? Some walked beside you and some only passed. Some are at home and walked in your thoughts. Wish them peace by name, one at a time. It takes about a minute.

Then think about the places you passed through. Some of them you will have walked straight past as though they were scenery. It is not too late. Pick one, a street or a square or a lane with a washing line across it, and wish it peace now, in your own words. It costs nothing, and it is a kind way to leave a place.

Notice, too, where you waited today with your eyes on somebody's hand: the person with the key, the driver at the crossing, whoever was deciding whether the kitchen was open. Waiting for a small mercy is a daily posture on a road, and you may have taken it up without knowing.

And did anyone look down on you today? It may have been a passing car, a remark, a glance at your boots, or a memory that came up on the hill without being asked. Notice where your eyes went when it stung. Did they stay on the sting, or did they lift? Forgiving it is not tonight's job. You only have to notice, and set it down for the night.

Tiredness makes the stings sharper. A remark lands harder at five in the afternoon than at nine in the morning, and a small insult can seem a large one when the body is empty. Eat something, drink water, wash, and judge the day afterwards, not before.

- Where today was I glad, and who or what had asked me into it?
- Which place or person did I pass that I could wish peace on tonight?
- When something stung, where did I turn my eyes?

## A short prayer

*For the gladness that found me today, thank you. For the places I passed through and the people who live in them, peace. For the sting I carried, let it lose its hold before morning. Let me sleep like someone who is expected somewhere tomorrow.*
