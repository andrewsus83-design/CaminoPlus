# How to use this book

This is a small book for eight slow days. It is made to go with the Camino Aragonés, the road that runs from Somport, in the Pyrenees, to Puente la Reina, ~160 km. The app's own notes on this route describe a mountain start and then rolling hills, a quiet road, and services that are sparse in places. A quiet road leaves room for a few short poems.

You read one day at a time, whether you are walking or at home. If your road takes more days than the book, repeat a favourite or rest. If it takes fewer, read the day that fits the day you are having.

## The songs

The book is built on fifteen psalms, Psalms 120 to 134. Their headings call them songs of ascents. They are traditionally understood as the songs pilgrims sang as they went up to Jerusalem, a city on high ground, where one always goes up. Scholars differ on how they were really used, and the next chapter says what is known and what is not. What nobody doubts is what is in them: a road, hills, a gate, danger, tears, tiredness, waiting for morning, and a house at the end. That is why they travel well.

The psalms keep their own order, two by two, with the last one on its own on the eighth day. The order happens to be a road.

- **Days 1 and 2 (Psalms 120 to 123).** Distress and setting out, then gladness at the city, and contempt.
- **Days 3 and 4 (Psalms 124 to 127).** Help in danger, then a return, tears and the work of building.
- **Days 5 and 6 (Psalms 128 to 131).** A household and a long affliction, then out of the depths and a quiet heart.
- **Days 7 and 8 (Psalms 132 to 134).** A house for God, people living together in unity, and a blessing at night.

## A day, in two parts

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** Two short stretches of psalm, in the exact words of the World English Bible, one for each psalm of the day. Read each one twice, and say the second reading aloud, or nearly aloud. These were songs before they were pages. Where verses are left out, a mark like this shows it: …, and the reflection tells you what is missing.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours. It starts from something on the road and lets the psalms open it. It says what they say, where they are hard, and what they might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for an hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have lived, with the psalms in mind, and two or three gentle questions. Nothing needs writing down.
- **A short prayer.** Plain words of our own, not the psalmists'. Say it as a prayer if you pray. Change a word where your own tradition would. If you do not pray, take it as a wish.

## Hebrew poems, shared

The psalms are Hebrew poems. They were Jewish before they were Christian, and both traditions have prayed them for a very long time. They are addressed to God and speak as believers, and we say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All are welcome here. You do not have to share the psalmists' faith to know what they are describing. You will meet the name Yahweh in the first line; the next chapter explains it.

Some of the fifteen are hard. A few ask for harm to fall on enemies, and one makes a household into a picture that many lives do not fit. The reflections say so when it happens and do not tidy it away.

## Before you set out

A psalm is not a weather forecast. In the mountains, check the forecast the evening before, start early, carry water and food, and tell someone your plan. Services can be few, so know where your next water is. For anything that worries you, see a doctor. In an emergency in Spain or France, call 112.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* means a pilgrim hostel: a simple place to sleep, usually in bunks, sometimes with a kitchen you share with strangers. *Buen Camino* is the greeting walkers give one another on the way, a wish that the road goes well.

Read slowly. A short song can carry a long day.
