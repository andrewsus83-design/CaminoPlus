# Day 6. Waiting for morning

*What do you do when you cannot make the morning come?*

## The passage

> Out of the depths I have cried to you, Yahweh. Lord, hear my voice. Let your ears be attentive to the voice of my petitions. If you, Yah, kept a record of sins, Lord, who could stand? But there is forgiveness with you, therefore you are feared. I wait for Yahweh. My soul waits. I hope in his word. My soul longs for the Lord more than watchmen long for the morning; more than watchmen for the morning. Israel, hope in Yahweh, for with Yahweh there is loving kindness. With him is abundant redemption. He will redeem Israel from all their sins.
>
> — Psalm 130:1 to 8 (World English Bible)

> Yahweh, my heart isn't haughty, nor my eyes lofty; nor do I concern myself with great matters, or things too wonderful for me. Surely I have stilled and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with his mother, like a weaned child is my soul within me. Israel, hope in Yahweh, from this time forth and forevermore.
>
> — Psalm 131:1 to 3 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

There is an hour, some time before dawn, when the dormitory is as quiet as it will get and you are awake. The pack is by the bed. Somebody is breathing slowly across the room. A thin line of light lies under the door. Everything you pushed aside during the walk now has room to sit up.

That is the hour of Psalm 130. *Out of the depths I have cried to you.* The picture is of a voice from a long way down, as from deep water. Notice how it begins. There is no request at first, only a plea to be heard: *Lord, hear my voice.* Before anything is asked for, the poet asks to be listened to. Anyone who has been awake in the dark knows how much that is.

Then a hard honest question. *If you, Yah, kept a record of sins, Lord, who could stand?* The picture is of a ledger kept on everyone, and the poet saying that nobody would come out well from it. The next line does not argue. It turns: *But there is forgiveness with you, therefore you are feared.* The oddity is the "therefore". The awe follows from the forgiveness and not in spite of it. The one before whom you could not stand is the one who lets you stand.

Then the waiting, in short plain pieces, like someone too tired for long sentences: *I wait for Yahweh. My soul waits. I hope in his word.* And the best image in the song: *My soul longs for the Lord more than watchmen long for the morning.* The watchmen are the sentries who keep a city's night watch. They are not hoping that morning will come, because they know it will. They are waiting for something certain, and the waiting is no shorter for that. The poet says it twice, *more than watchmen for the morning*, because that is what waiting sounds like. You say it again. For anyone awake at three, the lesson is a mild one. You cannot make the dawn come. You can only keep watch, and every morning of this walk has come so far.

Two places are hard. One is the word *sins* and the idea of a record. For some readers it is a tender and freeing word, and for others a heavy one, laden with guilt that was never theirs or shame they were handed. Read it, if you need to, as whatever you would rather were not on the record, and take the psalm's claim as it stands: that the record is not the last word.

The other is that the depths can be a good deal more than a bad night. Grief, fear, a darkness that does not lift by morning: the psalm gives such things a voice, and it does not say that the answer is quick. If what you carry is heavier than a walk can lighten, tell someone: a friend, a doctor, a helpline in your own country. If you are in danger of harming yourself, call 112 in Spain or France, or the emergency number where you are. Asking for help is no failure of the psalm. It is a good deal of what the psalm is about.

Psalm 131 is the quiet after. *Yahweh, my heart isn't haughty, nor my eyes lofty; nor do I concern myself with great matters, or things too wonderful for me.* The heading gives it to David, a king saying he has not set himself above anyone, which is an odd thing to say and a short song to say it in. What it describes is relief: no longer needing to master everything, to have a view on every question, or to settle your whole life on a road in Spain.

And then the picture of a quiet heart: *Surely I have stilled and quieted my soul, like a weaned child with his mother.* A weaned child is not a baby crying for milk. It is a child who has stopped demanding and rests against its mother's side, not for what it can get, only to be there. Notice the verbs: *I have stilled and quieted my soul.* The quiet is something made, with some effort, and not only given.

Some honest doubt belongs here. There will be days when the soul will not be stilled, and shouting at it makes it worse. The poet says the soul has been stilled. The psalm does not say how, or for how long, or what to do on the day it will not work. The nearest it comes to an answer is the child at the mother's side: be near what holds you, and stop asking for more.

For today, then, two things. Wait without pushing at the morning. And for one hour, set the great questions on a shelf, to be taken down later in daylight.

## For the walk

- What am I waiting for, and what would it be to wait for it for one hour without pushing at it?
- For ten minutes today, walk without trying to work anything out. When a big question arrives, note it as you would a bird on a wire, and let it be. Then come back to the sound of your feet, your breath, the surface of the path.

## At the end of the day

The lights go out in the dormitory, or the house goes quiet, and the room begins its night noises. If you wake at three, you already know what to do. Do not fight it. Keep watch, as the sentries did. Watching is a task, not a failure, and morning is certain even if it is slow. If you like, find out now which way the window faces. Then, in the dark, you will know where the first light is going to come from.

Think back over the waiting that the day contained. There is always more than you notice: waiting for the village to appear round the next bend, for the blister to stop, for a café to open, for a message that has not come. What were you really waiting for under the smaller things? And where did you try to hurry it? A good deal of walking is pushing at mornings that arrive at their own pace.

Were there great matters in your head today? There usually are, a decision at home, a relationship, a question about the whole shape of your life. The road gives them room, and that is one of its gifts. It is also tiring to keep carrying them. The second psalm says that you are allowed to set them down for the night. They are not going anywhere. They will be there at breakfast, and a little easier to look at.

If the depths came up today, that was allowed. If they did not, be glad, and do not go looking for them. And if they are with you now, in the quiet, be honest about it with somebody, tomorrow if not tonight.

There may also be a small ledger of your own running tonight: the sharp word at four o'clock, the pace you pushed, the thing you forgot to do. The first psalm says that a record kept on everything is more than anyone could stand. You can close yours for the night. It is allowed to open again tomorrow, but not at three in the morning, when every entry is written in capitals.

The tired mind behaves very much like a tired child: it wants everything at once, it will not be reasoned with, and it cannot say what it needs. What the psalm describes, a child that has stopped demanding, is what a tired mind wants. The road to it often runs through food, warmth and lying down before it runs through thinking.

Tonight's practice is the child's. Lie down, let the bed take the weight of you, and for one minute stop holding up your own head. The mattress and the floor and the building have been holding up other people for years.

- What am I waiting for, and where in the day did I try to hurry it?
- Which great question could I set down tonight and leave until daylight?
- Who is one person I could tell about whatever is heaviest, if I wanted to?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I have no answers, and I will not ask for any. Keep watch with me until it is light. Let my mind be like a child that has stopped crying and is simply held. If I wake in the dark, let me wait without fear. The morning will come, and I will be here.*
