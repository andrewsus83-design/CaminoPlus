# Day 1. Eyes to the hills

*Where does my help come from?*

## The passage

> In my distress, I cried to Yahweh. He answered me. Deliver my soul, Yahweh, from lying lips, from a deceitful tongue. … Woe is me, that I live in Meshech, that I dwell among the tents of Kedar! My soul has had her dwelling too long with him who hates peace. I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war.
>
> — Psalm 120:1 to 2 and 5 to 7 (World English Bible)

> I will lift up my eyes to the hills. Where does my help come from? My help comes from Yahweh, who made heaven and earth. He will not allow your foot to be moved. He who keeps you will not slumber. Behold, he who keeps Israel will neither slumber nor sleep. Yahweh is your keeper. Yahweh is your shade on your right hand. The sun will not harm you by day, nor the moon by night. Yahweh will keep you from all evil. He will keep your soul. Yahweh will keep your going out and your coming in, from this time forth, and forevermore.
>
> — Psalm 121:1 to 8 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

At the foot of a long climb there is a moment when you add everything up. The slope, the hours, the weight on your back, the water left in the bottle. On the Camino Aragonés the moment may come early, since the road starts in the Pyrenees and the app's notes call it a mountain start. Whatever your road is, you know the look that goes up to the ridge and asks a question.

Psalm 121 opens with that look: *I will lift up my eyes to the hills.* Readers have taken the line in two ways. In one, the hills are the danger, with their high places, their weather and whoever might be waiting there, and the question is a worried one. In the other, the hills are where hope points, towards Jerusalem and the house of God. Scholars differ, and the Hebrew will carry either. The answer carries both: help comes from the one *who made heaven and earth*. It does not come from the hills themselves, and it is not stopped by them.

But the day begins one poem earlier, with someone a long way from home. *In my distress, I cried to Yahweh. He answered me.* Notice the order. The first of the fifteen songs has a cry and an answer in its first breath, before anything else has happened. Then the complaint comes, and it is a very human one. The poet is surrounded by lying lips and by people who want a fight: *I am for peace, but when I speak, they are for war.* Anyone who has sat at a table where everyone was angry knows that loneliness. Meshech and Kedar are the names of distant peoples, one far to the north of Israel and the other out among the desert tents. The poet is probably not living in either. Many scholars read the names as a figure of speech for being so far from home that any place might as well be those.

Many people set out on a long walk to get away from something: a job, a house, an argument, a loss. The first two songs hold both halves of that. One is the leaving, with its tiredness of other people's noise. The other is the standing at the bottom of the hill, looking up. You may be in either today, or in both before lunch.

The passage is hard in two places. The first is in Psalm 120, between the stretches we have quoted. Verses 3 and 4 turn on the liar, and the answer is a line of its own: *Sharp arrows of the mighty, with coals of juniper.* The poet, hurt by lies, sees harm coming to the liar, and perhaps wants it. It is the first of several such verses in these fifteen songs, and we leave them off the page, not out of the book. Wanting someone who deceived you to pay is an old and honest feeling, and the psalm is honest enough to put it down. You do not have to make it a plan.

The second is the promise at the end of Psalm 121: the sun will not harm you, and you will be kept from all evil. Read flatly, as an insurance policy, that is not true to the world. Walkers get burnt and dehydrated, they fall, and some are hurt on roads that could not be called dangerous. Anyone who has lost someone on a road will not be comforted by a sentence that says it cannot happen. One way to read it is as trust rather than as a guarantee: a walker who is not alone, kept in a deeper sense than being spared. Others find even that too much, and that is allowed. The psalm does not cancel the forecast. Carry water, check the weather the night before, and start early. Day 3 says more about danger.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Only to lift the eyes. Not away from the path, but beyond your own feet, to see where you are. And there is a last line to take with you, the sense of the whole walk in a few words: *your going out and your coming in*. You go out of the door in the morning and you come in at night, and the psalm claims both ends of the day.

## For the walk

- What do I look at first when I am afraid or tired, my feet or the horizon? What would it be to look at the other one for a minute?
- At the next rise, stop and take three breaths. Lift your eyes to the farthest thing you can see and name it quietly: a ridge, a tower, a line of trees, the sky. Then look down at the path and go on. Do it once or twice today, not more. If the day is flat and nothing rises, do it at the next bench or gate.

## At the end of the day

Boots off. The first evening of a walk is mostly arithmetic: how far, how long, how much is left, and what the feet think of it. They will tell you plainly. Let them have their say, and then think about the other things the day contained.

You lifted your eyes today, or you did not. If you did, what was there? A ridge in the morning haze, a village that looked closer than it was, a bird, a very ordinary cloud. If you kept your head down against the slope, that is not a failure. Head-down is how most hills are climbed. But there may have been one moment when the road opened out and you could have looked up. It may come tomorrow.

Think too about what you left behind in order to be here. Perhaps it was only a sink of dishes and a phone that kept pinging. Perhaps it was larger. Psalm 120 does not hurry the poet past the distress. It lets it be said, once, in the first line, and it is answered. You might let it be said tonight: whatever you are carrying in from the other life, say what it is, plainly, even if only to the ceiling.

Then look for the answers, the small ones, because the poem is as interested in the answer as in the cry, and tired people forget to count them. A bench at the right moment. A nod from a stranger. A cloud across the sun at noon. A tap that ran cold when you needed it. No need to give any of it a name. Count it, that is all.

And there is the plain fact of the evening. This morning you went out, and tonight you have come in. The psalm claims both ends of the day, and so can you, if only because it is done.

Be honest about the tiredness. The first day is rarely the real one. Your body has not decided yet whether to be an ally, and your mind has not yet quietened. A bed that is not your own, a snore across the room, a light that clicks on at five. None of that is a sign that the walk is a mistake. It is a sign that it has begun, and that it will take some days to find its pace.

One practical kindness for the person who wakes tomorrow: fill the bottle, put the socks where you will find them, look at the forecast. The psalm's keeping is on a large scale, but a keeper's work is mostly small preparations like these, and you can do a little of it for yourself.

- Where did I lift my eyes today, and what did I see when I did?
- What am I glad to have left behind, and what am I carrying anyway?
- When I say I am being kept, what do I really rely on: the plan, the forecast, my own stubbornness, something else?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I put the pack down. Thank you for the road that held me today. Whatever I left behind, let it weigh a little less by morning. Keep my feet sound and my temper kind. If I do not know who is listening, let this be a wish.*
