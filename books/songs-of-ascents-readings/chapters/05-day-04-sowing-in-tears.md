# Day 4. Sowing in tears

*What do you do while you wait for the joy to come back?*

## The passage

> When Yahweh brought back those who returned to Zion, we were like those who dream. Then our mouth was filled with laughter, and our tongue with singing. Then they said among the nations, "Yahweh has done great things for them." Yahweh has done great things for us, and we are glad. Restore our fortunes again, Yahweh, like the streams in the Negev. Those who sow in tears will reap in joy. He who goes out weeping, carrying seed for sowing, will certainly come again with joy, carrying his sheaves.
>
> — Psalm 126:1 to 6 (World English Bible)

> Unless Yahweh builds the house, they labor in vain who build it. Unless Yahweh watches over the city, the watchman guards it in vain. It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil; for he gives sleep to his loved ones.
>
> — Psalm 127:1 to 2 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

Every long walk has a stretch that feels, afterwards, like a dream. A morning you cannot quite believe you walked. A day when the body did what you had been afraid it could not do. You tell it to yourself on the bench and it sounds a little made up.

Psalm 126 begins with that feeling, on a very large scale: the singers say *we were like those who dream*. The usual reading is that the singers are the people who came home from exile in Babylon, about the sixth century BC, though scholars differ on whether the song looks back at one event or many. Either way, it is the dazed joy of people who have got back what they thought was lost. Their mouths were full of laughter, and other nations noticed. Joy that large is visible from outside.

Then comes the turn that makes this a true song and not a greeting card. In the middle, the song turns from looking back to asking: *Restore our fortunes again, Yahweh, like the streams in the Negev.* They have come back, and they still ask for restoring. The Negev is the dry south of the land, where a riverbed can lie empty for months and then run with water after rain. The plea sits right beside the gladness, in the same song, with no embarrassment. Joy and unfinished business are neighbours. You reach the top of the pass and the other side is more road.

Then the best-known lines: *Those who sow in tears will reap in joy.* Commentators often point out that, in a poor year, sowing means putting into the ground the grain you might have eaten. The sower goes out because the field needs it, and weeps while he does it. The psalm does not tell him to stop weeping, or ask him to feel better first. He *goes out weeping, carrying seed*, the tears and the work carried together, one in each hand. For anyone who has set out on a walk because of a grief, this is the most honest sentence of the week so far. Nobody has to be finished with the crying before they walk.

That is the hard place, and it needs a plain word. It would be cruel to read the verse as a bargain: tears in, joy out. Some losses do not come round to joy, and some seasons are very long. What the psalm promises is that the sower will come back carrying sheaves, which is a promise about the harvest and not a promise that the harvest repays what was lost. If you are still in the weeping part, the song's concern is that you went out. That counts.

The second psalm of the day pushes the other way, against the opposite temptation. *Unless Yahweh builds the house, they labor in vain who build it.* Builders and watchmen are not told to stop. The psalm speaks to the one who has begun to think that it is all up to them: *It is vain for you to rise up early, to stay up late, eating the bread of toil; for he gives sleep to his loved ones.*

Walkers know that temptation well. The earliest start. The longest day. The most kilometres, as though the road were an exam and the result were yours alone. The psalm does not say the work is pointless. It says the work is not all on you, and it ends not with effort but with sleep. You cannot earn sleep by staying up late, and you cannot hurry the morning by getting up in the dark.

So the two songs are a pair. One is for the person who weeps as they sow, the other for the one who worries as they build, and each ends in something received: sheaves, sleep.

There is one more hard place, in verses 3 to 5, which we left off the page. They praise children as a heritage and a quiver full of arrows, and a man glad of many sons to stand with him at the city gate. It was one world's idea of security, and it sits badly with those who have no children, or have lost one. If those verses have ever hurt you, you have company, and nothing else in the psalm depends on them.

Today's request is small. Carry the seed anyway, whatever it is, and let the tears come along if they want to. And give the day a proper end.

## For the walk

- Is there something I am pushing today that I could ease off for an hour, as though the day were not all up to me?
- Choose one hour of today to walk without checking the pace, the distance or the time. When the hour is over, notice what you did not miss. If something heavy is walking with you, let it, and do not try to fix it. Carry it the way you would carry something that is for a purpose.

## At the end of the day

A day's work looks strange from a bed. You were so busy with it an hour ago, and now it is only a heap of small things that have stopped asking for anything. Let the heap sit there. You can look at it for a minute without sorting it.

What did you sow today? Not the great deeds, the small ones. A greeting that turned into a conversation. An hour spent beside someone slower than you. A word you decided not to say. Most of it you will never see grow, and that is the way of seed. You put it in the ground and you walk on.

Was there something today that felt like a dream? It may have been a view, or a moment of ease in the body, or a sudden lightness that had no reason. Name it. It does not need an explanation. And if there were tears, a few or many, the psalm does not make them a problem. They walk in the same company as the seed.

The second song speaks to anyone who is guarding something from a distance. You may have a house, a job, a person, a decision at home, and a quiet part of you has been standing watch over it all day, as the watchman stands on the wall. You cannot see it from here. Tonight the watch can be handed over. Say to yourself that it will keep until morning, and let the watchman off duty.

The psalm speaks of *the bread of toil*, and tonight's supper may be exactly that, eaten with sore legs. There is no scorn in the phrase. A day's effort is honest, and so is the meal after it. What the psalm refuses is only the idea that effort is the whole story.

Did anyone notice you today? In the first song, the other nations saw the joy and remarked on it. Perhaps a stranger smiled at you, or a host said you looked well. Joy shows more than we think, and it is allowed to.

The gift of the psalm is sleep, so make the end of the evening short. Wash, put your feet up for a few minutes, and let the body have the small things it has been waiting for all day. The mind builds a great many houses at two in the morning and they are almost always out of proportion. Do not plan tomorrow tonight beyond a start time and a bottle of water.

You may feel that today was not enough. Almost everyone does when tired. There are always more kilometres, more words, more care that you could have given. The psalm answers that gently: it is not all on you.

- What did I put into the ground today, however small, that I will not see grow?
- Where did something happen today that I could hardly believe?
- What am I trying to carry alone that I could put down tonight and sleep on?

## A short prayer

*Let the small things I planted today grow without me. Let the sorrow I carry walk beside me and not ahead of me. Take what I could not finish, and give me sleep. I will begin again when it is light.*
