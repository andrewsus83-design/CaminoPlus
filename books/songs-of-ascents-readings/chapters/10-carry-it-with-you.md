# Carry it with you

The eight days are over, or you have stopped early, or you mean to start again from the top. Any of these will do. Nobody is keeping a record. The book was only ever meant to walk beside you for a while, and it does not mind whether your road ended at Puente la Reina, somewhere short of it, or at your own front door.

That leaves one question: what to keep. Not the whole book, since nobody carries that. Three suggestions, and you may ignore all of them.

- **Learn one by heart.** Psalms 131, 133 and 134 are only three verses long, and Psalm 121 is eight. A psalm learned on foot makes a good companion, because the walking keeps time for it and because it is the right length for a mile or two. Say it under your breath on a bad hill, or in the queue for the kitchen.
- **Keep one small habit.** Lifting the eyes at a rise. A wish of peace on a village. A thank-you aloud after a narrow place. *Buen Camino* to someone you would rather not greet. Open hands for three breaths. Pick the one that cost you least and helped most, and do it again tomorrow.
- **Read what was left out.** Six of the fifteen psalms were quoted in part. In any Bible you can find the rest: verses 3 and 4 of Psalm 120, verse 5 of Psalm 122, verses 3 to 5 of Psalms 125 and 127, verses 5 to 8 of Psalm 129, and most of Psalm 132. Read them slowly and aloud, and notice which you can say and which you cannot. Remember that some Bibles number the psalms one lower.

## How to go on

You can go back to any day. They are short on purpose, and some will say more to you a month from now than they did on the morning you read them. Try them in a different order. Try the evenings only. Or keep one passage for a bad morning and say it aloud.

Be honest, too, about what did not work. Some of these psalms ask for things you may not be able to give: harm wished on enemies, a household drawn too narrowly, a promise that the sun will never hurt you. That is allowed, and it is no failing in you. A day that did not speak to you has done no harm. Leave it where it is.

## A last word

If the tradition is right, the people who first sang these songs did not stay in the city. They went up, and they went home. The last song reads like a sending-off, and the blessing in it is meant to go with the one who leaves. Going home is the second half of a pilgrimage, and often the harder half.

The first day gave you a line about *your going out and your coming in*. It is a good one to end on, because it describes a life as well as a walk. You go out, and you come in, and then, if the weather holds, you go out again. Nothing in it asks you to be certain, or finished. It asks for a next step, some patience with yourself, and, when one comes, a kindness from a stranger that you can pass on.

*Buen Camino.*
