# Day 8. A blessing by night

*What is said to you as you go?*

## The passage

> Look! Praise Yahweh, all you servants of Yahweh, who stand by night in Yahweh's house! Lift up your hands in the sanctuary. Praise Yahweh! May Yahweh bless you from Zion; even he who made heaven and earth.
>
> — Psalm 134:1 to 3 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

Somewhere, tonight, someone is awake so that you can sleep. Perhaps it is the keeper of a hostel with a key in a pocket, or a nurse on a ward, or a driver on the ring road, or a baker whose day began at two. The world is full of people who work through the night, and most of us only meet them at the edge of the dark.

The last of the fifteen songs is for exactly those people. It calls to *all you servants of Yahweh, who stand by night in Yahweh's house.* These were, presumably, the ones who kept the watch in the house of worship while the city slept. Many scholars read the song as a call and an answer. The pilgrims, at the end of their visit, call out to the night staff, and a voice replies with a blessing. We cannot be sure it was first sung at a leaving, but it reads like one.

Look at how short it is, and at its verbs. The songs before it are full of going: going up, going out, coming in, turning back. This one is for those who *stand*. Yesterday, in Psalm 132, the pilgrims were on their way into the dwelling place. On the second day their feet were standing in the gate. Now the walking is over, and what is left is a place to stand and keep watch.

After eight days and fifteen songs of distress, of hills, of narrow places, of tears and furrows and depths, the book closes with three verses. There is not a request in them. *Lift up your hands in the sanctuary.* Hands lifted is a very old gesture. It can mean praise or asking, and it can simply mean that the hands are empty. A walker's hands are empty only at the end of the day, when the pack is down and the poles are in the corner. It is a good moment to lift them.

*Praise Yahweh!* The collection that began with *In my distress, I cried to Yahweh* ends with praise. Notice that nothing has been solved. The lying lips of Psalm 120 are still out there. The furrows are still on the back from Psalm 129. The poet did not get an answer to every question, and the songs make no pretence that they did. Praise is not offered as a reward for fixing things. It is what comes next, after everything else has been said honestly. If the call to praise sounds too quick for you tonight, you do not have to force it. You can simply stay in the song.

Then the blessing, and a small change of voice. Until now the calls have been to a crowd, *all you servants*. But the blessing is for one person: *May Yahweh bless you from Zion; even he who made heaven and earth.* The singular matters. After the long company of the road, the one who is leaving hears a word said over them alone.

A blessing is a word, and it cannot promise anything. A tired walker may feel foolish receiving one. It is not a guarantee, and the psalms in this book have been clear enough that nobody is guaranteed anything. What a blessing does is send someone after their journey with good will. It is like a cup handed through a window. You take it, and you drink, and you go on.

Now see what has come round. The line about the one who made heaven and earth is the third time we have met it in these eight days. It was the answer on the first day, to the question of the hills: help comes from *who made heaven and earth*. It came again at the end of the narrow escape. And now it ends the book. Whoever made the hills that frightened or called you on Day 1 is named, at the end, as the one who sends you off. And the blessing *from Zion* gathers up a line we met on the fifth day, *May Yahweh bless you out of Zion*, which was said over the table. The same hand blesses the table and the road.

There is a kind of circle in this. The first day gave us the going out and the coming in: *your going out and your coming in*. The last psalm is a going out again. The pilgrims set off from the house, with a blessing behind them.

Honest places? Not many here, but two. Much of this psalm is a call to people in a particular building doing a particular job, and most of us are not. We borrow it, as people have always borrowed the psalms. And the ending of a road is rarely tidy. You may feel relief and loss, or elation and flatness, in the same hour. The psalm makes no mention of either. It says only what is to be done with the hands and what is to be said.

What might it ask of an ordinary day? Thank somebody who keeps the night. Lift your empty hands once. And let yourself be blessed, then bless somebody else, since a blessing is meant to travel.

## For the walk

- Who keeps watch so that I can sleep, and have I ever thanked them?
- At some point today, stop, put down whatever you are carrying, and lift your hands a little, palms open, for three breaths. Then wish something good, in your own words, on the next person you will meet. Walk on. If you cannot manage it aloud, do it in your head; the hands are the part that matters.

## At the end of the day

This may be the last evening of the book, and perhaps of the walk. Last evenings are strangely tiring. The body has been told that it can stop, and it takes that rather literally. If you feel elated and flat at once, that is common, and it is not a sign that something has gone wrong.

Look back over today, and over the days before it, as you would look at a road from the top of a pass. It began with a cry and a question about hills. There was gladness at a gate, a narrow place, some tears and a long day of work, an old furrow, a night with no sleep, a roof and a room full of strangers. None of it has to become a lesson. It happened, and you were there.

Give the ending a minute before you do anything with it. The mind wants to run ahead to the journey home, the photographs, the things you will tell people, the next walk. All of that can wait. A last evening is a good one to do nothing with, to sit with your hands empty and let what happened settle where it will.

Look at your hands for a moment. They held the poles, tore the bread, gripped the rail on the steps. Lift them a little, open, as the psalm says, and then lay them down on the blanket.

Who kept watch for you today? Not in a grand way. The person who put the light on at the corner, the one who left the door unlatched, the family at home who kept the evening free for your call. Many of the people who hold us up do it by night or at a distance, and we only notice when we are tired enough to be grateful. If there is someone to thank, you could do it now, in a line, or write it on a card for the morning.

Then, if you will let it, receive the blessing. It is a plain thing: a wish for your good, said in the singular, with all the weight of those who have said it before. A wish can be received without believing anything. You only have to be willing to be wished well.

And if the walk goes on tomorrow, take the blessing with you. The psalm sends the pilgrim out the way a host sends a guest to the door.

And sleep. Someone is awake, and you do not have to be.

- Who kept watch for me today, in ways I only half noticed?
- Over whom would I like to say a blessing before I sleep: a person, a place, the road itself?
- What am I taking home from these days that I did not bring?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for everyone who keeps the night so that others can sleep. Thank you for the road, the hills and the roof. Bless the people I will meet and the ones I have left. Bless me too, and send me on. I will sleep now.*
