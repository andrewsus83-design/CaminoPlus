# Day 7. Until I find a place

*What do you owe to the place where you lay your head, and to the people beside you?*

## The passage

> Yahweh, remember David and all his affliction, how he swore to Yahweh, and vowed to the Mighty One of Jacob: "Surely I will not come into the structure of my house, nor go up into my bed; I will not give sleep to my eyes, or slumber to my eyelids; until I find out a place for Yahweh, a dwelling for the Mighty One of Jacob." … "We will go into his dwelling place. We will worship at his footstool. Arise, Yahweh, into your resting place; you, and the ark of your strength. Let your priest be clothed with righteousness. Let your saints shout for joy!"
>
> — Psalm 132:1 to 5 and 7 to 9 (World English Bible)

> See how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to live together in unity! It is like the precious oil on the head, that ran down on the beard, even Aaron's beard; that came down on the edge of his robes; like the dew of Hermon, that comes down on the hills of Zion: for there Yahweh gives the blessing, even life forevermore.
>
> — Psalm 133:1 to 3 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

Late in the afternoon, when the legs have gone and the day has been all slope, there is only one thought, and it is a bed. Not a view, not a story, not a better version of yourself: a place to put the pack down and lie flat. Every walker knows how much a single roof can weigh in the mind.

Psalm 132 begins with someone whose longing for a place was so great that he gave up his own. *Yahweh, remember David and all his affliction*, it says, and then it recalls an oath. The king would not go into his house or his bed. *I will not give sleep to my eyes*, he swore, *until I find out a place for Yahweh, a dwelling for the Mighty One of Jacob.* The place was for the ark, the chest at the heart of Israel's worship, which, according to the books of Samuel, David brought to Jerusalem.

Set that beside your own wish for a bed and it is a strange picture: a man who would not rest until someone else was housed. Psalm 127, on the fourth day, said that sleep is given to those who are loved, and today's psalm has a man who refused it. The two do not cancel. Some days you are the sleeper, and some days there is something you love that comes before sleep. But it is worth noticing how big a wish for a place can be, once it is pointed in the right direction.

Then the pilgrims, in their own voices. *We will go into his dwelling place.* The going in is plain and glad, and then comes a line a pilgrim might say at the end of a road: *Arise, Yahweh, into your resting place.* A resting place. The song is for people tired of travelling, who want the one they love to be somewhere restful too.

Most of what is hard is in the verses we left out, more than half the psalm. Verses 10 to 18 hold a promise to David's family, and the promise has a condition: if the king's children keep the covenant, their children will sit on the throne for ever. They also say that Zion is chosen as God's resting place, and they end with a line about enemies, *I will clothe his enemies with shame*. The psalm makes a claim about one city and one royal family, and many readers will find it hard to take as it stands. Christians have traditionally read the promise to David's line as pointing to Christ. Jewish readers have their own readings. We will not settle the matter here. Treat the line about enemies as you treated the harder verses of Psalm 129. What stays on the page is the longing for a place to rest.

Psalm 133 is three verses, and it might have been written about an albergue. *See how good and how pleasant it is for brothers to live together in unity!* Anyone who has shared a room with twenty strangers knows that unity is not automatic. The rustle of a plastic bag, a light that clicks on, the snore, the sock. The poem does not pretend it is easy. It says *see*, as though surprised, as though it had noticed something that does not usually happen. The word is *brothers*, and it was plainly meant of kin who belong in one household. On a road it stretches to everyone sharing the room.

The poem's two pictures are worth a minute each. Oil poured on the head of Aaron, the first high priest, running down the beard and onto the collar of his robes: abundant, a little extravagant, and not tidy. Then *the dew of Hermon*, from a high mountain a long way to the north of Jerusalem, falling on the hills of Zion: quiet, unseen, arriving in the night, so that you wake and everything is fresh.

Both are pictures of something coming down from above. Unity in this psalm is not built by effort. It descends, like oil or dew, and the invitation is to be in the place where it falls. At the end of a day on foot, that may be a table where strangers share what they have. Nobody has to agree about anything. They have one thing in common, which is that they walked here.

Unity is not the same as putting up with everything. If a room feels unsafe, that is a reason to speak to the host or to leave it, and the psalm has nothing against that.

So, at the end of an ordinary day, the psalms ask three things. Be glad of the roof and of whoever is under it. Make one small thing quieter. And let the common feeling of the room arrive, since it cannot be forced.

## For the walk

- Where do I want to rest at the end of this day, and what would it be to rest in the company as well as in the bed?
- Before you arrive tonight, decide one small thing you will do for the room: fold your bag quietly, take your noise down, leave the kitchen cleaner than you found it. Do it without telling anyone.

## At the end of the day

A bed is a small, great thing. Whatever sort of place you found tonight, a bunk, a room, a corner on a floor, it is somewhere to put the day down. Before anything else, let yourself feel how good that is.

How did you find it? Perhaps it was easy. Perhaps it took a late walk, a knock, a worry, a "full" and a second try. Somebody made it possible. A person keeps the door, changes the sheets, switches the heating on. Whoever it was, they were doing it today, and most likely they will do it again tomorrow for someone else. If you can, thank them before you leave.

Then think about the people in the room. There were moments when they were a company and moments when they were only a crowd. There may have been a small shared laugh at supper that changed the temperature of the evening. Or no such moment at all, only a row of backs. Both happen. The psalm's dew arrives when it likes. You can only be outside when it falls. If you were the one who made the evening easier, even only by being quiet, that counts.

And the oil: was anything poured out generously today, more than you expected? An extra ladle, a shortcut offered, a long answer to a short question. Abundance on a road is usually small, and it goes where it is needed. Notice whether you were given some, and whether you passed some on.

Is there anything you would stay awake for, as David swore he would? A promise to someone at home, an apology that is overdue, a thing you meant to do before the walk started. You need not do it at midnight. But you might name it tonight, so that it does not go on being only a weight in the pocket. Writing it on a scrap of paper often moves it from the chest to the page.

If something about the room was hard, the noise, a stranger's habits, a bed too close, you do not need to turn it into a grievance tonight. Many of those things look different after sleep. If something was truly unsafe, tell someone tomorrow, or move now.

People are at their worst at living together when they are weary, and so the kindest thing you can do for a room is to be the first to be quiet. Put the phone down, wind the day up in a few minutes, and say good night aloud, in whatever language comes, to whoever is awake. It is the smallest dew there is, and it lands on everybody.

- Where did I find a place to rest today, and who made it possible?
- When did the people around me feel like a company, and when like a crowd?
- What one small thing could I do tonight to make this room kinder to sleep in?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for a roof, and for the people who keep it. Let this room be quiet and kind. Where there is friction, give us patience, and where there is none, let me notice it. Let me rest the way a good guest rests, grateful and easy. In the morning I will go out again.*
