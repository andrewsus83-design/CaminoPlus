# Day 5. A table and a furrow

*What do you do with a blessing and a wound in the same day?*

## The passage

> Blessed is everyone who fears Yahweh, who walks in his ways. For you will eat the labor of your hands. You will be happy, and it will be well with you. Your wife will be as a fruitful vine, in the innermost parts of your house; your children like olive plants, around your table. Behold, thus is the man blessed who fears Yahweh. May Yahweh bless you out of Zion, and may you see the good of Jerusalem all the days of your life. Yes, may you see your children's children. Peace be upon Israel.
>
> — Psalm 128:1 to 6 (World English Bible)

> Many times they have afflicted me from my youth up. Let Israel now say, many times they have afflicted me from my youth up, yet they have not prevailed against me. The plowers plowed on my back. They made their furrows long. Yahweh is righteous. He has cut apart the cords of the wicked.
>
> — Psalm 129:1 to 4 (World English Bible)

## Reflection

After a day on foot, bread is better than it has any right to be. A plain loaf, a bowl of soup, a few tomatoes: it tastes of the kilometres. Psalm 128 is a blessing about exactly that: *you will eat the labor of your hands.* The food is good because the day was work, and the work was yours.

The psalm opens on someone who *fears Yahweh* and *walks in his ways*, and both phrases need a little help. The fear is usually understood as reverence, a respectful awe, and not as fright. And walking is the oldest picture of how to live: a life is a walk. The blessing it offers is ordinary and rather lovely: work that feeds you, happiness, things going well, and a table.

And at the table, the household. *Your wife will be as a fruitful vine, in the innermost parts of your house; your children like olive plants, around your table.* Here we must be honest. The psalm speaks to a man, and the family is pictured from his chair. His wife is described and not addressed, and she is placed inside the house. The picture belongs to one kind of life in one kind of world. It leaves out a great many people: those who walk alone, whose table is small or empty, who have no children or have lost them, whose family is the reason they are on the road. The woman who is being described has her own day to get through, which the psalm does not mention.

Take what can be taken. A table with people round it as a picture of enough. Bread earned. New growth, olive shoots, around something steady. And remember that a walker is, by definition, away from the table. The blessing comes *out of Zion*, from the city, to someone at a distance, which is the position you are in. The road is not the opposite of home. It is a stretch of time away from the table, and the psalm blesses it.

Then Psalm 129 starts, and it is a different weather. *Many times they have afflicted me from my youth up.* It is a lifetime of harm, said twice, and said to the whole people: *Let Israel now say.* That is the same call as the song of the narrow escape, but this time it asks for a long memory and not a single rescue. The image is one of the hardest in the Psalms: *The plowers plowed on my back. They made their furrows long.* A back as a field. Anyone who carries an old wound, a body's or a heart's, knows what a furrow is. You carry a pack on that back too.

And yet: *yet they have not prevailed against me.* It is in the first sentence, as if the poet could not wait to say it. The harm was long, and it did not win. Then: *Yahweh is righteous. He has cut apart the cords of the wicked*, as though a harness had been cut from a plough team. The ploughed one is freed, and not by their own strength.

The harder part is in the verses we left out. In verses 5 to 8 the psalm turns from lament to curse. It asks that all who hate Zion be turned backward, that they be like grass on a rooftop, which withers before it grows, and that nobody passing should wish them a blessing. It reads as anger, said out loud by people who, as far as the song tells us, could do nothing but say it. Readers, Jewish and Christian, have handled such verses in different ways. Some read them as honest anger brought into prayer and not acted on. Some read them as a warning against becoming like the people who did the harm. Some set them aside. All of those are allowed here, and a book for walkers will not tell you to pray them.

One thing about the last verse, though, that belongs to the road. The curse ends with the wish that those who go by should not say a blessing. On a pilgrim road, blessing people who go by is the ordinary courtesy. *Buen Camino*, said in passing, is a small blessing of the very kind the psalm says is missing from the people it curses. You may say it to a stranger who irritates you. It costs nothing, and it is probably the best answer a walker has.

In an ordinary day that comes to three small things. Eat the bread slowly and be thankful for it. Carry old furrows without being defined by them. And bless somebody in passing, even if the blessing is only two words long.

## For the walk

- What old hurt am I carrying today, and can I name it to myself, without needing to do anything about it yet?
- Say *Buen Camino* to every walker you pass today, including one you would rather not. Mean it as a small blessing and nothing more. You do not need to like them. If you are on a quiet stretch with no one in sight, say it to the next person you meet.

## At the end of the day

A table, if you have one tonight. If you do not, a bunk and a bag of bread will do, and so will a kitchen shared with strangers. Wherever you eat, eat slowly. It is the one meal of the day that is entirely deserved.

Who sat with you? Perhaps a few walkers whose names you will not keep. Perhaps no one, and you ate by the window with your phone. And who was missing? The people you left at home are at their own tables tonight, with your empty chair or without it. You may miss them most at supper. If your table at home is an easy one, that missing is a kind of wealth. If it is not, you may be glad of any table at all.

The psalm also says that the food is the labour of *your hands*, but tonight's bread was most likely other people's labour too: whoever grew it, baked it, cooked it, or carried it to the table. A thank-you can go to them as well.

Then the furrows. Long hours on foot give old hurts room to surface, in the dull miles when the mind has nothing to do. Perhaps one came up today. You are not asked to solve it tonight. Notice where it sits in the body: the shoulders, the jaw, the stomach. Naming a place is a start.

Notice, too, that in the song the hurt is said aloud, to the whole people, and not carried alone. Telling an old hurt to a listener is a different thing from carrying it in silence. If someone at your table tonight is a person you could trust with a little of it, you may find that a little is enough. If not, a page of a notebook can be a listener of sorts.

If you felt angry, at someone, at something that was done to you long ago, or only at the day, that is allowed too. The second psalm shows that anger can be put into words, even sharp ones, and that words are a better place for it than the next person you meet. Nothing needs doing with it before sleep.

Anger and tiredness are cousins, and they will lie down together if you let them. Do not decide anything big when you are both. The anger will still be there in the morning if it is real, and it will be easier to carry in daylight.

- Who sat at my table today, in person or in thought, and who was missing?
- What old hurt came up on the road, and where in my body did I feel it?
- Whom could I bless tomorrow, even in one word, that I would normally walk past?

## A short prayer

*For the bread I walked to, thank you. For the people at my table, near and far, keep them well. For the old hurt that came up today, let it lie down now. I am not asking to forget it, only to carry it lightly. Let me have one kind word ready for a stranger tomorrow.*
