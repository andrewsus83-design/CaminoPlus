# Day 3. Content to bear it

*What do you do at a door you are afraid will stay shut, and with a burden that does not come off?*

## The passage

> He knocked, therefore, more than once or twice, saying-- "May I now enter here? Will he within Open to sorry me, though I have been An undeserving rebel? Then shall I Not fail to sing his lasting praise on high." At last there came a grave person to the gate, named Good-will, who asked who was there? and whence he came? and what he would have? … CHR. Here is a poor burdened sinner. I come from the City of Destruction, but am going to Mount Zion, that I may be delivered from the wrath to come. I would therefore, Sir, since I am informed that by this gate is the way thither, know if you are willing to let me in? GOOD-WILL. I am willing with all my heart, said he; and with that he opened the gate. … Then I saw in my dream that Christian asked him further if he could not help him off with his burden that was upon his back; for as yet he had not got rid thereof, nor could he by any means get it off without help. He told him, As to thy burden, be content to bear it, until thou comest to the place of deliverance; for there it will fall from thy back of itself.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Wicket-gate (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

Every long day ends at a door. A heavy wooden one, perhaps, with a bell you are not sure you may ring, in a village you have never seen, with a pack that has become part of your body. You have been looking forward to it for hours, and now that you are in front of it, a small hesitation arrives. What if it is full? What if they ask something you cannot answer? What if you are simply in the wrong place?

Christian has the same hesitation on a larger scale. Earlier he was told to head for a little gate at the end of a field, and now he has got there, scratched and muddy, after a bog and a wrong turning we are not reading. He knocks, and nothing happens. So he knocks again, *more than once or twice*, and he says, in a few lines of verse, what he fears: will the person inside open to a *sorry* man, who has been an *undeserving rebel*? Notice that he does not argue his case. He asks. It is the voice of someone who has decided he must try anyway, though he expects to be refused.

The door opens. A grave person called Good-will asks who is there, and whence he came, and what he would have. Christian's answer is the plainest thing in the passage: a poor burdened sinner, come from the City of Destruction, on his way to Mount Zion, and would you be willing to let him in? *I am willing with all my heart*, says Good-will, and opens the gate. There is no test, no catechism, no waiting in the rain. The only thing Christian has to do is knock and say who he is.

Just outside the passage, Good-will gives him a pull as he steps in. A castle stands near, and its captain shoots arrows at people who come up to the gate. Bunyan's world has enemies in it. Ours may only have a stretch of busy road before the village. Either way the sense is the same: when the door opens, go through it.

Now the part that stays with you. Christian asks the one thing every reader would ask. Can the burden come off here? The answer is not what he hoped. *Be content to bear it*, says Good-will, *until thou comest to the place of deliverance*, and there it will fall from his back of itself. It is a hard thing to be told, at the end of a hard stretch, by someone who has just let you in. But it is also honest. Arriving does not end the carrying. It gives you a direction, a welcome and a place to start from. The hostel door opens, and next morning you lift the pack again.

After the gate, Good-will sends Christian on to the house of a guide called the Interpreter, who has promised to show him *excellent things*. We do not have room for all of the rooms. The one that fits this morning best is a little parlour with two children in it, named Passion and Patience. Passion is cross, because he has been told to wait for his best things until next year, and he wants all of them now. He is given a bag of treasure and spends it all, and is left with rags. Patience sits quietly, and, the Interpreter says, will be the one to laugh last. It is a small sketch, but it is a fair picture of what a long walk teaches. You cannot have the whole road this afternoon. Nobody can walk the ~240 km of this road in a day, and anyone who tries to go faster than the road allows may end up, like Passion, with nothing in hand.

There are other rooms in the Interpreter's house that are much darker, and the About chapter says what they are and why we leave them out. This morning only the one parlour is open.

A word of doubt, to keep this honest. A real door on a real road is sometimes locked, or full, or shut for the afternoon, and no one inside is willing with all their heart. Bunyan's gate is not a promise about hostels. If the first door does not open, you ask at the next. What the passage gives you is a habit, and the habit is to knock, and to say plainly who you are and what you need. It is more use than a plan.

## For the walk

- Which door am I standing outside today, and what am I waiting to be invited to do?
- Be the first to say hello. At every person you pass, a walker, a farmer, someone at a window, give the greeting first: *bom caminho* in Portuguese, *Buen Camino* in Spanish. It costs nothing, and it is a small way of knocking. Then notice what the greeting does to your own shoulders.

## At the end of the day

By this third evening you have a routine, or the beginnings of one: where the shoes go, which pocket holds the pen, what you wash first. Routines are quiet things, and they are one of the kindnesses of a road. A walk that has settled into one has begun to hold you up.

Count the doors you passed through today. Some were actual doors: a café, a church, a hostel, a shop where you asked for water. Others were doors of another kind, a conversation you began or did not, a question you put to a stranger, a moment when you could have said *I am a bit lost* and said *I am fine* instead. How did each of them feel, going up to it? How did each feel on the other side?

Bunyan's gate asks nothing difficult. It asks only that the man knock, and say who he is. That may be harder than it sounds. Most of us would rather arrive having already earned a welcome, and it is easier to be admired than to be let in. If you spent today wanting to arrive without having asked anything of anyone, that is very human, and a road is a good place to practise the other thing.

And then the burden, which did not fall. Perhaps you felt it again today, a little lighter than yesterday or a little heavier. *Be content to bear it* is a strange instruction, and it sounds like resignation. Read another way, it sounds more like permission: you do not have to be rid of it by tonight, and nobody is keeping score. The pack is a thing you put down at the end of the day and pick up in the morning, and so, for now, is the other weight.

A word for Patience, too. If you were in a hurry today, to cover more than you planned, to reach the next place sooner, to feel the benefit of the walk before it was ready, you were Passion for an afternoon. Nothing bad has happened. Patience is simply the habit of making the best things last, and a long road is generous with chances to practise it.

If the last stretch to your door today was along a road, let it be a small reminder to take that part slowly. Where there is no verge, walkers are usually advised to keep to the side facing the oncoming traffic, and to step well off the road when a vehicle passes. Bunyan's gate has arrows near it, and the last approach to a welcome is a place where people hurry and forget to look.

It is also worth noticing what was waiting on the other side. A stamp in a pilgrim's passport, a plate of something hot, a bunk with a number on it, a stranger's nod: none of it was earned in any sense that a ledger would recognise, and all of it was a gift. That is a good thing to say thank you for, to someone or to nobody.

- What door did I hesitate at today, and what happened when I went through it?
- Where did I want to be rid of something that has to be carried a while longer?
- Was there an hour today when I wanted everything at once?

## A short prayer

*Give me the courage to knock, and the plainness to say who I am. Let me be content to carry what I must until the carrying is done. Teach me the patience of someone who can wait for the best things. Open the door tonight, and let me sleep inside it.*
