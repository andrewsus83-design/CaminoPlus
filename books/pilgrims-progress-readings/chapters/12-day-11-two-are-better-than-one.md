# Day 11. Two are better than one

*What keeps you awake, near the end of a long road?*

## The passage

> I saw then in my dream, that they went till they came into a certain country, whose air naturally tended to make one drowsy, if he came a stranger into it. And here Hopeful began to be very dull and heavy of sleep; wherefore he said unto Christian, I do now begin to grow so drowsy that I can scarcely hold up mine eyes, let us lie down here and take one nap. CHR. By no means, said the other, lest sleeping, we never awake more. … HOPE. I acknowledge myself in a fault, and had I been here alone I had by sleeping run the danger of death. I see it is true that the wise man saith, Two are better than one. Hitherto hath thy company been my mercy, and thou shalt have a good reward for thy labour. … CHR. Now then, said Christian, to prevent drowsiness in this place, let us fall into good discourse. HOPE. With all my heart, said the other. CHR. Where shall we begin? HOPE. Where God began with us. But do you begin, if you please. CHR. I will sing you first this song:-- When saints do sleepy grow, let them come hither, And hear how these two pilgrims talk together: Yea, let them learn of them, in any wise, Thus to keep ope their drowsy slumb'ring eyes. Saints' fellowship, if it be managed well, Keeps them awake, and that in spite of hell.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Enchanted Ground (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

There is an hour after lunch that every walker knows. The sun is warm, the road is straight, the stomach is full, and the eyelids have begun to weigh something. Nothing is wrong. You are only comfortably, dangerously tired, and a patch of grass by the path looks like the best bed in the world.

Bunyan has a country for that hour, and it comes late in the story, after the mountains, when the end is not far. It is called the Enchanted Ground, and its air *naturally tended to make one drowsy*. Hopeful, who has walked through everything with Christian, says that he can scarcely hold up his eyes, and he suggests that they lie down for one nap. Christian says no, and says why: *lest sleeping, we never awake more*.

It is worth being fair to Hopeful. In a sentence that is not in the passage, he says that *Sleep is sweet to the labouring man*, and that they might be refreshed by a nap. That is true, and a good deal of walking wisdom agrees with it. A rest in the shade is not a sin, and a body that cannot rest cannot walk. Nobody should take this passage as a rule against sitting down. If it is hot, or you are unwell, or you are simply spent, stop, drink water, and rest in a safe place, not by the edge of a road or a path where you may not be seen.

What Christian is afraid of is narrower and sadder. In Bunyan's story the Enchanted Ground lies near the end of the journey, and the shepherds on the mountains had warned the pilgrims about it. The danger, as we read it, is not tiredness. It is going slack when the end is near: lying down in the middle of the road and not getting up. It is a country where plans can be dropped within sight of the finish.

So what is Christian's remedy? Not a rule, and not a shout. He says, *let us fall into good discourse*. Let us talk. Hopeful agrees with all his heart, and when Christian asks where they should begin, Hopeful gives a good answer: *Where God began with us. But do you begin, if you please*. In other words, tell me how it started. Why did you set out? What was it that made you leave? In the lines that follow, Hopeful tells the whole of his own story, and it is long, and a good deal of it is about doctrine, and we have not quoted it. The point is the question. *How did you come to start this?* is the best question to ask on a drowsy afternoon, and it works as well on a bench in the sun as on any Enchanted Ground.

There is a sentence in the passage that is worth reading again slowly. Hopeful says: *had I been here alone I had by sleeping run the danger of death*. And then: *Hitherto hath thy company been my mercy*. Hopeful is quoting the Bible's book of Ecclesiastes, where the reasoning is plain: two are better than one, because if one falls the other can lift him up. That is as clear a statement of what companionship is for as you will find. It does not say that company is pleasant, although it often is. It says that company has been a mercy, because it kept him awake. And the little song that Christian sings ends with the same idea: fellowship, if it is managed well, keeps people awake.

Two honest things. First, not everyone on this road has a Christian beside them, and some walk alone because they want to. Bunyan is not against solitude. Christian walks alone for long stretches of the book. Later in the same country a young man called Ignorance says that he takes his pleasure in walking alone, and Bunyan means him as a warning. We do not need to follow Bunyan there. A person who walks alone can stay awake in other ways: a phone call to someone at home, a song sung aloud, a podcast, a conversation with a farmer over a wall.

Second, the talk in the book does not stay light. After this passage it becomes long and doctrinal, and sharp, and in the end Christian and Hopeful go on without Ignorance, and the book leaves no doubt which side it is on. You can take the question and leave the rest.

## For the walk

- Who is awake with me today, and whom am I helping to stay awake?
- When the drowsy hour arrives, ask whoever is beside you how it began: *how did you come to start this?* Then listen. If you are walking alone, ask a friend by message, or say it out loud to yourself, quietly: when did the idea first come, and what was happening then? See whether the answer wakes you up.

## At the end of the day

The drowsy hour has come and gone. Go back to when it was, and what it felt like. A slow heaviness after a meal, an irritable dullness in the middle of the afternoon, a sudden wish to give up and take a bus. And what woke you? It may have been a hill, or a shout from a car, or someone's voice, or a joke, or the next waymark, or only your own stubbornness. It helps to know what works, because tomorrow it will come again.

A heavy lunch and a hot sun deepen the slump. If today's was a bad one, notice what you ate and how much you drank, and try something lighter and more water tomorrow before you blame your character.

If you walked with a companion today, think of what they gave you without knowing it. Not a great deal, perhaps. A remark about the weather, a pace you could match, a silence that was easy. Hopeful's phrase is *thy company been my mercy*. Is there someone whose company was a mercy to you today? It would be good to tell them, in words, before you sleep. A mercy that is not named tends to go unnoticed.

Notice, too, that the first thing Christian does after proposing talk is to sing. Did you sing today, even under your breath? It is an old and cheap way of keeping the eyes open on a long road.

If you walked alone, what kept you company? A song, a thought, a landscape, a call home? There is no shame in any of it. Some days you need people, and some days you need to be left to yourself, and the road is wide enough for both.

It is also worth saying that the end of a road can be the dangerous part. When it comes within reach, there is a pull to go easy: to cut a corner, to skip the stretching, to stop looking at the waymarks. Feet that have got you this far still need their care, and a map still needs reading, and a bottle still needs filling. A road is not over until you have stopped walking on it.

Plan for tomorrow's slump. If the drowsy hour came at about the same time today as it did yesterday, plan for it. Stop a little before it arrives, in shade, and eat something and drink water, instead of waiting until your head drops. A short, chosen stop is better than a long, forced one.

Last, the opposite of the Enchanted Ground is a proper night. A drowsy afternoon is often the body asking for something you did not give it last night. Tonight, sleep as well as you can: a dark room, a quiet bunk, no screen in your hand. Sleep that is chosen is the best answer to sleep that is simply fallen into.

- When did the drowsy hour come today, and what woke me?
- Whose company was a mercy to me today, and have I told them?
- If I walked alone, what kept me company?

## A short prayer

*Keep me awake to what matters, and let me sleep soundly when the time comes. Give me a companion for the long afternoons, and let me be one for someone else. Teach me to ask how it began. Do not let the nearness of the end make me careless.*
