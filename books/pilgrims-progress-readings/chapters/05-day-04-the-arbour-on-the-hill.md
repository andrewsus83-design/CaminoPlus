# Day 4. The arbour on the hill

*How do you climb, and where do you let yourself rest?*

## The passage

> He ran thus till he came at a place somewhat ascending, and upon that place stood a cross, and a little below, in the bottom, a sepulchre. So I saw in my dream, that just as Christian came up with the cross, his burden loosed from off his shoulders, and fell from off his back, and began to tumble, and so continued to do, till it came to the mouth of the sepulchre, where it fell in, and I saw it no more. … I looked, then, after Christian, to see him go up the hill, where I perceived he fell from running to going, and from going to clambering upon his hands and his knees, because of the steepness of the place. Now, about the midway to the top of the hill was a pleasant arbour, made by the Lord of the hill for the refreshing of weary travellers; thither, therefore, Christian got, where also he sat down to rest him.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Cross and the Hill Difficulty (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

There is a gradient at which conversation stops. You know it when you reach it: the sentences get shorter, then a single word, then only breathing and the sound of boots. Somebody ahead of you has their hands on their knees, and you do not need to ask why.

The passage has two halves, and they are very different in weight. The first is the heart of Bunyan's story. Christian, still bent under the burden, comes to a rising piece of ground, and on it stands a cross, and a little below it a sepulchre, which is an old word for a tomb. As he comes up to the cross, the burden loosens from his shoulders and falls from his back, tumbling until it drops into the mouth of the tomb, and he sees it no more. In the lines that follow, he gives three leaps for joy and goes on singing. Three shining beings tell him his sins are forgiven, strip off his rags and dress him in clean clothes, set a mark on his forehead and give him a roll with a seal on it to carry. This is a Christian image, and for Bunyan the centre of everything. We say so plainly. A reader who does not share the faith can still see the shape, and the shape is this: the weight comes off at a place, not as a prize for effort, and not before the story says so. Christian did not shrug it off. It fell.

You may recognise something in that, whatever you call it: a grief that eased on an ordinary afternoon without being asked, or a decision that stopped hurting. It does not come by timetable, and Bunyan is careful about that. It took Christian a bog, a gate and a good deal of running to get here. Most days nothing of the sort happens, and the burden is still on your back when you wake. Some days it does not.

The second half of the passage is about the very next thing, and it is a hill. Bunyan calls it the Hill Difficulty, and the narrow way goes straight up it. Notice how he describes the climb: Christian *fell from running to going, and from going to clambering upon his hands and his knees*. There are three gears, and he goes through all of them, and Bunyan does not say a word against him for it. Anyone who has gone up a long hill in the heat knows that sequence. The man who ran at the cross is on his hands and knees by the top, and nothing in the book suggests he has done anything wrong.

Halfway up, he finds a pleasant arbour, a shaded seat that the *Lord of the hill* has put there for the refreshing of weary travellers. Someone thought of you. Someone built a seat, in a shady place, at exactly the point where a walker would want one. A bench by a path, a fountain in a square, a shelter with a roof and a wall, a tap at a churchyard gate: none of them is yours, and all of them are meant for whoever comes. Christian sits down to rest, and the passage stops there.

The story does not. He falls asleep, and his roll slips out of his hand. He walks on without it, and only at the top does he feel for it and find it gone, and then he has to go all the way back to the arbour to look for it, scolding himself at every step. Bunyan is severe about this: the arbour, he says, was only meant for *a little refreshment for his weariness*. We think he is too hard on the man. A rest is part of climbing, and a sleep in the shade is not a sin. But the practical sense in the story is right, and it is easy to take. Before you leave a place where you have rested, touch your pockets.

A pilgrim's nearest thing to the roll is the *credencial*, the folded card that walkers carry and have stamped along the way (a stamp is a *sello* in Spanish, a *carimbo* in Portuguese). We are not saying that Bunyan's roll is a credencial, and it is not the same thing. We only mean that there is something you cannot do without, and a bench is the very place you may leave it.

One caution, in plain words. If a climb brings chest pain, faintness or confusion, that is not the time for Bunyan's three gears. Stop, sit in the shade, drink, and ask someone for help. In Spain and Portugal the emergency number is 112.

## For the walk

- Which gear am I in right now, and is it the right one for this hill?
- When you next sit down, whether it is a bench, a wall or a kerb, stay long enough to drink some water and look at the view. Then, before you stand up, touch each pocket and the top of your pack once, and say aloud what is in it. It takes ten seconds, and it is worth it.

## At the end of the day

If there was no hill today, there was something that felt like one: a long, straight afternoon, a stretch of tarmac, an argument that would not leave your head. The gears were the same. Think about which one you were in for most of the day, and whether you chose it or only found yourself there.

Running, going, clambering. If you want a rule for tomorrow's hill, go at a pace at which you could still say a short sentence aloud, and drop a gear before you have to. Most days you will go through all three, and it is easy to forget that the order does not matter. A day that began at a run can end on hands and knees, and the other way round. None of it tells you anything about the road, or about you, except what your legs know. Bunyan does not shame his man for the crawl. It is worth the same courtesy tonight.

Then the arbour. Where did you stop today, and what did the stopping give you? A rest that leaves you more tired than before is not a rest, and a rest that lasts too long can cost an afternoon, as Christian found. Both are easy mistakes, and the cure for both is only to notice. Did the bench do its work? Did you thank whoever put it there, in your head or aloud? Someone also put out the water, mended the path, painted the arrow, and kept the church open so that you could sit in the cool. Most of the kindness of a road is done by people you will never meet.

And did the burden shift at all? It may have, and if so, take the lightness as it comes and do not examine it. It may not, and then Day 3 still holds: be content to bear it a while longer. The cross in the story stands at a particular place, and nobody gets there by trying harder.

Tonight, unlike Christian, you are allowed to sleep, and to sleep soundly. A day of hills leaves the body glad of any chair, and an evening is a poor time for hard thinking. The questions below will keep.

Last of all, the pockets. It is the most ordinary thing in the whole reading, and the one most likely to matter tomorrow. Count what you came with: the pass, the phone, the wallet, the hat. If you carry a credencial, take it out and look at today's stamp, with its place and its date and its slightly smudged ink. It is a small proof that you were here, and the kind of thing that is easy to lose and hard to replace. If something is missing tonight, it is better to know now, while the place where you may have left it is still open.

- Which gear was I in for most of today, and did I choose it?
- Where did I rest, and did the rest give back what it took?
- Did I leave anything behind today, on a bench or in a café, that I should go back for, or let go?

## A short prayer

*For the hill, give me breath. For the bench, give me thanks. If my burden slips from me, let me notice. If it does not, let me carry it well. Let me wake in the morning with everything I came with.*
