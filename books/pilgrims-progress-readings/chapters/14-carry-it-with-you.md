# Carry it with you

You have reached the end of the twelve days, or you have stopped early, or you mean to start again more slowly. Any of those is fine. This book was made to keep you company on a road, and a road has little use for a book once it is over, except to be remembered kindly.

Bunyan ends his own story with a request. He has told his dream, and now he asks the reader to make of it what they can.

> Put by the curtains, look within my veil, Turn up my metaphors, and do not fail, There, if thou seekest them, such things to find, As will be helpful to an honest mind. What of my dross thou findest there, be bold To throw away, but yet preserve the gold; What if my gold be wrapped up in ore?-- None throws away the apple for the core.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, The Conclusion (English original, 1678)

It is a generous way to close a book. The author gives you leave to throw away what does not fit, and to keep the gold. We would say the same of ours. Some of these days will have rubbed, and some passages will have asked more than you could give, or less than you hoped. That is allowed. If a day did nothing for you, nothing has gone wrong.

## What to keep

Not everything. Nobody carries a whole book. Here are four small things to choose from.

- **A step.** From the second day: when the ground goes soft and fear hurries your feet, go to the plain things first. Water, food, a seat, the next waymark. They were laid there for you.
- **A hand.** *Give me thy hand*, says Help in the bog, and Christian gives it. Ask when you need to, and give when you can.
- **A key.** Somewhere in your pocket there is a reason, a sentence or a name that you forgot you had. Take it out now and then and look at it.
- **A voice.** The one ahead of you in the dark, who thought he was alone too. You may be that voice for someone else, and a greeting costs nothing.

If you keep even one of these for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from anything.

## How to go on

Go back whenever you like. The days are short, and they were written to be read more than once. A day that did nothing in the first week may say a great deal in the fifth. Read them out of order. Read only the evenings for a week. Read one passage aloud on a bad morning, and nothing else.

You might also read Bunyan himself. This book has taken twelve small passages out of a longer story, and the original is in the public domain, in the same text we used: Project Gutenberg ebook number 131. Bunyan wrote a second part some years later, which this edition does not contain. Read it a page at a time, and skip what is hard. His own closing verse allows it.

And be honest about what you leave behind. Bunyan's world is stern, and a modern reader may close the book with questions about judgement, about the family Christian left, about the pages we did not quote. They are fair questions, and they keep.

## A last word

John Bunyan wrote a story about a man on a road and called it a dream. He gave the road a bog, a gate, a hill, a house, two valleys, a fair, a castle, a mountain, a drowsy country and a river. They are the weather of a long walk. What he added, in the end, was company: a hand in the bog, a voice in the dark, a friend in the water.

That is worth taking home. You do not have to know exactly where the road goes. You need the next step, a little patience with yourself, and now and then a person at the right moment to say that they can feel the bottom.

Walk again. Next year, if you can. Or tomorrow, round the block, with your phone left behind and your hands open.

*Bom caminho, and Buen Camino.*
