# Day 8. What will ye buy?

*What do you actually want from today?*

## The passage

> And as they wondered at their apparel, so they did likewise at their speech; for few could understand what they said; they naturally spoke the language of Canaan, but they that kept the fair were the men of this world; so that, from one end of the fair to the other, they seemed barbarians each to the other. … But that which did not a little amuse the merchandisers was, that these pilgrims set very light by all their wares; they cared not so much as to look upon them; and if they called upon them to buy, they would put their fingers in their ears, and cry, Turn away mine eyes from beholding vanity, and look upwards, signifying that their trade and traffic was in heaven. … One chanced mockingly, beholding the carriage of the men, to say unto them, What will ye buy? But they, looking gravely upon him, answered, "We buy the truth."
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, Vanity Fair (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

The road passes through towns, and towns sell things. There are bakeries, cafés, a pharmacy where you can ask for a plaster, a stall with shells on strings, a shop whose window is full of every kind of walking stick. A pilgrim on foot is in the market whether or not they mean to be. You are carrying money, you are hungry, and you are new.

Bunyan has a town of his own for this, and a fair in it that never closes. It is called Vanity, and the fair is Vanity Fair. Before the pilgrims arrive, Bunyan lists what is sold there, and it is a long list: houses and lands, honours and titles, pleasures of every kind, and in the end lives and souls. It is a picture of a world in which everything has a price, and the pilgrims have to pass through it because the road to the city goes straight through the town.

Take care with this one. Bunyan's fair is not the café where you will have lunch, and it is not a verdict on the people who sell bread and postcards to walkers. A village is a home, and many of the people who live on a pilgrim road keep the Way going. Nothing here should make you look down on a stall-holder. It is a satire, and it is aimed at a particular kind of greed, and the pilgrims in it are hardly models of manners. What Bunyan has caught is how it feels to be a stranger who is passing through.

First, you are different. The pilgrims' clothes are unlike those of anyone who trades in the fair, and the whole town stares. Then comes a line that any foreign walker will understand: *few could understand what they said*. Bunyan has them speak the language of Canaan, which is his way of saying that they talk like people from another country, and the town and the pilgrims seem like barbarians to each other. You may know a small version of this: a conversation in which both sides are friendly and neither follows. The boots, the pack, the sunburn and the shell make you a visible stranger. That can be a kind of freedom, and it can be a weight.

One sentence from a stretch we have not quoted is worth keeping. Anyone who wants to go to the city and yet not go through this town, Bunyan says, *must needs go out of the world*. There is no road round it. A pilgrimage is not a way of avoiding money, shops and people with something to sell. It goes through them, and the question is how you walk.

Second, they do not look at the wares. When the sellers call to them to buy, the pilgrims put their fingers in their ears, and cry *Turn away mine eyes from beholding vanity, and look upwards*. And then comes the best question in the passage. Someone says, half in mockery, *What will ye buy?* And the pilgrims look gravely at him and answer: *We buy the truth.*

Nobody would recommend that answer to a stall-holder. A stall-holder who asks what you will buy is only doing a job, and the polite reply is *a coffee, please* or *just looking, thank you*. But as a question put to your whole day, it is very good. What are you buying with this walk? Not with the euros in your pocket: with the hours. You may not have a noble answer. Time, a quiet head, a body that works again, a stamp in a book, a long talk, an end to a bad year. Some answers are only a way of saying that you need to be somewhere else for a while. All of them are fair, and it is useful to know which one is yours.

Now the hard part. In the pages after this, the town turns on the pilgrims, and what follows is not a market at all. They are mocked, beaten, put in a cage, and brought before a judge. One of them, Faithful, is condemned and burned at the stake. Bunyan had himself been jailed for what he believed, which may be why these pages are so bitter. We have stopped where it is still only a question, and we do not read on. Nor should you read it as a rule that a pilgrim is always right and a town is always wrong. Real towns are neither.

What it asks of an ordinary day is small. Before you go into a shop or a café, know what you came for. Come out with it. Keep your head up, and let the town go about its own day, which is not yours. And be a good stranger: thank people, in their language if you can, and give a plain answer to a plain question.

## For the walk

- What do I actually want from the next hour?
- Before you go into a shop or café today, stop at the door and say to yourself, in a few words, what you came in for. Buy that, and say thank you in the local language: *obrigado* if you are a man, *obrigada* if you are a woman, in Portugal, or *gracias* in Spain. Notice what the pause does.

## At the end of the day

Count up what you bought today. Water, bread, something sweet, a hot drink, a plaster, a postcard. If you are honest the list is probably short, and a few of the things on it were not needed. That is all right. A day is allowed to have a pastry in it. Some of what you bought, you bought because you were tired, or lonely, or bored, and a sweet thing at four o'clock can save a stage. There is nothing to confess. It is only worth knowing which of your purchases were hunger and which were something else.

Then think about what else you spent, because there is another currency, and it is attention. Where did your attention go today? Some of it went where you meant it to go, on the path, on a view, on a person. Some of it went on a screen, or on a thought about the end of the road, or on whatever the next sign was selling. You do not have to be hard on yourself, but it is useful to see where it went, since you have only so much.

Remember where you felt like a stranger. It may have been the way a conversation stopped when you walked into a shop, or a joke you could not follow, or a language you could not read on a menu, or only the sense of being the one person in a square who was going somewhere. What did the strangeness show you? Some people find that it makes them kinder, because they are less sure of themselves. Some find that it makes them stiff. Both are normal. Neither lasts.

Bunyan's pilgrims were not well treated, and the fair thought them mad. You were, we hope, treated much better. Think of the ones who sold you something without fuss, or gave you directions, or only nodded as you went by. A village is a home, and you passed through the middle of somebody's day. A thank-you is the smallest rent there is. If you forgot to give one, there will be another chance tomorrow, and the person at the next counter will be just as glad of it.

It is also worth looking at what cash you have left for tomorrow, since small places on country roads do not always like to take a card, and a long stretch between towns is a poor place to find out.

Last of all, the question. What will ye buy? If you had to answer it for the whole road in a single word, what would the word be? You do not have to say it tonight, and it may change by the weekend. But once it has been said, even silently, you may find that you walk a little differently the next morning.

- What did I actually want today, and did I get it?
- Where did I feel like a stranger, and what did it show me?
- Whom did I thank today, and in whose language?

## A short prayer

*Let me want what is enough. Let me be a good stranger and a grateful guest. Where I am stared at, let me be gentle. Where I am passed over, let me not turn bitter. Let me spend my attention on what is true.*
