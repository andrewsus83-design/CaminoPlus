# About the book and its author

## The man

John Bunyan was born in 1628 at Elstow, near Bedford in England, and died in 1688. He was a tinker, which is to say a mender of pots and pans, and later a preacher in Bedford, among the Protestants who would not conform to the Church of England and who were punished for it in his day. The file we use says none of this: these are general facts, and we give the dates as "about" ones in the sense that sources disagree on small details. What the same sources agree on is that Bunyan was jailed in Bedford for preaching without leave, and that he is usually said to have written the first part of this book there. Scholars differ about which of his imprisonments it was: a long one that began in about 1660, or a shorter one in the mid-1670s. The book was first printed in 1678.

## The book

*The Pilgrim's Progress* is an allegory. Bunyan says in the opening lines that he lay down in a "Den" and dreamed, and the whole story is told as that dream. The edition we use carries the full title *The Pilgrim's Progress from this world to that which is to come* and the words "delivered under the similitude of a dream". It opens with a verse apology, in which Bunyan explains with some humour why he wrote it: he did it, he says, to gratify himself, and then he showed it round, and some said *let it live* and some said *let it die*. It ends with a verse conclusion, which asks the reader to look "within my veil" for what is useful.

The edition holds the first part only. A note at its head says that Bunyan wrote a sequel some years afterwards, and that the first part is "a complete work in itself". The sequel, which follows others on the same road, is not here, and we do not quote it. The first part follows one man, Christian, from the City of Destruction to the gate of the Celestial City, with a companion, Faithful, for part of the way, and another, Hopeful, for the rest. The places he passes have names that say what they are: the Slough of Despond, the Hill Difficulty, the Valley of Humiliation, Vanity Fair, Doubting Castle, the Delectable Mountains, the Enchanted Ground.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

Bunyan's own verse says it plainly: *This book will make a traveller of thee*. Here is the stanza.

> This book will make a traveller of thee, If by its counsel thou wilt ruled be; It will direct thee to the Holy Land, If thou wilt its directions understand: Yea, it will make the slothful active be; The blind also delightful things to see.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, The Author's Apology for his Book (English original, 1678)

It is a story of feet and weather, of bad ground and good company, of being lost and being found again. It has a man who sinks in a bog, a hill that makes him crawl, a house where strangers take him in, a smooth path that turns out to be a trap, and a friend who holds his head above water. Anyone who has walked for days will know the sort of thing it means.

## What is hard, and what we left out

It is also a book of its century, and a severe one. We say what is hard, so that you are not surprised by it in the original.

- The burden Christian carries is, for Bunyan, the weight of sin and the fear of judgement. The book takes hell as real. Christian leaves his wife and children behind when they will not come, and the first part does not make that easy.
- Two places in the first part name the Pope as an enemy: a cave at the end of the Valley of the Shadow of Death, where two giants once lived, and a line about Rome in the description of Vanity Fair. A pilgrim walking to Santiago may well be Catholic and may wince. We left both out, and say so here instead of passing over them.
- The Interpreter, a guide Christian meets early, shows him a man shut in an iron cage who says he cannot repent. Vanity Fair ends with a trial, a death and a burning. In the dungeon of Doubting Castle, the giant urges his prisoners to kill themselves, and one of them refuses. The Enchanted Ground gives way to a long conversation that is mostly about doctrine. The book closes on a man who came to the gate without the paper he needed, and who is taken away. We have left out the cage, the burning and the last page, and we deal with the dungeon with care on Day 9.

We took twelve short passages, one for each of twelve stretches of the road, from the narrative and the verse, and nothing from the many notes the edition also carries. The order is Bunyan's own. The choosing and the reflections are ours.

## The edition and the licence

The text is *The Pilgrim's Progress*, by John Bunyan, in the original seventeenth-century English, as prepared by Project Gutenberg volunteers and published there as ebook number 131. The file names no translator and no modern editor, and says nothing of a year. The date 1678 is the well-known date of the first part, and it is general knowledge, not something the file tells us. The text is old enough to be in the public domain, and every quotation in this book was checked against it, word for word.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. We kept the old spellings, capitals and punctuation, and the labels that mark who speaks. The edition has numbers in curly brackets between its paragraphs, Bible references in square brackets, and short notes in the margin. They are the editors' or Bunyan's marginal notes, not part of the story, and we did not copy them. Where a passage skips a stretch, or a note stood between two sentences, a mark like this shows it: …. Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
