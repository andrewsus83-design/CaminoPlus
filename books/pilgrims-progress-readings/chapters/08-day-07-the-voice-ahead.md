# Day 7. The voice ahead

*Who else is on this stretch with you, and may be thinking they are alone?*

## The passage

> The pathway was here also exceeding narrow, and therefore good Christian was the more put to it; for when he sought, in the dark, to shun the ditch on the one hand, he was ready to tip over into the mire on the other; also when he sought to escape the mire, without great carefulness he would be ready to fall into the ditch. Thus he went on, and I heard him here sigh bitterly; for, besides the dangers mentioned above, the pathway was here so dark, and ofttimes, when he lift up his foot to set forward, he knew not where or upon what he should set it next. … When Christian had travelled in this disconsolate condition some considerable time, he thought he heard the voice of a man, as going before him, saying, "Though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for thou art with me." … Then he was glad, and that for these reasons: First, Because he gathered from thence, that some who feared God were in this valley as well as himself.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Valley of the Shadow of Death (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

Every walk has its grey hours. The light goes flat, the path is the same in every direction, and nothing you can point to has gone wrong. Or something has gone wrong, and you cannot see how to mend it, and the next step is a matter of faith in the ground. Such hours do not need a night to happen in, although a night will do.

Bunyan gives them a place, the Valley of the Shadow of Death, and it is the darkest page of his road. The narrow way runs between a deep ditch on one hand and a dangerous bog on the other. The ditch, he says, is where the blind have led the blind. The bog is a quag with no bottom, and even a good man who falls into it cannot find a place to put his foot.

That is the first half of the passage, and its sharpest line is about steering. When Christian tries to keep clear of the ditch, he is ready to tip over into the mire. When he tries to keep clear of the mire, he is ready to fall into the ditch. Most people know that dance in some form. You push too hard because you fear being late, and then you fear the pain and stop. You try to be cheerful after a gloomy morning, and cannot keep it up. You swing from one danger to the other, and the path between them is only as wide as your foot. It is dark besides, and when he lifts his foot to move forward, *he knew not where or upon what he should set it next*. Few sentences say it so well: you do not know what the next step will land on, and you take it.

The second half is a small miracle. In the dark, some way along, Christian thinks he hears a man's voice ahead of him. It is saying words that he knows, from the twenty-third Psalm: though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for thou art with me. And Christian is glad, and he gives his reasons. The first is the one the passage keeps: some who feared God are in this valley as well as himself. He is not the only one. Someone has gone ahead of him, in the same dark, saying the same words.

A little later he calls out to the man in front, and gets no answer. Bunyan explains why: the man in front *also thought to be alone*. Hold that one for a minute. The voice ahead of you thought it was alone. Whoever is a few hundred metres up the path, in the same weather, with the same ache, may think so too, and may not have heard you, and may not know you are there. Not every call is answered. But hearing someone was enough to change the valley, and in a little while the day broke, and Christian said that God had turned *the shadow of death into the morning*.

The detail that comes next is as true as any in the book. In the daylight, Christian looks back, *not out of desire to return, but to see* what he had come through in the dark. He sees how narrow the way was between the ditch and the mire. It is hard to look back at a bad stretch with any other purpose than wishing it away. To look at it in good light, simply to know what it was, is another thing, and it is a form of courage.

Three honest remarks. First, this valley is full of terrors that the passage leaves out, fiends and flames and a mouth of hell beside the path, and the book says Christian was worse put to it here than in his fight with Apollyon. Among them are blasphemous thoughts whispered in his ear, which he took for his own. If thoughts come to you that you did not choose and cannot shake, and they are heavy or last for days, talk to a doctor or someone you trust. Asking for that kind of help is no weakness. It is the same thing as calling out to the voice ahead. Second, at the far end of the valley the book turns to a quarrel of Bunyan's century, and the About chapter says what we left out. Third, a plain safety word. An actual road in the dark is not a place for proving anything. Start in daylight, and if dusk catches you, stop at the next place with a roof and a light. In an emergency the number in Spain and Portugal is 112.

## For the walk

- Who else is on this stretch of the road, ahead of me or behind, who may think they are alone?
- In the quiet part of the walk, say one line aloud that you would like someone behind you to hear: a greeting, a verse, the last words of a song. It need not be anything solemn. A voice ahead may be the only company somebody has this hour. If you hear one ahead of you, notice whether it helps.

## At the end of the day

The grey hour of the day has probably passed by now, whatever it was. Go back to where it began. It may have been a long road beside a field with no end, or the mid-afternoon dip, or a moment of fear that came from nowhere. Perhaps there was a moment when two possible mistakes stood on either side, and any step looked wrong.

In the story, Christian's path is narrow between the ditch and the mire. What were your two? Starting too fast against stopping too early, perhaps. Pressing on against giving in. Telling someone how you felt against saying that you were fine. Most of us have a favourite ditch. It helps to know which one it is, because that is the one we fall into when we are afraid.

Then look at that stretch once more, in the light of evening, as Christian does at dawn: not out of desire to return, but to see what it was. It is often smaller than it felt when you were inside it. It is sometimes exactly as narrow as it felt, and then it is something you have come through, and you can say so.

Think now about the voices ahead. Was there one today? It might have been a laugh at a café, a language you could not follow in a tone you understood, a pair of boots you saw far up a path, a message that arrived from home when it was needed. And whose voice might you have been for someone behind you? You may never know. A greeting, said without thought, can be the one thing someone hears that day.

There is a careful point in the second half of the Valley, beyond the passage. When the sun comes up, Bunyan says that the rest of the way is, if anything, more dangerous than the first half, so full of traps and pits that Christian could not have crossed it in the dark. But he can see them now. It is a useful thing to know. Many hard things can be faced in daylight that cannot be faced at night.

One small job for tonight. If tomorrow's start is before sunrise, a head torch is worth having, and worth checking tonight while there is still a light to check it by. Do not set out into an unfamiliar dark on a bad road if you can wait for the dawn.

If tonight is low, be plain about it. Tiredness paints everything grey. Eat something, wash, lie down, and take no large decision before morning. If the low feeling is more than tiredness, and does not lift, or grows, tell someone: the host, a friend, a doctor. If you are in danger, the number is 112.

- When did the path narrow today, and which two dangers was I swinging between?
- Whose voice did I hear ahead of me, and whose might have heard mine?
- What looks different tonight, in the evening light, from how it looked this afternoon?

## A short prayer

*In the dark stretches, let me hear a voice ahead of me. Let me be one for someone behind. Show me where to set my foot, and if I cannot see, let me stand still until the light. Turn the shadow into morning.*
