# Day 2. Look for the steps

*When fear hurries you, what do you miss?*

## The passage

> Now I saw in my dream, that just as they had ended this talk they drew near to a very miry slough, that was in the midst of the plain; and they, being heedless, did both fall suddenly into the bog. The name of the slough was Despond. Here, therefore, they wallowed for a time, being grievously bedaubed with the dirt; and Christian, because of the burden that was on his back, began to sink in the mire. … HELP. But why did not you look for the steps? CHR. Fear followed me so hard, that I fled the next way, and fell in. HELP. Then said he, Give me thy hand: so he gave him his hand, and he drew him out, and set him upon sound ground, and bid him go on his way. … True, there are, by the direction of the Law-giver, certain good and substantial steps, placed even through the very midst of this slough; but at such time as this place doth much spew out its filth, as it doth against change of weather, these steps are hardly seen; or, if they be, men, through the dizziness of their heads, step beside, and then they are bemired to purpose, notwithstanding the steps be there; but the ground is good when they are once got in at the gate.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Slough of Despond (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

By the second day the first-morning energy has gone, and what is left is the road as it really is. The legs are stiff in new places. The thing you were looking forward to has turned out to be mostly ground. Somewhere in the middle of the morning a small voice asks what you were thinking, and the odd thing is that it asks it when nothing in particular has gone wrong.

Bunyan has a place for that voice, and he gives it a name. Christian has set out with a neighbour, Pliable, who has been won over by talk of a better country. They are walking along in high spirits, describing its pleasures to each other, and they are *heedless*. The ground gives way, and they are both in a bog. It is called the Slough of Despond. A slough is a marsh, and to despond is to lose heart, so the name says exactly what the place does.

The first thing to notice is when it happens: at the height of the good talk, not the bottom of a bad day. A hopeful hour is no protection, and sometimes it is the very time the feet are least careful.

The second is how differently the two men take it. Pliable asks where Christian is now, and Christian answers truthfully that he does not know. Then Pliable turns on him: *Is this the happiness you have told me all this while of?* If this is how things go at the very start, he says, what will they be like by the end? With a struggle or two he gets out on the side nearest his own house, and goes home. Christian is left behind, still struggling in the mire because of his burden.

Then Help arrives, and the exchange that follows is short. He asks a question, not a scolding: why did you not look for the steps? Christian's answer is the truest line in the passage. Fear followed him so hard that he took the nearest way, and fell in. Fear hurries people. It makes the first path look like the only one, and it takes away the patience to look about for the one that was laid there for you. Help does not lecture. He says *Give me thy hand*, and Christian gives it.

The steps are the other thing to notice. Bunyan says there are good, sound steps placed right through the middle of the bog. But when the place is at its worst, in a change of weather, the steps are hard to see, and a tired, giddy head steps beside them. That is the rhythm of a bad patch on a long walk. The arrows are still there. A grey morning makes them harder to read, and a mind that is tired or frightened looks for any way out and not for the marked one.

Here is a practical way to read it. The steps are the plain things that do not depend on your mood: water, something to eat, a bench, a dry sock, the next waymark. They are not cures for despondency. They are only what is under your feet while you work out what you feel. When the ground goes soft, go to the plain steps first, and decide about everything else afterwards.

Now the hard part, and Bunyan is not gentle about it. Pliable, who goes home, is soon mocked by his neighbours for turning back, and later in the book he is held up as a warning. We do not need to take that over. On a road, turning back, cutting a stage short, taking a rest day or a bus to the next town are not disgraces, and sometimes they are the only sensible thing to do. The only caution in Bunyan's story that carries over is a quiet one: do not decide about the whole road in the middle of the bog. Decide on a dry day, after a meal and some sleep.

And where is Help? He is not guaranteed. The story has him walk up at the right moment, and a road does not always oblige. Sometimes help is a stranger, sometimes it is a message from home, sometimes only a wall to sit against. What Christian does that is within anyone's reach is the last thing: he puts out his hand. In a bog that may be the hardest step of all.

## For the walk

- Where did fear make me take the nearest way today, and not the next step?
- When a doubt turns up, say *slough* quietly to yourself, the way you might name a bird. Then do the plainest step in sight: a drink of water, a sock straightened, a look for the next waymark. Treat the doubt as weather and the steps as ground.

## At the end of the day

The second evening is often the one on which the body sends in its first proper bill. A knee, a heel, a shoulder, a back that was fine yesterday: they will all want a word. Give them one before the questions, with water, a stretch and your feet up for a few minutes.

Think back to the moment today when the day tipped. Most days have one. It was not the hardest hour, probably, and it may not have had any special cause. It could have been a long stretch beside traffic, or the hour after lunch, or the first climb, or the point at which you realised how far you still had to go. A small, quiet voice said that this was a mistake. Bunyan has Pliable say it aloud, as if to someone else: *Is this the happiness you have told me all this while of?* It is worth asking who the *you* is. It may have been a friend who talked you into this road. More often it is the part of you that talked yourself into it, and that part deserves an answer before bed.

Then look for the steps. There may have been some that you took without noticing: a sit-down at the right time, a bite of food that cleared your head, a stranger's *Buen Camino* that came at the very moment you needed it. If you took none, and got through anyway, the day still had steps, only you did not see them. Either way it is useful to learn which ones work on you, because tomorrow's bog will be like today's.

A kind word for Pliable, whom Bunyan is hard on. He was not wicked. He was a man who found the going rougher than the talk. Most walkers have been Pliable at least once in an afternoon, and some have been Pliable for a whole morning and then got up and gone on. Bunyan is hard on him, and you need not be hard on yourself. Doubts are always larger in the evening, when the body has spent its sugar. A hard decision about the whole road does not belong on a bunk at nine o'clock at night. Eat first, sleep, and decide in the morning, if there is anything left to decide.

Bunyan makes a wry point in the same scene, in words we have not quoted. Help says that for ages labourers have been tipping cartloads of wholesome advice into the slough, and that it is a slough still. Advice does not drain despondency. If someone you walked beside today sank into a bog of their own, a hand will probably do more than a good reason, and a cup of something hot more than either.

- When did the day tip into doubt today, and what was my body asking for at that moment?
- Which plain steps did I take today, whether or not I noticed them?
- Is there a hand that was offered to me today, in words or in kindness, that I did not take?

## A short prayer

*When the ground goes soft under me, show me the step. When fear hurries my feet, slow them. Give me a hand to hold, and the grace to take it. Let the doubts settle in the dark, and let me wake on firmer ground.*
