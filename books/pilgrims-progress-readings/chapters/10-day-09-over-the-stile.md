# Day 9. Over the stile

*What do you do when an easier path runs beside the hard one, and what do you carry that you have forgotten?*

## The passage

> Now, I beheld in my dream, that they had not journeyed far, but the river and the way for a time parted; at which they were not a little sorry; yet they durst not go out of the way. Now the way from the river was rough, and their feet tender, by reason of their travels; so the souls of the pilgrims were much discouraged because of the way. … Now, a little before them, there was on the left hand of the road a meadow, and a stile to go over into it; and that meadow is called By-path Meadow. Then said Christian to his fellow, If this meadow lieth along by our wayside, let us go over into it. Then he went to the stile to see, and behold, a path lay along by the way, on the other side of the fence. It is according to my wish, said Christian. Here is the easiest going; come, good Hopeful, and let us go over. … Now a little before it was day, good Christian, as one half amazed, brake out in passionate speech: What a fool, quoth he, am I, thus to lie in a stinking Dungeon, when I may as well walk at liberty. I have a Key in my bosom called Promise, that will, I am persuaded, open any Lock in Doubting Castle. Then said Hopeful, That's good news; good Brother pluck it out of thy bosom and try.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, By-path Meadow and Doubting Castle (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

You know the stretch. The proper way is cobbles, or stones the size of eggs, or hard tarmac that rings in your heels, and beside it, over a low fence or through a gap, runs a strip of soft grass that looks as if it had been laid for tired feet. Nobody would blame you for looking at it.

Bunyan puts exactly that in front of Christian. The river that had kept him company moves away from the way, and the road becomes rough. The pilgrims' feet are *tender, by reason of their travels*, and their spirits sink with them. A little ahead there is a meadow on the left, and a stile to climb over into it. A path runs along the other side of the fence, side by side with the road. *It is according to my wish*, Christian says, and so it is. *Here is the easiest going*.

Pay attention to Hopeful, the companion who joined Christian after the fair. In the lines the passage skips, he asks the right question: *how if this path should lead us out of the way?* Christian says it does not seem likely, since it runs along the road. And Hopeful, being persuaded, goes over the stile after him. The doubt was spoken, and heard, and then it gave way. It is a common way to go wrong in a pair: one says perhaps, the other says it will be fine, and neither is quite sure.

What follows is Bunyan at his sternest. A man called Vain-confidence, who is walking ahead and says the path goes to the gate, falls into a pit in the dark and is killed. There is thunder and rain, the water rises, and the two cannot get back to the stile. Bunyan's narrator adds a line to himself, in brackets, which is the wisest thing in the episode: *it is easier going out of the way, when we are in, than going in when we are out*. They sleep under a shelter, and in the morning they find that it is in the grounds of Giant Despair, whose castle is called Doubting Castle. He takes them, beats them, and shuts them in a dungeon. They are there from Wednesday morning until Saturday night, with no light, no food and no water.

We say plainly what we have not quoted. On the second day the Giant tells the prisoners that their only way out is to kill themselves, and Christian, in his misery, is half inclined to agree. It is Hopeful who refuses, and what he says is the heart of the whole episode: *let us be patient, and endure a while*. He points out that they have been through worse, that the Giant has fits and may lose the use of his limbs, and that a way out may come. If the thought of ending your life has ever been near you, or comes near on this road, please tell someone today: a doctor, a friend, someone at home, the host where you are staying. If you are in danger now, the emergency number in Spain and Portugal is 112. Despair is a place, and a place can be left.

In the passage, you meet the way out. Before dawn on the Saturday, Christian cries out, as if waking: what a fool he has been, to lie in a stinking dungeon when he could walk free. He has a key in his bosom, called Promise, that will open any lock in the castle. He had it all along. Hopeful says it is good news, and tells him to pluck it out and try.

What is the key? In Bunyan's book it is the promises of God, which Christian had stopped thinking of. For you it may be something quite different, and you may be the only one who knows: a reason you set out, a letter, a sentence somebody said once, a plan for a bad day, the plain fact that you have come this far. The thing about the key is that it was in his pocket all the time. The key did not turn every lock with ease, either. One of them, in the lines after the passage, was very stiff, and it opened all the same. Christian and Hopeful had also spent the night praying, which may be the nearest thing a person has to staying awake together and not giving up.

There is one more thing in the story, and it is the kindest. When they first discover that they are lost, Christian says he is sorry, he had led his brother out of the way, and will Hopeful please forgive him. And Hopeful answers: *Be comforted, my brother, for I forgive thee; and believe, too, that this shall be for our good*. When they get out, they set up a pillar at the stile, with a warning cut on it for those who come after.

## For the walk

- What easy path runs beside me today, and what does my own small doubt say about it?
- At the next junction, fork or tempting shortcut, stop for ten seconds before you step off the way. Look for the waymark, and say Hopeful's question aloud to yourself: *how if this path should lead us out of the way?* Then decide. If you do take the shortcut, take it knowing why.

## At the end of the day

If the going was rough today, begin with your feet. Take off the socks, look at the soles and between the toes for anything red, wet or tender, and let the skin dry in the air. Tender feet do not get tougher by being ignored.

Most days have a stile in them. It may have been small: a short cut across a car park, a skipped stretch, a bus you did not need to take, a conversation you cut short because it was easier. Some of them are fine. A road with a good alternative is not a sin, and nobody should be made to walk in the wrong shoes for a principle. The question is only whether you asked, as Hopeful did, where the easy path goes.

If you did ask, and went over anyway, notice what it cost, if anything. Bunyan's line says that going out of the way is easier than coming back, and that is a truth about habits as much as about paths. A small stile today can become a large one by the end of the week.

Doubting Castle is a place you may not have visited today, and if so, be glad. But every walker visits it a little, in the form of a long afternoon in which everything looks hopeless and nothing in particular is wrong. If you did, what was in your pocket? It need not be large. It may be a text from someone, or a photograph, or a line you copied on the first day. Take it out now and look at it, because keys are easiest to find in the evening. And if you did spend the afternoon in the castle, and are still there tonight, say so to someone. A bunk in a quiet room is a poor place to keep a giant.

Tiredness is a poor adviser about shortcuts. A day that began with a plan and ended with a bus may have been exactly right, or it may have been the afternoon's fatigue making decisions for the morning. You can tell the difference tomorrow, with a clear head, and meanwhile nothing needs to be settled.

Turn to your companions, too. Did you say something sharp today, to someone you are walking with or beside? Did someone say something to you? Christian and Hopeful began their trouble with an apology and a forgiveness, and it is not hard to believe that this helped them through the days that followed. If there is a sentence waiting to be said tonight, say it.

There is the pillar, too. They did not just escape. They left a sign. It is worth asking what sign you could leave: a word for the next walker about a bad stretch, a note in a visitors' book, a kind word to someone who is a day behind. Much of what you rely on along this road was left by someone who went before.

- When was the easy way tempting today, and what did I ask before I took it?
- What do I carry that I have forgotten I have?
- Is there someone I should forgive, or ask to forgive me, before I sleep?

## A short prayer

*When the easy way calls, let me ask where it goes. When I am lost, let me forgive and be forgiven quickly. Remind me what is in my pocket. Keep a light for whoever is coming behind.*
