# Day 12. I feel the bottom

*At the last crossing, whose feet are on the ground?*

## The passage

> Now, I further saw, that betwixt them and the gate was a river, but there was no bridge to go over: the river was very deep. At the sight, therefore, of this river, the Pilgrims were much stunned; but the men that went in with them said, You must go through, or you cannot come at the gate. … They then addressed themselves to the water and, entering, Christian began to sink, and crying out to his good friend Hopeful, he said, I sink in deep waters; the billows go over my head, all his waves go over me! Selah. … Then said the other, Be of good cheer, my brother, I feel the bottom, and it is good. … Then they both took courage, and the enemy was after that as still as a stone, until they were gone over. Christian therefore presently found ground to stand upon, and so it followed that the rest of the river was but shallow. Thus they got over.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the River and the gate of the Celestial City (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

Near the end of any long walk there comes an odd mixture. You want to arrive, and you do not want it to be over. You can see how far you have come, and a part of you would like to turn round and do some of it again. If today is your last walking day, you may know the feeling already. If it is not, you will meet it soon.

Bunyan's last scene is built on that mixture, and we should say plainly what it is. The river in the passage is death, and the city beyond it is heaven. That is how Bunyan means it, and he does not hide it. We are not pretending that the last day of the Camino is a death, and nobody who arrives at the end of a pilgrimage has crossed that water. But the shape is the shape of every ending: a stretch that has to be gone through, with no bridge and no way round. Some readers will have a loss behind them on this road, and this may be the day that touches it. Go slowly with it.

Look at what the passage gives. The pilgrims have been led to within sight of the gate, and between them and it lies a river, and it is very deep. There is no bridge. Two shining men who have walked with them say, without ceremony, *You must go through, or you cannot come at the gate*. They ask if there is another way, and the answer, a line later, is that only two people in the whole of history have been let by. So they go into the water.

At once Christian begins to sink. His cry borrows its words from the Psalms, and it is the most naked sentence in the whole book: *I sink in deep waters; the billows go over my head, all his waves go over me!* He is the man who fought Apollyon and walked through the Valley of the Shadow, and he is afraid in a way he has not been before.

Then Hopeful speaks, and his sentence is the plainest in the scene. *Be of good cheer, my brother, I feel the bottom, and it is good.* He does not say that he is not afraid. He does not make an argument. He says what his feet have found. There is a floor under the river, and it holds.

In the lines between, we are told what the crossing costs. Christian loses his hold of everything. He cannot remember the good things of his road. He is troubled by the sins of his whole life, and by the phantoms of hobgoblins and evil spirits. Hopeful has *much ado* to keep his brother's head above the water, and sometimes Christian goes under and comes up again *half dead*. Christian says: *it is you, it is you they wait for; you have been Hopeful ever since I knew you*. And Hopeful answers: *And so have you*. It is a better thing than a speech. Two friends who have walked a long way tell each other the truth in the water.

Then the turning. Christian hears a word that holds him, and they both take courage. He finds ground to stand on, and *the rest of the river was but shallow*, and they get over. On the far bank, waiting for them, are the two shining men who had led them down to the water.

There are two hard things to say. First, Bunyan's account of the crossing is Bunyan's, and it is a comfort he offers from his own faith. It is not a promise that all endings are easy, or a prediction. The terror in it is real, and Bunyan allows it. Second, the book's last page, which we have left out, turns dark: a man who reaches the gate without the paper he needed is taken away. Bunyan ends with a warning. We have stopped at the river.

What it asks of an ordinary day is something we all know and seldom do. When you cannot feel the bottom yourself, let someone tell you that they can. And when it is your turn to be the one with your feet on the ground, say so plainly, and say it while you are still holding the other person up. At the end of the road, and at the end of anything, the person beside you matters more than your own courage.

## For the walk

- Who has held my head above water on this road?
- Choose one person who walked beside you, for a day or for a long way, and thank them by name. Say it aloud if they are near, or send it in a message if they are not. Tell them one specific thing they did, however small, and do not spoil it with a joke.

## At the end of the day

If this is your last evening on the road, it may feel strange. The day was a day like the others, and it ended, and the thing that you were walking towards is either behind you or next door. If you are not at the end of your road tonight, keep this page for the evening that you are.

Let the twelve days go past you, quickly, in the order they came. A great weight on the first morning. A bog that came on the second day, and a hand that came down for you. A gate that opened to a knock, and a burden that did not drop. A hill that you crawled up, and a bench halfway. A house where somebody asked where you had come from. A valley where a voice read out your faults, and another where you heard someone ahead in the dark. A town that sold everything. A smooth path that went wrong, and a key in a pocket. A high place with a shaky view. An afternoon in which two were better than one. And now the river.

Arrival itself may have been loud, or flat, or something you could not name. If you wept at an odd moment, or felt nothing at the expected one, neither was wrong. A threshold is allowed to be whatever it is.

Did the burden fall? You may know, and you may not. The book promised nothing about that. If it did fall, somewhere, on a day you did not expect, then say thank you, to someone or to nobody. If it did not, *be content to bear it* still holds. A road has not failed you because the weight is still there. It may be that it is lighter, or better understood, or only better carried.

Think of the ones who have held your head up. It might be a person who walked beside you for a week, or one who said a word in a café that made the next hour possible. It might be a friend by message, or a host who put out a hot dish at the right time. Hopeful's answer to Christian, *and so have you*, is worth taking to bed. Whoever held you up was being held up by someone as well.

You may find your first day without an arrow to follow a little strange. If so, it will pass, and it needs no explanation. Eat well, sleep on, and let the legs have their say.

All the bells in Bunyan's city ring at the end of the story. We will not say that any particular bell, in Santiago or anywhere else, is that bell. But a bell is a bell. If one rings tonight, in whatever town you have reached, let it count for you.

- Who held my head above water on this road, and have I thanked them?
- What do I want to carry away from these days, and what shall I leave here?
- What is the plain bottom under my feet tonight, the thing I can stand on?

## A short prayer

*For the ground under my feet, for the hand that held me, for the road behind me and whatever comes after: my thanks. Let me finish gently. Let the bells be rung for everyone who arrives, however they arrive. Let me sleep.*
