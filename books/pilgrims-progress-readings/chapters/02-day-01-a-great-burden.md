# Day 1. A great burden

*What are you carrying that does not show on the scales?*

## The passage

> As I walked through the wilderness of this world, I lighted on a certain place where was a Den, and I laid me down in that place to sleep: and, as I slept, I dreamed a dream. I dreamed, and behold, I saw a man clothed with rags, standing in a certain place, with his face from his own house, a book in his hand, and a great burden upon his back. … I looked, and saw him open the book, and read therein; and, as he read, he wept, and trembled; and, not being able longer to contain, he brake out with a lamentable cry, saying, "What shall I do?" … In this plight, therefore, he went home and refrained himself as long as he could, that his wife and children should not perceive his distress; but he could not be silent long, because that his trouble increased.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the opening of the dream, the City of Destruction (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

The first morning has a small ceremony of its own. You lift the pack, swing it onto your shoulders, tighten the straps until the weight sits on your hips and not on your neck, and take a first step out of a door that is not yours, in Porto or wherever your road begins. Somewhere in those few seconds you learn what you have really brought, and the kilos you packed are the smallest part of it.

Bunyan opens his book with a man who is carrying something he cannot put down. The book is an allegory, a story in which every person and place stands for something else, and Bunyan tells it as a dream. He lies down in a *Den*, falls asleep, and sees a man in rags, with his back to his own house, a book in his hand and a great burden on his back. The burden is not luggage. In Bunyan's story it is a person's guilt, and the fear of what comes after death, which the man has found by reading his book. Not every reader will share that fear, or put it that way. It is worth seeing what Bunyan does with it all the same, because he is shrewd about the human side.

The man opens the book and reads, and as he reads he weeps and trembles, and then he cries out, *What shall I do?* That is the whole first move of a pilgrimage: a plain question, said aloud. It is not a plan and it does not come with an answer. Many walks begin there, in a trouble that has outgrown the place where it was kept.

Notice, too, how Bunyan places him. He is standing with his face turned from his own house. He has not left yet. He has only turned round, and that is where most journeys really begin, a good while before the first step, in a turning of the head that nobody else can see.

Then comes a detail that is easy to read past. He goes home and holds himself in *as long as he could*, so that his wife and children will not see. Anyone who has carried a worry to the dinner table and talked about something else knows that sentence. When he can no longer hold it in he tells them, and they do not believe him. They decide that some fever has got into his head, and they put him to bed, hoping sleep will settle him. The night is as bad as the day.

Here is where the passage is harder than it looks, and we should say so. The passage stops before the man sets off, and when he does he runs, with his fingers in his ears, and leaves his family behind. Bunyan does not make that easy. Much later in the book a woman asks him why he did not bring them, and he weeps and says how gladly he would have done it. If you are walking away from people for a fortnight, you may find that this part of the story presses on a bruise. A phone call home is allowed.

There is a second hard thing. For Bunyan the burden is sin, and the danger behind the man is a city that will be burned. You may carry something quite different, a bereavement, a decision, a year of work that wore you down, a question you have not dared to ask, or nothing heavier than a wish for a different kind of day. The story does not need you to carry his burden. It only asks you to take seriously that you are carrying one. People set out on this road for many reasons, and some of them have no burden at all but curiosity. Bunyan's road has room for that too.

What does this ask of an ordinary first day? Mostly honesty about the weight. The pack you can check, on a scale or by the ache in your shoulders. The other weight you can only notice. It helps to know that you do not have to solve it today. Nothing in the story takes the burden off on the first page. It will be a long way before it falls, and Day 4 of this book comes to that. For now the job is smaller, and the man in the dream has already done it: stop, look at what is on your back, and say aloud that it is there.

And one doubt, because this book would rather be honest than soothing. Some days the burden is only a poor night's sleep, a tight hip and a lunch that was too far away. Not everything needs to be an allegory, and a day is allowed to be only a day.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying today that is not in the pack? Ask it once, lightly, and do not hunt for the answer.
- At your first stop, loosen the straps and let the pack rest on the ground for a full minute. While it rests, name the other weight in one word, to yourself, and tell nobody. Then lift the pack again and notice whether it feels any different.

## At the end of the day

The boots come off, and the day makes its first honest report: where the strap bit, where a seam rubbed, which sock was a mistake. After a first day the body always has more to say than the mind, and it is worth listening before you do anything else. Air your feet, look for any spot that is hot or tender before it turns into a blister, drink some water, and eat something with salt in it. The questions below can wait ten minutes.

Think back to the question from the morning. Perhaps you did not give it a thought, and the road took all your attention: the signs, the feet, the next turning. That is not a failure, and it is a kind of rest. Perhaps it came and went, at a bench or in a long flat stretch, and each time it came it was a little smaller or a little larger than you expected. Either way, you now know something that you did not know at breakfast, which is how it feels to walk with it.

Bunyan's man, in the pages just after the passage, stands still in the field because he cannot tell which way to go. He looks this way and that as if he would run. That may be how this evening feels, to be out on the road without yet being sure why. It is allowed. In the story a stranger comes up, and he does not give the man a map. He points over a wide field and asks whether he can see a light, and says to keep that light in his eye. For the next two weeks a yellow arrow on a wall or a stone is the nearest thing to that, and nobody needs more than the next one.

There is also the matter of the people you left. Perhaps one of them crossed your mind today, in a village, on a bridge, at a table for one. It may be worth sending them a line tonight: two sentences, no more, to say where you slept. It costs little, and it can lighten the evening more than a hot shower. If nobody is waiting, a line in a notebook does the same job: a record that you were somewhere, on this date, and that you came through it.

One practical thing belongs here too. If the pack felt heavier than you hoped, tonight is the time to ask whether one item can go: sent home, given away, left on a shelf. The other weight cannot be posted anywhere, but it too can be shifted a little, just by admitting what it is.

Be gentle about tiredness. A first evening is the worst time to judge a road, because the body has not yet learned the work and the mind is trying to learn it all at once. If the answer to *what shall I do?* feels too large tonight, shrink it to what you can see: eat, wash, lie down. Tomorrow is a day's walk, not a verdict.

- What did I carry today that I did not pack?
- Whom did I think of, and is there one thing I would like them to know?
- What is the smallest step I can take tomorrow morning?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I set the pack down, and let the weight be what it is. I do not have to solve it before sleep. Show me tomorrow's first step, only that one. Keep the people I have left, and keep me.*
