# Day 6. All this is true

*What do you say when the voice you dread reads out your faults, and every item is accurate?*

## The passage

> APOL. Thou didst faint at first setting out, when thou wast almost choked in the Gulf of Despond; thou didst attempt wrong ways to be rid of thy burden, whereas thou shouldst have stayed till thy Prince had taken it off; thou didst sinfully sleep and lose thy choice thing; thou wast, also, almost persuaded to go back at the sight of the lions; and when thou talkest of thy journey, and of what thou hast heard and seen, thou art inwardly desirous of vain-glory in all that thou sayest or doest. CHR. All this is true, and much more which thou hast left out; but the Prince whom I serve and honour is merciful, and ready to forgive; but, besides, these infirmities possessed me in thy country, for there I sucked them in; and I have groaned under them, been sorry for them, and have obtained pardon of my Prince.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Valley of Humiliation (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

Downhill is not the easy half of a walk. Anyone whose knees have taken a long descent knows it: each step lands a little harder than the one before, the toes press into the front of the boot, and a loose stone is a real danger. Bunyan knew it too. When the household at the Palace Beautiful walk Christian down to the valley, he goes down *very warily*, and still *caught a slip or two*. His hosts give him a loaf of bread, a bottle of wine and a cluster of raisins before they leave him, and that is the right provision for a downhill stretch.

The valley has a name that sounds like a punishment, the Valley of Humiliation. It is only low ground. Humility and humus, the Latin word for earth, share a root, and the valley is simply the place nearest the ground. But Bunyan's low ground is not quiet. Almost at once a monster comes over the field to meet Christian. He has scales like a fish, wings like a dragon, feet like a bear, fire and smoke from his belly, and a mouth like a lion's. His name is Apollyon. Christian thinks about turning back, and then remembers that he has no armour for his back, so he stands his ground.

What the monster does first is not fight. He talks. He claims Christian as his subject, he urges him to come home, he offers him better wages, and he says that servants of the other master tend to come to a bad end. When none of that works he does the cleverest thing a critic can do, and reads Christian his own record. That is the passage.

Look at the list. It is not made up. Christian fainted at the beginning, when he was almost choked in the bog, which Apollyon calls by a slightly different name. He tried wrong ways to be rid of his burden, a detour we did not read. He slept when he should not have, and lost the roll that was his pass. He was nearly talked into going back at the sight of the lions. And when he talks about his journey, says the monster, he is secretly after praise. Three of those five are things this book has already shown you, one at a time, in these pages: the bog, the sleep, the lions. The accuser has been keeping notes.

Now notice what Christian does. He does not argue. He does not say that the bog was not his fault, or that anybody would have slept, or that the lions were frightening. He says: *All this is true, and much more which thou hast left out*. That is a remarkable sentence. He confesses the list and adds to it. He is more honest about himself than his accuser. And in the same breath he goes on: the Prince he serves is merciful and ready to forgive, these weaknesses were already in him when he left his old country, and he has been sorry for them and has been pardoned.

There is the pattern of the day. A voice reads out your faults, and it is accurate, and the test is what you do with the accuracy. The accuser wants it to be a reason to turn round. Christian takes it as a plain account, says *that is true*, and carries on walking. Humility of that sort is not self-contempt. It is accuracy about yourself, held without panic.

Two hard things need saying. First, the story is a fight with a devil, and the fight takes more than half a day and leaves Christian wounded and nearly beaten before it is done. Some readers will put a name like the inner critic, or the voice at three in the morning, in place of Apollyon. The passage works either way, and we need not choose for you. Second, the answer *all this is true* does not fit every accusation. Some are unfair, and some are exaggerated, and some come from people with their own reasons. If the voice is saying that you are worthless, that is not true, and it is not a list at all. And where the true thing is that you hurt someone, humility asks for more than a sigh. It asks you to put it right.

In an ordinary day it asks something small. Notice what the critic says on the descent, on the mile you are walking slowest, on the day somebody passes you. Ask which part is simply the case. A slow pace, a wrong turn, a sharp word at breakfast: say that it is true, and then take the next step. Humility is a good companion on a downhill, because it keeps your eyes on your feet.

## For the walk

- If the voice in my head read out today's faults, which would be simply true?
- Choose one small true fault, perhaps your pace, a wrong turning or a grumble, and say to yourself, quietly, *that is true*. Then walk ten steps without arguing with it or adding to it. On the tenth step, add one plain sentence of your own: *and I am still walking*. See whether it changes the weight.

## At the end of the day

Before any questions, a plain thing. Have you eaten? Christian is given bread, wine and raisins before he meets the monster, and not after. A critic is much louder on an empty stomach, and most accusations sound less final once there is soup in the body. If the voice was hard on you today, look at your supper before you look at your soul.

Then go back to the descent, whichever it was. A real one, with sore knees, or a figurative one: a mistake, a mood, a conversation that went lower than you wanted. What was the critic's version of the day? Try to put it into three or four sentences, in its own words, harsh and complete. That may be harder than it sounds, because we are used to hearing it as a noise and not as a text.

Then do what Christian does. Go through the sentences, one at a time, and mark the ones that are true. You will probably find some. Say so, plainly, without defending. And then notice what the critic left out. It left out the miles. It left out the hour you sat with someone else's trouble, the water you gave, the moment you took the correct turning after hesitating. Christian says his accuser has left out *much more*, and he means the faults. You may find that the accuser has left out much more of the other kind too. A list that is accurate only in one direction is not an account.

There is a line in the fight itself that is worth taking to bed. At the worst of it Christian is flat on the ground, and his sword has flown out of his hand. He reaches for it and catches it, and says *when I fall I shall arise*. He does not say that he will not fall. He has just fallen. The sentence is about what happens after.

If one of the true items on tonight's list was about another person, something said sharply or something left undone, then put it where you can find it tomorrow. A short message, a word at breakfast, a small amends. It does not have to be large, and it does not have to be done tonight. It only has to be done.

It is also all right to find that none of it is true. Some days the voice is wrong, and then the honest thing is to say that, and to go to bed.

And be easy on your legs. A day of descent is hard on the knees and calves, and the night is the time they send their reply. Water, a stretch, a seat with the feet up, and bed.

- What did the critic in my head say today, and which part was simply true?
- What did it leave out?
- Have I eaten and drunk enough to hear it fairly?

## A short prayer

*Let me be honest about what I am, and kind about what I am not. When my faults are read to me, let me say that is true and keep walking. Feed me before the fight. Teach me to go downhill with care, and to be at home on low ground.*
