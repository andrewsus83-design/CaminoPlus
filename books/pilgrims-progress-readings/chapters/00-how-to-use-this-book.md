# How to use this book

This is a small book for twelve days. It is made to go with the Camino Portugués from Porto, a road of about two weeks and ~240 km, with a day or two to spare for rest and rain. Read one day for each day of walking, or one a day at home, and nobody will mind if you skip one, repeat one, or save a day for a rest day.

*The Pilgrim's Progress* was written by John Bunyan in the seventeenth century, and it is an allegory of a pilgrimage: a story in which every road, house and traveller stands for something else. Its hero, Christian, sets out from the City of Destruction with a heavy burden, and the book follows him, place by place, to the gate of a distant city. That is why it fits any road, and not only this one. It was never a guidebook to a particular way. It is a story about what a long walk does to a person: the weight, the bog, the borrowed comfort, the hill, the fright, the wrong turning, the company, the river.

Every day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A short stretch of Bunyan's own words, copied exactly from the public-domain text of the first part of his book, old spelling and old grammar and all. *Thee* and *thou* and *hath* are there. Read the passage twice, the second time aloud if you can, even under your breath. Old English slows the eye down, and that is half its use. Speakers in the story are marked as Bunyan marks them, by short labels such as *CHR.* for Christian.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Bunyan's. It starts from something on the road, a strap, a stile, a bench, and lets the passage open it. It says what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. Most of them are better done on your feet.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have lived, with the morning's passage in mind, and two or three questions to lie down with. You do not need to write anything.
- **A short prayer.** Original and short. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

*The Pilgrim's Progress* is a Christian book, of a strongly Protestant kind, and it takes sin, judgement and hell more seriously than most readers do today. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Bunyan believed to recognise the bog, the hill, the wrong turning and the river. Where the book is hard, we say so, and the next chapter says what we have left out.

## The shape of the twelve days

The days follow the story in its own order, from the first page to the last.

- **Days 1 to 4, setting out.** The burden, the bog, the gate, and the hill that follows the cross.
- **Days 5 to 8, the middle of the road.** A house that takes pilgrims in, a valley of humility, a valley of darkness, and a fair where everything is for sale.
- **Days 9 to 12, trouble and arrival.** A smooth path that goes wrong, a high place with a view, a drowsy country, and a river.

The shape is a shape, not a timetable. The book cannot know which day of your walk is the hard one. Read the day that fits the day you are having. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off. At home it works the same way: the morning with your tea, the evening before bed.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a communal kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well. Other words are explained where they first appear.

Read slowly. Bunyan wrote to be read aloud, to people with a long day ahead of them.
