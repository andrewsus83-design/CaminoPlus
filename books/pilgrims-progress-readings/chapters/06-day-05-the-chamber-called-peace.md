# Day 5. The chamber called Peace

*Who asks you where you have come from, and what does it do to be asked kindly?*

## The passage

> Then she asked him whence he was, and whither he was going, and he told her. She asked him also how he got into the way; and he told her. Then she asked him what he had seen and met with in the way; and he told her. … So she smiled, but the water stood in her eyes; and after a little pause, she said, I will call forth two or three more of the family. So she ran to the door, and called out Prudence, Piety, and Charity, who, after a little more discourse with him, had him into the family; and many of them, meeting him at the threshold of the house, said, Come in, thou blessed of the Lord; this house was built by the Lord of the hill, on purpose to entertain such pilgrims in. Then he bowed his head, and followed them into the house. … Thus they discoursed together till late at night; and after they had committed themselves to their Lord for protection, they betook themselves to rest: the Pilgrim they laid in a large upper chamber, whose window opened towards the sun-rising: the name of the chamber was Peace; where he slept till break of day, and then he awoke and sang-- "Where am I now? Is this the love and care Of Jesus for the men that pilgrims are? Thus to provide! that I should be forgiven! And dwell already the next door to heaven!"
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Palace Beautiful (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

At the end of a long day the first questions are always the same. Where did you start this morning? How far have you come? Where are you heading tomorrow? They are asked at doors, over soup, across a bunk, and they are asked so often that they can sound like a form to be filled in. Then, now and then, someone asks them as if the answer mattered.

Christian reaches such a place at nightfall, after the hill and the arbour, and after the worst fright of the day. Bunyan sets two lions in the narrow way up to the house, and Christian sees them and stops, and thinks of going back. Then comes a line that is worth keeping: *The lions were chained, but he saw not the chains.* The porter, whose name is Watchful, calls out that they are chained, and that he should keep to the middle of the path. They roar, and do him no harm. It is a very small piece of wisdom for a walker with an active imagination. Some of what frightens you on the way is chained, and you cannot see the chain from where you stand.

At the door a grave young woman called Discretion, who is one of the household, comes out and asks him who he is. The passage gives it in a rhythm that is almost a lullaby. She asks whence he came and whither he is going, and he tells her. She asks how he got into the way, and he tells her. She asks what he has seen and met with on it, and he tells her. Three times Bunyan writes the same plain clause, *and he told her*, and you can feel the story being drawn out of a tired man a piece at a time, and being heard.

Then comes the detail that makes the scene. She smiles, but the water stands in her eyes. She is moved by what he has said. She goes to fetch the rest of the family, and they meet him on the threshold with a welcome: *Come in, thou blessed of the Lord*. This house, they say, was built for such pilgrims as he. He bows his head and follows them in.

Think what that does to a person. To be asked about your road by someone who has time for the answer, and to see it land, is a rare form of rest. It may be more than the bed. Most of us carry the story of the day undelivered, and a good host, or a good friend, or a stranger across a table, is a person to whom it can be delivered. You do not have to make it good. Three sentences will do. The listener does the rest.

There is a second half to the passage, and it is the quietest. They give Christian a large upper room, with a window that opens towards the sunrise, and its name is *Peace*. He sleeps until the day breaks, wakes, and sings, and the song is a kind of astonishment: *Where am I now?* he asks, and cannot believe that he is provided for, that he is forgiven, and that he lives *already the next door to heaven*. It is not a deep thought. It is the plain pleasure of a good bed after a hard day, with morning at the window.

Now the honest part. The house is generous, and it is also a little stern. After the welcome, its members question him closely before supper, even about the family he left behind. He is kept for a few days. They show him the records of the house and then an armoury, and before he goes they dress him from head to foot in armour, because a bad stretch is waiting for him just beyond the next hill. In Bunyan's story, rest is also preparation. And a reader who has not come for any of this should not feel they owe a host an inner life along with the fee. A bunk, a soup and a quiet corner can be hospitality enough.

Bunyan also gives his house a name, the Palace Beautiful, and four women to keep it: Discretion, Prudence, Piety and Charity. They are the virtues of a good host. Perhaps they are the virtues of a good guest as well: to arrive without fuss, to take your turn, and to be glad of what is on the table.

## For the walk

- Who has asked me where I started, and have I let them hear the whole answer?
- Today, instead of asking the next walker you meet which country they come from, ask where they started this morning, or where they began the whole road. Then listen to everything they say, without finishing their sentences. When it is your turn, tell yours in three sentences, and stop. Notice how much you want to say, and how little is needed.

## At the end of the day

Wherever you are sleeping tonight, whether it is a single room, a bunk among twenty, or a floor with a mat on it, the room has a name of its own, and you may not have thought to ask it. Bunyan calls his *Peace*, and he calls it that before anyone has slept in it, as a wish. Name tonight's room for what you hope it will hold.

Peace in a shared room is not guaranteed. Someone will snore, someone will rustle a bag at dawn, someone will shine a torch in your eyes. It is part of what a pilgrim's house costs, and it is a small thing next to what it gives. A courtesy goes a long way in a dormitory: pack the night before, keep the light low, and say good morning to whoever you wake. You are somebody else's noise tonight.

Go back over the day and listen for the stories. Whose did you hear? It need not have been long: where someone planned to stop, why someone had come, whom someone was missing at home. The best listening is the kind that is not interrupted, even when you have a better story to tell. And whose story did you hold back? Some stories wait until the evening, and for some there is no good listener on the road.

The lions, too. What frightened you today, and what turned out to be chained? A road is full of things that look worse from a distance: a dog behind a gate, a stretch of traffic, a hill on the map, a conversation you were afraid to have. Perhaps you passed one without trouble and only realised afterwards that it had not been loose at all. That is worth knowing for tomorrow.

Try telling the day in three sentences, as Christian told his story at the door: where I started, what I met on the way, where I am bound. You may be surprised how much of a day fits into them, and how much of it you can then put down. If nobody is there to hear it, say it quietly to yourself, or write it in a notebook, or send it to someone who would like to know.

It is true that the evening is the loneliest part of the day for many people who walk alone. If tonight is like that, a message home counts as a listener, and so does a bowl of soup at a table where someone else is eating. Nobody asks for a performance.

Be gentle about the end of a long day. If you arrived too tired to listen, or to tell, or to be anyone's company, that is not a failure. A bed, and a quiet room, and a morning at the window are enough for one night.

- Whose story did I listen to today, and did I stay to hear the end?
- What did I fear today that turned out to be chained?
- What would I call the room I am sleeping in tonight?

## A short prayer

*For the roof, the bread and the bed, my thanks. Bless whoever opened the door tonight, and whoever will rise early to close it. Let this be a room called Peace. Let its window face the morning.*
