# Day 10. Leaning upon their staves

*What can you actually see from here, and what is only something like it?*

## The passage

> They went then till they came to the Delectable Mountains, which mountains belong to the Lord of that hill of which we have spoken before; so they went up to the mountains, to behold the gardens and orchards, the vineyards and fountains of water; where also they drank and washed themselves, and did freely eat of the vineyards. Now there were on the tops of these mountains Shepherds feeding their flocks, and they stood by the highway side. The Pilgrims therefore went to them, and leaning upon their staves, (as is common with weary pilgrims when they stand to talk with any by the way), they asked, Whose Delectable Mountains are these? And whose be the sheep that feed upon them? … Then said the Shepherds one to another, Let us here show to the Pilgrims the gates of the Celestial City, if they have skill to look through our perspective glass. The Pilgrims then lovingly accepted the motion; so they had them to the top of a high hill, called Clear, and gave them their glass to look. … Then they essayed to look, but the remembrance of that last thing that the Shepherds had shown them, made their hands shake; by means of which impediment, they could not look steadily through the glass; yet they thought they saw something like the gate, and also some of the glory of the place.
>
> — John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part One, the Delectable Mountains (English original, 1678)

## Reflection

Often the best afternoon of a walk comes straight after the worst one. The ground rises, the weather clears, and a place turns up with water in it and a seat in the shade. You have not earned it in any way you could name. It is simply there, and you are a person who has been through something and has come out on the other side of it.

That is what happens to Christian and Hopeful. They get out of Doubting Castle, they put up their warning at the stile, and they walk on until they reach the Delectable Mountains. Bunyan gives them everything a tired person would ask for: gardens and orchards, vineyards and fountains of water. They drink, they wash, and they eat freely. It is one of the places where the book is least afraid of simple pleasure.

On the tops of the mountains are shepherds feeding their flocks, who stand by the road. The pilgrims go up to them, *leaning upon their staves*, and Bunyan adds a parenthesis in the middle of his sentence, which is the loveliest thing in it: *as is common with weary pilgrims when they stand to talk with any by the way*. It is a very old picture, a walker on a stick, resting on it with both hands, talking to someone who lives in the hills. If you walk with poles, you will know the posture. If you do not, you will find that a wall or a gate does as well.

The shepherds' welcome, a few lines on, is the kind you would want. They ask where the pilgrims have come from, and how they got into the way, and then a third question, which is a better one than most hosts ask: *By what means have you so persevered therein?* How did you keep going? Bunyan notes, with a little of his usual sternness, that few who begin the journey ever reach these mountains. When the pilgrims have answered, the shepherds look on them lovingly and say *Welcome to the Delectable Mountains*. They take them to their tents and give them whatever is ready.

Then they show them the city. There is a hill called Clear, a perspective glass, which is an old name for a telescope, and a promise that the gates of the Celestial City can be seen through it. And here is the detail that is rarely noticed. The pilgrims cannot hold the glass steady. Their hands shake. Bunyan says why: it is *the remembrance of that last thing that the Shepherds had shown them*. They manage to see something, and Bunyan is careful about how much. *Yet they thought they saw something like the gate, and also some of the glory of the place*.

Be clear about what the last thing was, because this is where Bunyan is hardest. Earlier on the same morning the shepherds took the pilgrims to a steep hill where men lay dashed at the bottom, and to a place where blind men wandered among tombs. They said that the blind men had been pilgrims who went over the same stile into the same castle, and were taken, and blinded, and left to wander. Then they opened a door in a hillside, and showed them a by-way to hell. The pilgrims looked at each other with tears running down. That is why the glass shakes.

And that is the true part. Nobody sees clearly with hands that are still shaking from a fright. After a car passes too close, after a dog, after a slip on a wet stone, the body takes a while to settle, and nothing you look at is quite steady. It is not a failure of nerve. You are allowed to wait. And when you do get a glimpse of the thing you were walking towards, it is probably partial: something like the gate, some of the glory. It would be a poor road that showed the whole of it from a hill on the way.

Notice what the shepherds do not do. They do not hurry them. They ask them to stay a while and get acquainted, and they show them things, and they let them look through the glass even though it will not go well. A place that gives you a view you cannot manage, and lets you try, is a generous place.

## For the walk

- What can I actually see from here, and what am I trying to see that is not yet in view?
- At the first high or open place you come to, stand for a minute leaning on a pole, a wall or a gate. Name three things you can see clearly. Then name one that is hazy, far off or hidden by weather, and do not try to make it clearer. Let your hands be still. That is the whole practice.

## At the end of the day

The shepherds' question is a good one to carry into the evening: by what means have you so persevered? Not the grand answer. The small ones. What kept you going today, in the dull middle of it? A particular song, a promise of coffee, a pair of boots in front of you, the thought of a person, the plain stubbornness of putting one foot in front of the other. You may find that it is three or four very small things. Say them over once, as the opposite of a list of failings. They are worth knowing, since they are what you will lean on tomorrow.

If your hands, or your voice, shook today after a fright, from a car or a dog or a bad step, that is how a body works. Sit down, drink something, and let it pass. There is nothing to prove, and no view is worth looking at while you are still shaking.

Think, too, about what you saw today and how much of it was only something like. A glimpse of a distant hill, a first sight of a place you had read about, the end of the road turned from an idea into a few days. A view is a good thing, and a view is not a destination. Be glad of what you saw, and do not hold it to account for what it did not show.

If someone who lives here offered you a word today about tomorrow's weather or the next hill, hold on to it. People who live among hills know things that a map cannot show, and Bunyan's shepherds are the ones who know where the ground is bad. A local's word about a river, a path or a sky is worth more than a confident guess.

The shepherds also give the pilgrims whatever is ready, without ceremony. If a hostel or a table offers you a shared meal tonight, take what is put in front of you. It is one of the oldest courtesies of the road, and it is easier to receive than people think.

And then the note. As the pilgrims leave, one of the shepherds gives them *a note of the way*. Later in the story, when they come to a fork where two ways look equally straight, they forget to read it, and are caught in a net. We leave that scene out, but the lesson is plain enough. Read the note when you are at a stand. Tonight, before you sleep, write one for tomorrow: three short lines, where you turn, what you carry, what you remember. Put it in the pocket you will reach for first. It is easier to write a note when you are rested than to find one when you are lost.

- By what means did I keep going today?
- What did I see clearly today, and what was only something like?
- What will I write on my note for tomorrow's first fork?

## A short prayer

*Let me rest where rest is given. Let the shaking pass from my hands. Show me enough, though it is only something like the whole. Give me a few words on a page to read when I am at a stand. And bless whoever keeps the hills.*
