# The Pine Country: Segovia to Puente Duero

## The character of these days

Four days, ~103 km, across the Tierra de Pinares. The Association's guide describes it as an immense plain, ~100 km long on this road, dotted with pine woods and cut by river gorges, with no mountains, only low plateaus and eroded valleys. It is flat, wide and often quiet.

The pines grow on sand, old dunes that the trees have fixed, the guide says, and the sand is the thing to respect. It slows you down and, the guide warns, can injure you if you try to keep your usual speed. From just before Añe to Simancas it counts more than ten pine woods, adding up to ~30 km in a stretch of ~87 km. Take it slowly, and never force the pace on loose ground.

### Day 5: Segovia to Santa María la Real de Nieva (~32 km)

You leave Segovia by the Puerta de Santiago in the old wall, and the Association's guide traces the way: past the site of a vanished Romanesque church of Santiago, down the walk named for Saint John of the Cross, who liked to stroll there, over the Eresma river, then past the Gothic monastery of El Parral and the octagonal Romanesque church of the Vera Cruz, which it dates to the early thirteenth century.

The villages that follow, Zamarramala, Valseca and Los Huertos, are small. Near Los Huertos the path follows an old railway line, the Segovia–Medina del Campo line that ran from 1884 to 1985 and has been a greenway since 2013, the guide says. Past the Eresma the first sandy pine wood begins. Añe is ~21 km from Segovia and a natural place to split the day, with ~11 km left. Santa María la Real de Nieva was founded in 1392, the guide says, by Catherine of Lancaster after a shepherd's vision of the Virgin, and its monastery church holds the grave of Blanche of Navarre, who died there in 1441.

### Day 6: Santa María la Real de Nieva to Coca (~19 km)

The Association counts ~22 km, so call it ~19 to ~22. Nieva is only ~2 km on from Santa María, the shortest gap between settlements on the whole road, the Association says, with a Romanesque-Mudéjar church. Then the Tierra de Pinares proper: a sandy plain of pines, with the high-speed railway running alongside for a while. Nava de la Asunción comes ~10 km after Nieva.

The path enters the gorge of the Eresma and follows it through a large pine wood to Coca. The Association's guide says the town was Celtiberian and then Roman, that its walls are Roman in origin and were rebuilt in the Middle Ages, and that it has a fifteenth-century Mudéjar castle. It calls Coca the birthplace of the emperor Theodosius the Great. It is a short day, with time left to see the town.

### Day 7: Coca to Alcazarén (~28 km)

Again the sources part: the Association counts ~25 km. You leave Coca by the tower of San Nicolás, cross the river Voltoya, and follow a wide track through pines beside the Eresma to Villeguillo, ~7 to ~10 km on, depending on the guide.

Beyond it the road becomes the Cañada de los Gallegos, also called the Cañada Real de Madrid. Galician reapers walked it, and the Mesta's flocks, and the Association says the three names tell its story: *real* for the stockmen, *de los Gallegos* for the reapers, and *de Madrid* for its reach. After ~2 km you cross from Segovia into Valladolid province. The ~18 km from Villeguillo to Alcazarén are the second longest gap between settlements on the road, the Association says, and the Junta's table gives the same ~18 km. Carry water and food for all of it. Alcazarén is the first town in Valladolid province on the road, and the Junta's guide describes its Romanesque-Mudéjar church of Santiago, with an altarpiece to the Apostle.

### Day 8: Alcazarén to Puente Duero (~24 km)

Valdestillas, ~16 km on, has a name that the Junta's guide explains: the "Valle de las Astillas", the valley of the splinters, from the timber that grew here. A bridge over the Adaja there has cutwaters and an arch that the Junta's guide says go back to the Romans.

Then ~8 km more to Puente Duero. Its medieval bridge over the Duero was blown up by the French in 1812 and rebuilt in the middle of the nineteenth century, the Association's guide says, and the village has been part of Valladolid since 1960. Here the road divides: straight on to Simancas, or north-east into the city. The choices chapter explains both. One more pine wood waits on the way to Simancas, the last of the road, the Association says.
