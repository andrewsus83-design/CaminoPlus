# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino de Madrid starts in the old centre of the capital, at the church of Santiago y San Juan Bautista on the Plaza de Santiago, a short walk from the Royal Palace. The Comunidad de Madrid's tourism pages give that as the start. The present church dates from 1811 and replaced a medieval one. Near it are two streets called Conchas and Veneras, "shells" and "scallop shells", which the Madrid association's guide points out as a sign of how old the pilgrim habit is here.

From there the road runs north and then north-west, almost in a straight line, to Sahagún. The Confraternity of St James describes it that way: over the Sierra de Guadarrama, through Segovia and Coca, across the Duero, and over the Tierra de Campos by Medina de Rioseco. It crosses two regions, the Comunidad de Madrid and Castilla y León, and four provinces: Madrid, Segovia, Valladolid and León.

The Madrid association's guide describes the ground the road crosses in several areas. This book uses five of them, joining the short slope below the pass on the Segovian side to the sierra:

- The foothills, ~50 km from Madrid to Mataelpino, rising from ~650 m to ~1,000 m.
- The Sierra de Guadarrama, over the Puerto de la Fuenfría, the highest point of the whole road.
- The Tierra de Pinares, ~100 km of pine woods and sandy farmland between Segovia and Puente Duero.
- The Montes Torozos, ~50 km of open uplands from Simancas to Medina de Rioseco.
- The Tierra de Campos, ~60 km of cereal plain to Sahagún.

At Sahagún you cross the river Cea by the Puente de Cantos, and the Association's guide says that from there you are on the Camino Francés.

## How long it is, and who says so

The app's catalog gives ~320 km. Published figures differ:

- The Confraternity of St James and the Association's guide say ~321 km.
- The Spanish Wikipedia article gives ~324 km in its infobox.
- The Junta de Castilla y León gives ~325 km, of which ~242 km are in Castilla y León.
- The Association's own website, on its page about itself ("Quiénes somos"), gives ~360 km for "the Camino de Madrid proper" and a pass of "almost 1,900 m", figures that its printed guide does not repeat. This book follows the guide.

The Confraternity says the road takes about two weeks without rest days. This book's thirteen walking days add up to ~325 km on the Junta's figures.

To Santiago itself the Association's guide counts ~676 km from Madrid, and the Junta's figures come to ~690 km. The last ~355 to ~365 km are the Camino Francés from Sahagún, which has its own book in this series, *The French Way*.

## A young road on old ground

As a waymarked pilgrim route this one is new. The Madrid association, the Asociación de Amigos de los Caminos de Santiago de Madrid, which this book calls the Association, says it was founded in 1987. It says that between 1993 and 1996 it researched and traced the road from Madrid by Segovia and Valladolid to Sahagún and marked it with yellow arrows, presented it in September 1996 at an international congress of Jacobean associations in Carrión de los Condes, and published a first guide in 1999, a Holy Year. The Junta's guide calls it the most modern of the traditional routes to Santiago. The Association is a members' association, not a public body, so this book says "the Association says" wherever it leans on it.

The ground, though, is old. The Association's guide puts ~35 km between Colmenar Viejo and Cercedilla on the line of Roman secondary roads, and ~150 km from Cercedilla to Simancas on the line of the Roman road that the Antonine Itinerary, a Roman register of roads and distances, shows between Titulcia, in what is now the Madrid region, and Septimancas, today Simancas. For centuries the stockmen of the Mesta, the old Castilian association of livestock owners, used the same line to move their flocks between the cool mountains of summer and the warm lands of the south in winter. And for almost five hundred years, until the railway linked Castile and Galicia in the middle of the twentieth century, Galician reapers walked these tracks to cut the grain of the two Castiles. The Association calls them the nearest ancestors of today's pilgrims on this road.

## What it feels like

Terrain first. The Association says ~97 percent of the way is earth, with asphalt under three percent, much of it on quiet local roads. The Confraternity calls it almost entirely footpaths and cañadas, and says it is well waymarked with yellow arrows. The Comunidad de Madrid added granite markers and wooden posts between 2001 and 2004, the Association's guide says, and the Junta's guide says the whole road is marked with arrows and milestones.

Then the shape of the walk. A city start with ~11 km of streets. Three days along the foot of the sierra. One big climb. A long drop to Segovia, then flat, wide country where the horizon does most of the work. Villages are small and, in places, far apart: the Association counts a ~7.6 km average between settlements, and ~31 km with none over the sierra.

It is quiet, and you should expect to be mostly alone with it. That is part of the road's character, and part of what it asks of you.
