# Before you go

## The credencial

The credencial is your pilgrim's passport. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says it has two practical purposes: access to the hostels of the Way, and certification when you ask for the Compostela. It says the credencial gives no rights of its own, and that it must be issued by a parish, a confraternity, a diocese, an association of friends of the Way, or another Christian institution connected with the pilgrimage.

In Madrid, the Association issues it in person at its office, and its credencial page says it also gives a short talk on the Way at the same visit. Hours and conditions change, so look at the Association's website first. Better to have it before you leave the city than to look for one on the road. The Comunidad de Madrid's tourism page adds that you can have it stamped in Tres Cantos, at the end of the first day.

Stamps are normally collected where you sleep, the Office says, but also in parish churches, monasteries, town halls and other places. On a road with few hostels, that matters: a church or town hall stamp is as good as any.

## Getting there, and back

Madrid is the capital and largest city of Spain. Its airport is ~13 km north-east of the Puerta del Sol, and Wikipedia calls it the country's busiest, with 68.1 million passengers in 2025. The road starts in the old centre, near the Royal Palace, so you can begin on foot from wherever you sleep.

Getting home is the question to settle early. Sahagún is a small town. Look up trains and buses before you go, and do not plan a tight connection for the day you finish. Cercedilla has a railway station on the Madrid–Segovia line, which the Association's guide says it has had since 1888, and the Confraternity says you can take the train from Cercedilla to Segovia if the sierra is closed to you.

## Training and pace

The Junta's guide says you do not need to be a highly trained athlete, but you should pace your effort to your possibilities, and train with walking sessions beforehand. It suggests starting with shorter stages, adding longer ones after the first week, and averaging ~25 to ~30 km a day. It also advises an occasional rest day.

For this road, do one thing in particular: walk a ~30 km day at home, with your pack, before you leave. The days over the sierra and across the pine country are of that size, and there are few places to stop partway.

## What to carry

Light. The Association's leaflets say no more than ten percent of your body weight, and preferably never more than ~6 to ~7 kg. The Junta's guide says the same rule of a tenth, and warns that over ~8 kg you may regret it.

- **Footwear.** The Junta recommends light boots that let your feet breathe and protect your ankles, with trainers as an option in summer. Whatever you choose, it must not be new. The Association's leaflets add petroleum jelly and technical socks against blisters, and the advice to stop at the first sign of rubbing and look at the spot.
- **Layers and rain gear.** The Confraternity says to expect mountain weather from Madrid to Segovia. Carry a waterproof that also covers your pack, and a warm layer even in summer for the pass.
- **Sun.** Hat and sun cream. The Junta advises against wearing only a T-shirt and shorts in the first few days.
- **A sleeping bag,** which the Junta calls essential for pilgrims' hostels. A light one in summer, a warmer one in winter, the Association's leaflets say.
- **Water and food.** The Junta says to keep your bottle full at all times, and not to carry more than a day's food. On this road, add water for the long gaps (day 4, day 7) and fill up at the last village before them.
- **A small first-aid kit.** The Junta lists plasters, an anti-inflammatory cream and sun cream, among other things.
- **Documents.** Your credencial, your identity document, and your health card or European health card if you come from abroad, the Junta says.

## Money, phones and people at home

The Association's leaflets say there is no need to carry large sums, and that there are cash machines in the main towns. The Junta advises using cards rather than cash. Villages are small, so keep a little cash for them.

Tell someone at home your plan, as the Junta advises, and leave them your list of days. Do not rely on a phone signal everywhere. Save your maps before the sierra.

## Language

Spanish is the language of the road. The Confraternity says it is not impossible to walk it without any Spanish, and strongly urges you to learn as much as you can before you set out.

## Guides and waymarks

The road is marked with yellow arrows, and the Association says a group of its members goes over the markings every spring. In the city of Madrid itself the Association's guide, in its 2014 edition, says the way is not waymarked: the arrows begin at the Plaza de Castilla, so use a map for the streets before it.

Two guides are worth having, and both can be downloaded from their publishers. The Association's guide has maps at 1:50,000 and a description of every place on the road, in Spanish. The Junta's pilgrim guide, 144 pages in the edition read for this book, is published in Spanish, English, French and German, and has a table of distances from Madrid to Sahagún. Both are named at the end of this book.
