# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

The app's catalog rates the Camino de Madrid 3 on its scale of 1 to 5, the same as the Camino Francés. It rates the Camino del Norte and the Camino Primitivo 4, and the Camino Inglés 2.

The Confraternity of St James is blunt about where the difficulty sits. Apart from the climb over the Sierra de Guadarrama, it says, the walking is flat and easy, with no hills of any significance, and it is hard to get lost. The climb itself it puts at ~650 m in ~8 km up to the Puerto de la Fuenfría.

So the slopes are not the test. The test is made of other things:

- One stiff day over the pass, ~31 km long, with no village on it.
- Several days of ~25 to ~30 km on open ground, with little shade.
- Sand in the pine woods, which slows you.
- Services that thin out: the app's route notes say lodging is sparse, and the Association's guide counts ~14 km as the average distance between places that host pilgrims.
- Solitude. You may go a day without meeting another walker.

The Junta's guide says the ideal average is ~25 to ~30 km a day, and it suggests starting with shorter days until your body is used to it. After the first week, it says, you will be ready for longer ones. That is sound advice here, because the road's longest gaps come early.

## Who it suits

- **Walkers who want quiet.** The Confraternity says the road appeals to people looking for solitude and the wide horizons of the *meseta*, the high plain of central Spain, and that very few pilgrims walk it.
- **Anyone who lives in Madrid, or lands there.** The Association traced and marked the road so that pilgrims could leave from the door of their own home. Its guide says Madrid has been the Spanish region that sends most pilgrims to Santiago by the cathedral's counts, as of 2014. You can step out of a flat and be on the Way.
- **People with about two weeks.** The Confraternity says Madrid to Sahagún can be walked in two weeks, and calls it a pilgrimage that suits a busy person.
- **Walkers who have done a Camino before** and want one with more path and less road. The Confraternity says there is more path and less road here than on any other pilgrim route.
- **Walkers who like to plan.** Things are open and shut here in ways a busy road would not need you to know. You will check the day ahead more often.

You do not have to walk it as a pilgrim in the religious sense. The Association says it marked the road in a pilgrim spirit, not as a simple hiking route, and adds that nobody minds if you begin as a walker: the road is long, it says, and generous.

## Who should choose another road

- **If you want company and a bed you never have to check,** choose the Camino Francés. It is far busier and has more services. This book's sister volume, *The French Way*, covers it.
- **If you want to arrive in Santiago in two weeks.** This road ends at Sahagún, ~355 to ~365 km short of the cathedral. Two weeks on this road bring you to a small town in Tierra de Campos, not to Santiago. At the Junta's ~25 to ~30 km a day, the Camino Francés from there is another ~12 to ~15 days.
- **If long hot days with no shade worry you,** think hard about July and August, or walk in spring or autumn. The next chapter has the details.
- **If you cannot comfortably walk a ~30 km day,** plan every long stretch with a split. The chapter on choices shows where.
- **If you are walking for a Compostela from a short walk,** the chapter on arriving explains what the Pilgrim's Office asks.

None of this is a medical opinion. If you have a health worry, ask a doctor before you decide, and test your boots and your pack on a long day at home.

## The short version

Nothing in the walking is technical. The road asks you to be self-reliant: to carry water, to start early, to read the weather over the sierra, and to be content with your own company. If that sounds like what you want, it is a good road.
