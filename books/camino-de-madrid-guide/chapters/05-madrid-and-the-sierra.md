# Madrid and the Sierra: Madrid to Segovia

## The character of these days

Four days, ~101 km, from the pavements of a capital to the top of a granite range and down the far side. The first three days follow the foot of the Sierra de Guadarrama and the fourth crosses it.

The distances in this chapter and the next two are the Junta's: each day is the sum of the town-to-town figures in the table of the Junta de Castilla y León's pilgrim guide. The Association's guide, which counts kilometres its own way, comes within ~3 km on most days, and the text says where it differs more. Neither guide divides the road into stages, so the thirteen days of this book are this book's own grouping. The first four match the four stages the Comunidad de Madrid's tourism pages list from Madrid to Segovia. Many pilgrims split the road differently, and the choices chapter shows where.

### Day 1: Madrid to Tres Cantos (~23 km)

You start at the church on the Plaza de Santiago and walk north through the old city. The Association's guide counts ~11 km of Madrid's streets before open country, and in its 2014 edition the arrows begin only at the Plaza de Castilla, so carry a map for the first part. The way goes up the Castellana, past four skyscrapers and two hospitals, and through Fuencarral, once a village and now a district.

After that the city thins into a path between the railway and the long wall of the Monte de El Pardo, a Mediterranean forest that the guide says was a royal hunting ground by the fourteenth century. Tres Cantos is a young town: the guide says it was planned in the 1970s and its first residents arrived in 1982. A granite waymarker erected by the Comunidad de Madrid in 2004 stands by the path, and you can have your credencial stamped in the town. About half of today is city, and the rise is gentle. Do not push the pace.

### Day 2: Tres Cantos to Manzanares el Real (~28 km)

The Association's guide puts the day at ~27 km. The path climbs along the stream of the Tejada and crosses it on stepping blocks, up to eleven times, and the guide warns that in rainy periods the water can cover the blocks and you must take your shoes off.

Colmenar Viejo, ~12 km from the start, is a natural place to split the day, with ~16 km still to go. Its church, the guide says, has a noble coat of arms carved with scallop shells over the door. Beyond it you follow a *cañada* and cross the Manzanares river by the bridge of the Batán, which the guide calls Roman. Manzanares el Real sits at the foot of La Pedriza, with a fifteenth-century castle. The Camino Mendocino, a road from Guadalajara, joins the Camino de Madrid here, the Comunidad de Madrid says.

### Day 3: Manzanares el Real to Cercedilla (~20 km)

The Comunidad de Madrid describes this stage as one at the foot of La Pedriza that takes you fully into the sierra, and the Junta's guide calls the stretch around Mataelpino demanding. The Association's guide has the path leaving Manzanares westward, crossing the Collado de la Jarosa, and reaching Mataelpino at ~1,080 m, below the peak of La Maliciosa.

Then comes Navacerrada, ~1,200 m, a town. It shares its name with the road pass higher up the range, which is not your route: the Association's guide crosses the road that climbs to it and goes on by tracks to Cercedilla. Cercedilla, at the foot of the Siete Picos, was a Roman *mansio*, a staging post, on the same road you will walk tomorrow, and the guide says it has had a station on the Madrid–Segovia line since 1888. Fill your bottles here. Tomorrow, on the direct way, has no village on it.

### Day 4: Cercedilla to Segovia (~31 km)

The big day. The Association's guide counts ~31 km between Cercedilla and Segovia, the longest gap between settlements on the whole road. The Confraternity of St James says the climb is ~650 m in ~8 km, on the old Roman road, to the Puerto de la Fuenfría. Sources give the pass at ~1,790 m (the Comunidad de Madrid) and ~1,796 m (the Association and the Junta). At the top runs the boundary between the Comunidad de Madrid and Castilla y León, and a plaque of 2005 remembers the Association's founding president, José Antonio Cimadevila.

The descent is long and easy. The guide mentions the ruins of an old post house and of the inn that Cervantes names in *Rinconete y Cortadillo*; below them you reach the Cruz de la Gallega, a viewpoint over the whole Castilian plain. There you can go straight on to Segovia or detour east by Valsaín to La Granja. The way into Segovia passes the Roman aqueduct, ~28 m high and built without mortar, the Junta's guide says.

Check the weather the day before. The Confraternity says to avoid the sierra in snow or bad weather: there are no farms, villages or facilities. The choices chapter has the alternative.
