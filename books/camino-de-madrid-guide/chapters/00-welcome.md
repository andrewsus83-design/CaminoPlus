# Welcome

The Camino de Madrid leaves the capital of Spain to the north, climbs the granite Sierra de Guadarrama by a Roman road, passes the Roman aqueduct of Segovia, crosses the pine woods and the cereal plains of Castile, and ends at Sahagún, where it meets the Camino Francés. The app's catalog gives it ~320 km. It is a young pilgrim road laid on very old tracks, and the Confraternity of St James, a charity in the United Kingdom, says very few pilgrims walk it.

**The promise of this book is simple.** By the last page you will know whether a quiet road from the capital is your Camino, how its thirteen walking days fall, where the long stretches without a village are, and what to check before you leave home.

## How to use it

If you are a month or more away, read the next four chapters in order: the Way in brief, whether it suits you, when to go, and what to do before you go. Then read the days, one chapter for each landscape (the sierra, the pine country, the plains), and the chapter on the choices this road offers.

If you are on the road tonight, go straight to your day. Each says what the walking is like and where to take care.

If you are only dreaming, start with "Is this Camino for you?" and finish with "The inner Way".

## What this book is not

It is not a live map, a timetable or a booking service. It names no hostels, cafes or shops, and it gives no prices, opening hours or bed counts. They change, and a wrong one costs a tired person dearly. For those, use the CaminoPlus app and ask locally.

This book was written with AI assistance, from public sources, and its author has not walked the road. So it gives you sources instead of memories, and it says plainly when the sources disagree.

On this road they disagree about the length, the pass, the number of stages and more. Two guides do most of the work here: the printed guide of the Madrid association that marked the route, which this book calls the Association's guide, and the pilgrim guide of the Junta de Castilla y León, the regional government, which it calls the Junta's guide. It names them each time it leans on one. Every distance carries a tilde, **~**, because it is approximate. Where something depends on the season (snow on the pass, heat, mist, a hostel that is shut), it says *check before you go*, and means it.

## A few words you will meet

*Buen Camino*, "good Way", is what pilgrims and locals say as you pass. An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel. The *credencial* is your pilgrim's passport, a folded card that you carry and have stamped along the way. Each stamp is a *sello*. A *puerto* is a mountain pass. A *cañada* is a historic drove road for livestock. The *Compostela* is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules, which the chapter on arriving sets out.

Carry what you need, drink before you are thirsty, and take your time over the long hill. *Buen Camino.*
