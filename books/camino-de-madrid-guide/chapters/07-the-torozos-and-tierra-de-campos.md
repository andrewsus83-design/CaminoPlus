# The Torozos and Tierra de Campos: Puente Duero to Sahagún

## The character of these days

Five days, ~122 km, from the Duero to Sahagún across two kinds of high plain. The Association's guide calls the first, the Montes Torozos, a plateau without horizons that gives a false sense of altitude, ~50 km of dry uplands broken by shallow valleys. It calls the second, the Tierra de Campos, an immense steppe with almost no trees except along the rivers, where cereal fields run out until they meet the sky, and where, near the end, the crest of the Cantabrian mountains shows to the north. The Confraternity of St James adds that the villages sit on spurs above steep-sided ravines.

There is little shade here. Carry water, start early in warm weather, and expect long views and long straight tracks.

### Day 9: Puente Duero to Peñaflor de Hornija (~27 km)

The Association counts ~26 km. You walk by the river to Simancas, ~6 km on, with its long medieval bridge over the Pisuerga and its castle, which holds the General Archive of Simancas. The Association's guide says the Romans knew it as Septimanca, and that in 939 a Castilian-Leonese army beat that of Abd al-Rahman III here.

Leaving Simancas, the guide says, you go under the motorway by a pedestrian underpass, and the track rises gently over cereal fields to Ciguñuela, whose church has two images of Saint James. Then Wamba, with its Romanesque church. The guide says the Visigothic king Recesvinto died here in 672 and was first buried here, and that his successor, Wamba, was chosen here. Peñaflor de Hornija is ~7 km beyond.

### Day 10: Peñaflor de Hornija to Medina de Rioseco (~23 km)

The Association counts ~24 km. Castromonte comes ~9 km on. Off the road, the Junta's guide says, lies the monastery of La Santa Espina, ~6 km from Castromonte, founded in 1147 and named for a relic, a thorn said to come from Christ's crown. It is a side trip, not part of the day.

Medina de Rioseco is a town of churches. The Junta's guide singles out its church of Santiago for its size and its wealth of images of Saint James. The Association adds that the town is where one branch of the Canal de Castilla ends, and that tradition says Saint Francis of Assisi reached it from Sahagún, on his pilgrimage to Compostela, between 1213 and 1215. From here the Tierra de Campos begins.

### Day 11: Medina de Rioseco to Cuenca de Campos (~27 km)

Here the guides differ most: the Association counts ~22 km, the Junta ~27 km, and the gap sits in the first half of the day. Take the longer figure for your planning. You pass Berrueces and Tamariz de Campos, then Moral de la Reina, ~19 km from Medina on the Junta's table (the Association says ~14 km) and ~8 km short of Cuenca de Campos, which makes a natural place to split the day. The Association's guide says Moral de la Reina has two small bridges of Roman origin.

One warning from the Association: near the boundary between Medina de Rioseco and Berrueces the path runs for a few hundred metres on the verge of the N-601, a national road. Face the traffic, keep to the edge, and cross only where you can see.

### Day 12: Cuenca de Campos to Santervás de Campos (~22 km)

Both guides agree on ~22 km. After ~5 km comes Villalón de Campos. Then Fontihoyuelo, where, the Association says, the wooden columns of the humble church's porch are carved with scallop shells and pilgrim staffs. Santervás de Campos follows, ~10 km on, with a Romanesque-Mudéjar church of the twelfth century on the river Valderaduey.

The Association notes that the road leaves the province of Valladolid and enters León just beyond. Tomorrow is the last day before the join. The land has risen a little, and it is still open.

### Day 13: Santervás de Campos to Sahagún (~23 km)

Melgar de Arriba and Arenillas de Valderaduey come first, then Grajal de Campos with its castle, begun in 1517 and one of the last in Spain built to withstand the artillery of its time, the Association says. Beyond Grajal the Association gives two ways: by San Pedro de las Dueñas, with its Gothic-Mudéjar monastery, or straight to Sahagún.

Just before the town stands the sanctuary of the Virgen Peregrina, a former Franciscan convent restored in 2011. The Association says the carved Virgin there, dressed as a pilgrim with a staff, dates from 1687 and has been patron of the Camino de Madrid since 2013.

Sahagún lies between the rivers Cea and Valderaduey. The Junta's guide calls it a major milestone on the Pilgrims' Route, the end of the seventh stage of the Codex Calixtinus. The Association's guide counts ~355 km from here to Santiago, and the Junta ~364 km. You leave by crossing the Cea on the Puente de Cantos, and from there you are on the Camino Francés.
