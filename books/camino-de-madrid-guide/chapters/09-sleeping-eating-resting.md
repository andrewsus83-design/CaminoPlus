# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Where you sleep

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: shared rooms and bunk beds. The Association's guide says hospitality on this road is offered by parishes, town councils, institutions and private people. Some work on a *donativo* basis, which means you give what you can. The Association's leaflets are firm that *donativo* does not mean the stay costs nothing: give at least what it has cost the hosts.

Plan for fewer beds than on the busy roads. The app's route notes say lodging is sparse and tell you to check municipal hostels. The Association's guide counts ~14 km as the average distance between places that host pilgrims, with the sierra gap of ~31 km. The Confraternity of St James says there is more accommodation than you might expect for so few towns, but that guesthouses and small hotels come at intervals of ~30 km, and that between Simancas and Sahagún only two places offer them on the road itself. It also says that donation-based and inexpensive pilgrim hostels are limited, and campsites very few.

This book names no hostel, and does not say which towns have one, because that changes. Three ways to find out:

- Look in the CaminoPlus app.
- Look at the Association's news page. It has published dated notes on the state of the road's hostels, the latest seen for this book being from 1 October 2026.
- Ask as you go. The Association's leaflets say to confirm in each hostel which ones are open for the next stretch, in winter and even in summer.

Advice on booking differs. The Junta's guide says the pilgrim hostels take no reservations, and that large groups should look for other places to sleep. The Association's leaflets say to book a bed if in doubt. Both can be true, since hostels differ. If a stage is long and the next bed is far, a call ahead is worth making.

## Hostel manners

The leaflets set out a few rules that apply on any Camino, and they matter more where beds are few.

- Respect the *hospitaleros*, the volunteers or staff who host you, and the other pilgrims.
- Lights go out in the late evening and rooms must be empty again early in the morning.
- Keep your pack on the floor, never on a bunk, since it can carry unwelcome insects in from the road.
- Carry a sleeping bag, which the Junta calls essential.

## What you eat

You will often be far from a shop. The Association's leaflets say there are shops in the main towns, and that you should carry little food. On this road that means: carry enough for the long days (the sierra, and the ~18 km before Alcazarén), and buy what you can in the last village before them. The Junta's guide says not to carry more than a day's food and to keep your water bottle full. The Association adds that heavy meals before you arrive are best avoided.

In the evening you will find what each place offers. You may be offered a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims, but ask. The Junta's guide says Segovia's hallmark dish is roast suckling pig. Beyond that, eat what the village eats.

## Rest days

The Junta's guide suggests an occasional rest day, or alternating longer days with shorter ones that end in places where you would like to stay longer. On this road the choice of place is easy.

**Segovia** is the natural one, after the sierra. Its aqueduct and old town are on the UNESCO World Heritage list, which the Association's guide dates to 1985, and the Junta's guide calls its cathedral the last Gothic cathedral built in Spain. The Confraternity says that even Segovia is a collection of small village centres, which may suit you after the pass. **Coca** and **Medina de Rioseco** are smaller and quieter, with castle and churches, if you would rather rest in a town of a few thousand people than in a city.

If you would rather rest in a big city, the detour to Valladolid is one of the choices described in the chapter before this one.
