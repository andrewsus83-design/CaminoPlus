# Body, weather and safety

## Feet and knees

Your feet carry the walk. The Xunta de Galicia says to take special care of them: apply foot cream, wear seamless cotton socks and footwear that has been broken in, keep your backpack light, carry a small first-aid kit, avoid large meals and take short, regular breaks. Treat a hot spot when you feel it, not when it is a blister, and stop for ten minutes to do it.

Knees suffer on the descents, and the Francés has several: the last ~6 km into Zubiri, the drop from the Alto del Perdón, the long way down from the Cruz de Ferro to Molinaseca, and the descent from the Alto do Poio to Triacastela. Shorten your stride, keep your knees soft and use poles if you like them. If you can, stop early on those days.

## Heat

On the Meseta the land is open and the sun is direct. The Xunta advises hats or caps against sunstroke, and sunscreen of high protection factor. Pamplona and Burgos have recorded ~42 °C and ~39 °C in July. In summer:

- Start at first light, and finish by early afternoon.
- Carry more water than you think you need, and drink before you are thirsty.
- Plan the long stretches. Day 17 passes ~17 to ~18 km with no village.
- If you feel dizzy, confused or sick, stop in the shade, drink, cool yourself and ask for help.

## Cold, rain and mountains

The weather changes with the height. The route over the Pyrenees is closed to walkers in winter, and the lower road by Valcarlos is the choice in bad weather. The Xunta notes that many stages are in mountains, at about a thousand metres above the sea. The Cruz de Ferro, at ~1,500 m, and O Cebreiro, at ~1,300 m, stand higher than the towns below them and are usually colder. In Galicia it rains in every season, and in autumn and winter heavily.

Carry a waterproof layer in every month, as the Xunta advises, and a cap for the cold in high places. If fog or storm closes in on a mountain stage, turn back to the last village, or wait, and do not push on to keep a schedule.

## Roads, vehicles and the dark

Much of the way is on paths, but not all of it. The Xunta warns that on the way from Arzúa the road is crossed several times, and the Camino follows stretches of the N-547 highway. The Confraternity of St James says that a reflective vest is a legal requirement in Spain when you walk on roads in darkness or poor visibility, and a good idea in hunting season. Where there is no path, walk facing the traffic, and step off the road when a lorry is coming.

If a dog comes towards you, stay calm, do not run, and walk on.

## When to stop

Stopping is part of the plan. Stop for pain that grows rather than fades, for a fever, for dizziness, or for anything that worries you, and see a doctor. A day or two lost to rest is cheaper than a month lost to injury. Tell someone your plan, and check that your insurance covers you in Spain.

In an emergency in Spain, as elsewhere in the European Union, call **112**. It is the European emergency number, and it connects you to the emergency services.
