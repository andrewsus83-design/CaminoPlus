# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and usually a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office lists "access to hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way" as the first practical use of the credencial, and says that hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

Hostels differ from region to region, and from year to year. In Galicia the Xunta runs a network of public hostels, and its rules give a sense of the kind of house you will find. The places are occupied as pilgrims arrive, and people on foot have priority. The maximum stay is one night, except in cases of illness or force majeure. Lights go out in the evening, and you must leave early in the morning. Elsewhere, hostels run by parishes, associations, towns and private hosts have their own rules, and private hostels, hotels and guesthouses can often be booked ahead. Which places exist, and which are open this month, is for the CaminoPlus app and for local advice, and this book does not list them.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and do not rustle bags at night or before dawn. Pack your rucksack in the hall.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed, and shoes where the house puts them.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. Somebody will snore, and if it is you, apologise at breakfast.
- Keep your valuables close.

**Bedbugs** are a risk in any shared bed anywhere. Check the seams of the mattress, keep your pack and clothes off the bed, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is not a reason to avoid hostels, only a reason to look before you lie down.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating. A good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

Many places along the Way offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what is on it, and what it costs, before you sit. A set meal is a good way to eat well on a long day and an easy way to try what the region cooks. If you do not eat meat, or have an allergy, learn the Spanish words for what you cannot eat and say them first.

The road passes through many food regions, and here are a few names to look for.

- **La Rioja** is wine country, as its Rioja designation says.
- **Burgos** is known for its blood sausage, *morcilla de Burgos*, and **Castilla y León** for milk-fed lamb, *lechazo*, usually roasted.
- **Galicia** has *polbo á feira*, octopus cooked Galician style, which Wikipedia lists as typical of Melide, and the cheese of Arzúa and A Ulloa, which has a protected designation of origin.

You do not need to eat all of it. Pick one local thing a day, and eat it without a screen.

Water matters as much. Fill your bottle in each town, and take food for the long stretches, above all on day 17, ~17 to ~18 km without a village.

## Resting

The Francés is long, and it is easy to treat it as a race with the calendar. Resist that. A rest day is part of the walk, and Wikipedia's own estimate of a typical walk allows for one or two.

The natural places are the cities: Pamplona after the Pyrenees, Burgos before the Meseta and León before the mountains. They are big enough to fill a day, and each has something worth the time. A day in Santiago at the end is the other one, and the chapter on arriving says why.

On the road, listen to your body before your plan. Take the short days as short days. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
