# The Way in brief

## The route

The French Way, the *Camino Francés*, starts in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port, whose name means "Saint John at the foot of the pass". It was the old capital of the Basque province of Lower Navarre, and Wikipedia calls it the second most popular start of the French Way. From there the road crosses the Pyrenees to Roncesvalles, then runs west through Pamplona, Logroño, Burgos and León to Santiago de Compostela.

The catalog gives the length as ~773 km. Published figures run from about 770 to about 780: Wikipedia's example itinerary starts at ~769 km from Santiago, German Wikipedia quotes the cathedral's pilgrim portal at ~774 km, and the 33 stage figures in this book add up to ~777 km. Which is right depends on where you measure from and which variant you walk, so treat ~775 km as a good guide.

Wikipedia says a typical walk takes at least four weeks, with one or two rest days. This book follows 33 usual stage towns, an average of ~24 km a day.

## Where the road comes from

Three routes across France (from Tours, Vézelay and Le Puy-en-Velay) meet at Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. A fourth, the Aragonese Way, crosses the Pyrenees at Somport and joins the French Way at Puente la Reina, ~690 km from Santiago. Later the Camino Primitivo joins at Melide and the Camino del Norte at Arzúa.

## A short history

The Xunta de Galicia, the regional government, gives this account on its official portal. Around the year 820 the tomb of St James the Elder was found in a wood called Libredón, and King Alfonso II of Asturias ordered a church built there. That church was the seed of the cathedral and the city. Asturian kings were among the first known pilgrims, and by the 11th to 13th centuries people were arriving from France, Italy, Germany, England and beyond, on foot, on horseback and by ship. A network of hospitals founded by kings, nobles and townspeople took them in, and in the small medieval ones it was customary to keep twelve beds, in memory of the twelve apostles.

The road got its name from the settlers. German Wikipedia says the name *Camino Francés* is first recorded in the 12th century, at Astorga, and refers to the French settlers whom the kings of northern Spain invited to build market towns along it.

Book V of the *Codex Calixtinus* is a medieval guide to this road, covering its stages, sanctuaries, hospitality, fountains and food. It is often attributed to the French cleric Aymeric Picaud. The Xunta dates the codex to 1135; Wikipedia says about 1138 to 1145, and calls the attribution to Picaud likely rather than certain.

The pilgrimage never stopped, but it thinned. The Reformation, the wars of religion and the French Revolution cut the numbers, and in 1589, fearing an English attack, the archbishop of Santiago had the apostle's remains hidden; they were found again in 1879. The recovery of the road began in the 20th century. The Xunta names the first modern associations of friends of the Camino, founded in Paris in 1950 and in Estella in 1963, and the visit of Pope John Paul II in 1982. UNESCO inscribed the Camino Francés on the World Heritage List in 1993.

## The Way today

In 2024 the Pilgrim's Office issued 499,239 Compostelas, and 47 per cent of them went to people who walked the French Way, according to Wikipedia: about 235,000 people. Of those, about 14 per cent started in Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. About 65 per cent started in Sarria, the town ~115 km from Santiago, which the chapter on where to start explains.

## What it feels like

The road changes character five times, and this book is built on that.

- **The Pyrenees and Navarra.** A steep start, then green hills, Pamplona and the rolling country beyond.
- **La Rioja.** Vineyards and small towns.
- **Burgos and the Meseta.** A great cathedral, then the high plateau, at ~800 to ~900 m, for days.
- **León and the mountains.** A great city, then the mountains: the road passes ~1,500 m at the Cruz de Ferro, its highest point.
- **Galicia.** O Cebreiro, at ~1,300 m, is the gateway. From there to Santiago the Xunta measures ~155 km through a greener, wetter country.

People walk this road for many reasons. It is long enough to ask something of you, and the villages along it have been receiving pilgrims for more than a thousand years.
