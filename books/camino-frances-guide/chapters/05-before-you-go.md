# Before you go

## The credencial

The *credencial* is your pilgrim's passport, and you need it for two things: to sleep in pilgrims' hostels, and to prove your walk when you ask for the Compostela. The Pilgrim's Office describes the official model as a card of sixteen concertina-folded pages. The first page holds your details, and the rest hold boxes for stamps. Each stamp is a *sello*.

You can get one from the Pilgrim's Reception Office in Santiago, from confraternities and associations of friends of the Way that the Cathedral has authorised, from parish churches and from pilgrim hostels. The Office keeps a list for your country. Get yours before you leave if you can.

Stamps are normally collected where you sleep, and also in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. The Office also describes a digital credential for phones. For the Compostela it speaks of the stamped credencial, so carry the paper one, and ask the Office if you want to rely on anything else.

## Getting there, and back

Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port is in France. A regional train, the TER, runs from Bayonne, along a line ~52 km long, according to Wikipedia. Timetables and connections change, so check them before you book.

Santiago has its own airport, Santiago-Rosalía de Castro, ~12 km from the city, with flights to Spanish and other European cities. Leave a spare day or two at the end: the walk finishes where you can rest, and the Compostela queue can be long.

## Training

The Confraternity of St James suggests that, if you are not an experienced walker, you walk regularly, add longer distances and hills as you improve, and aim to be able to cover 20 km or more a day on varied ground. It also advises:

- Break in new boots or shoes and socks before you go.
- Practise with your loaded rucksack, because a pack changes the way your feet strike the ground, and with poles, if you will use them.
- Train for the downhill too. This matters on the Francés: the descent from the Cruz de Ferro, ~850 m in ~19 km, is one of the biggest of the walk.
- Take rest days in your training, and stretch before and after.

The Xunta de Galicia says to start training some months ahead, with walks that grow gradually longer, up to training walks of 23 to 30 km. If you have a medical condition or any worry, speak to a doctor or physiotherapist first.

## Packing

Carry less than you think. The Confraternity notes that some people advise no more than 10 to 15 per cent of your body weight, counting the day's food and water: a litre of water weighs a kilogram. It suggests a base weight, without food and water but with the rucksack, of ~7 to ~10 kg, and a rucksack of 35 to 60 litres. Weigh your pack at home.

The Xunta's list of basics includes boots and light shoes for the evening, seamless socks, two T-shirts and two jerseys, a sleeping bag, a canteen, high-factor sunscreen, a hat or cap, a small first-aid kit, a phone and charger, and a waterproof garment in every season. Earplugs and a torch are among its optional extras. Add little to that list: a lighter pack is the better advice.

Three notes. The Confraternity says that a reflective vest is a legal requirement in Spain when you walk on roads in darkness or poor visibility, and a good idea in hunting season. Poles and a stick are a matter of taste, and most airlines will not let you carry a stick in the cabin. And the weather you meet may be anything from cold rain to ~40 °C, so pack for the month you will walk.

## Shoes and feet

The Xunta advises a hard sole and a heel that is not too soft, and comfortable shoes broken in beforehand. The Confraternity suggests boots in winter, late autumn, early spring, on very rough ground or if you have ankle problems, notes that some people dislike Gore-Tex boots in hot dusty weather, and advises buying from a specialist shop that will let you try them at home. Do not buy boots that do not quite fit just because the price is low. Foot cream, seamless cotton socks and footwear that has been broken in are the Xunta's advice for keeping your feet healthy.

## Money, phone and language

The Xunta advises carrying your documents and cards and not much cash. Spain uses the euro. Keep a little cash for small places that may not take cards, and ask in the app where you are.

Charge your phone every evening. CaminoPlus is built to work without signal, so check in the app that the maps you need are on your phone before you leave home.

Spanish is the language of the road, and Galician is also an official language in Galicia. Place names appear in more than one form: Roncesvalles is also Orreaga in Basque, Pamplona is also Iruña, and Palas de Rei is also written Palas de Rey. A few Spanish words, *por favor*, *gracias* and *buenos días*, go a long way.

## Health and booking

See a doctor if you have any condition that the walk could affect, and check that your insurance covers you in Spain. In May to September, and in a Holy Year, book beds ahead where you can. The Confraternity says so for Holy Years in particular.
