# The Way in brief

## Where it starts and ends

The road starts at the Sé, the cathedral of Lisbon. Wikipedia says it was begun after the Christian conquest of the city in 1147, on the site of the main mosque, in a late Romanesque style. The yellow arrows begin there. From the Sé the way follows the Tagus upstream through Vila Franca de Xira and Azambuja to Santarém, Golegã and Tomar. The regional tourism board of Central Portugal takes over at Tomar and names Alvaiázere, Ansião and Coimbra, and after Coimbra Mealhada, Águeda, Albergaria-a-Velha, São João da Madeira and Grijó, before the road enters Porto.

The Xunta de Galicia, the regional government of Galicia, lists the places of the Portuguese Way in Portugal as Lisbon, Santarém, Coimbra, Porto, Barcelos, Ponte de Lima and Valença do Minho, where the Minho is crossed into Galicia. From Porto the road is the one *The Portuguese Way* describes: Barcelos, Ponte de Lima, Tui, Pontevedra, Padrón and the cathedral of Santiago.

## How long

The CaminoPlus catalog gives the whole road as ~620 km. Sources differ:

- The Confraternity of St James says ~640 km from Lisbon to Santiago via Porto, and ~252 km from Porto.
- Wikipedia gives ~620 km in its box and ~610 km in its text.
- One published stage table counts ~617 km in 27 stages. The sum of its 16 stages from Lisbon to Porto is ~374 km, and of its 11 from Porto ~242 km.
- An older Confraternity sheet for the Lisbon to Porto stretch says ~380 km.

This book follows that table's stage towns and numbers the days from 1 to 27. From Tomar to Porto they agree with the regional board's account, apart from Cernache, which the board calls a short detour. Other sources count 24 to 28 days for the whole road. None is the right one: stage towns depend on where people can sleep, and that changes. The days:

- Day 1: Lisbon to Alpriate, ~22 km
- Day 2: Alpriate to Vila Franca de Xira, ~19 km
- Day 3: Vila Franca de Xira to Azambuja, ~20 km
- Day 4: Azambuja to Santarém, ~33 km
- Day 5: Santarém to Golegã, ~31 km
- Day 6: Golegã to Tomar, ~31 km
- Day 7: Tomar to Alvaiázere, ~32 km
- Day 8: Alvaiázere to Ansião, ~23 km
- Day 9: Ansião to Cernache, ~26 km
- Day 10: Cernache to Coimbra, ~11 km
- Day 11: Coimbra to Mealhada, ~25 km
- Day 12: Mealhada to Águeda, ~24 km
- Day 13: Águeda to Albergaria-a-Velha, ~16 km
- Day 14: Albergaria-a-Velha to São João da Madeira, ~29 km
- Day 15: São João da Madeira to Grijó, ~19 km
- Day 16: Grijó to Porto, ~15 km
- Days 17 to 27: Porto to Santiago, eleven days, as *The Portuguese Way* has them

## What it feels like

Four landscapes follow one another. First the Tagus and its floodplain, the Ribatejo: flat, farmed, and long, with four stages in a row of ~30 km or more, much of it open to the sun. Then the limestone hills after Tomar, with olive groves, oak woods and Roman roads. Then the vineyards of the Bairrada and the small industrial towns of the district of Aveiro. Last, Porto, and the green north.

The CaminoPlus app's own note calls the road mostly flat, with some road sections. The Confraternity says the Lisbon to Porto stretch has plenty of road walking at first, becomes more beautiful after Santarém, and that the days between Tomar and Coimbra are delightful. It adds that the road is now well waymarked, but has less support for pilgrims than the road from Porto on.

## Why it exists

The Xunta says the Portuguese road grew in importance from the twelfth century, after Portugal became independent, and that the country's north to south road network was organised around the pilgrimage, through Lisbon, Santarém, Coimbra and Porto. It names two Portuguese pilgrims: Elizabeth of Portugal, the "Holy Queen", who made the pilgrimage in the fourteenth century and was buried at Coimbra with a pilgrim's staff, and King Manuel I, who made the pilgrimage from Lisbon to Santiago in 1502. The regional board adds that Queen Teresa is said to have founded a hostel for pilgrims at Albergaria-a-Velha in 1117.

Wikipedia describes an older, western road from Lisbon, by the thermal hospital of Caldas da Rainha and the monastery of Alcobaça, and says that pilgrims used Roman roads to reach Coimbra and had to reach Porto before the city gates closed at night. The road you walk today is the one the stage tables and the regional board describe: the Tagus road.

## Company on the road

Wikipedia, reporting the Pilgrim's Office statistics, says that in 2025 the Portuguese Way was the second most walked route to Santiago, with 19 per cent of pilgrims, and that most who received the Compostela started in Tui (41 per cent), Porto (26 per cent) or Valença (18 per cent). Together those three starts make 85 per cent, so every other start, Lisbon included, shares the remaining 15 per cent. The Confraternity says that most pilgrims start in Porto but many walk from Lisbon.

Read together, these suggest a quiet road before Porto. That is a reading of the numbers, not a promise.
