# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life, and its absence

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office says hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

Between Lisbon and Porto the network is thinner than on the road from Porto. The Confraternity says the road has less support for pilgrims, but that new albergues open every year and it is much easier now than it was five years before. Via Lusitana publishes a list of hostels on its site, and warns that the information may sometimes be out of date. Some places on it ask for a donation, *donativo*, and not a fixed sum. The sources also mention guesthouses, rural houses, parish houses and youth hostels, and the older sheet records that in 2013 some volunteer fire stations gave pilgrims a room for a donation, a courtesy that was becoming rarer. Via Lusitana suggests that when you stay in a hotel or guesthouse you say you are a pilgrim and show your credencial, since some give a discount.

Which places exist, which are open this month, and whether you can book differ from town to town and change from season to season. This book does not list them. Use the CaminoPlus app for where to sleep tonight, ask locally for tomorrow, and do not rely on one source before a long day such as Day 4.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and do not rustle bags at night or before dawn. Pack your rucksack in the hall.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. Somebody will snore, and if it is you, apologise at breakfast.

**Bedbugs** are a risk in any shared bed. Check the seams of the mattress, keep your pack off the bed, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is not a reason to avoid hostels, only a reason to look before you lie down.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating. A good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

The regional board's guide points to what each stretch is known for. At Tomar it recommends the convent sweets, the *doçaria conventual*. At Coimbra it names the Santa Clara pastries, a convent sweet too. At Mealhada, the sparkling wines and the roast suckling pig of the Bairrada. In Águeda, the local pastry. At Rabaçal, a local cheese with a protected name.

You do not need to eat all of it. Pick one local thing a day, and eat it without a screen.

Water matters more than food. Fill your bottle in each town. On Day 4 the table speaks of a long stretch with no shade and no services, and the older sheet counts about nine kilometres without any facilities near the end, so carry lunch and water from Azambuja. On other days ask the evening before where you can buy your lunch.

## Resting

It is easy to treat the walk as a timetable to be kept. Resist that. A day of rest is part of the walk.

On this road the three natural places for it are Tomar, after the three longest days; Coimbra, a city that rewards more than a morning; and Porto, at the end of the Lisbon road and the start of the Porto one. The other place is the end. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it deserves more than a night.

On the road, listen to your body before you listen to your plan. Take the short days as short days. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
