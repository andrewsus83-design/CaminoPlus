# Body, weather and safety

## Feet

On a road that is mostly asphalt, your feet take the weight. Look after them at lunch, not in the evening: shoes off, socks changed, a hot spot treated before it becomes a blister. Hard surfaces tire shins and knees as well as soles, so shorten a day before you push through a pain that is growing.

## Heat

This is the main danger of the first week, and it can build quickly. The Tagus plain is hot and dry in summer, and part of Day 4 is open farmland with no shade and no services. The regional tourism board's advice is plain: make the way by day, preferably in the morning, avoid the hottest hours, and shelter from shortly before sunset until daybreak. It also advises drinking regularly and stopping for ten minutes every two hours, to rest and eat a little.

- Start at first light in summer, and plan to finish before the afternoon.
- Carry more water than you think you need on the long days, and fill up at every town.
- Wear a hat and sun protection. Carry something to eat.
- If you feel faint, sick or confused, stop in the shade, drink, and ask for help. Call 112 if it does not pass.
- If the forecast is for a heatwave, do not walk a ~33 km day. Shorten it, or rest.

## Roads, tracks and railways

Much of the way runs beside national roads and through industrial estates. The Confraternity advises, when you must walk on a road, to stay aware of the traffic and use the strip between the road and the drainage ditch. Where there is no path, walk on the shoulder, facing the traffic, and cross where you can see and be seen. The older sheet says the shoulders on the national roads are usually wide, and also reports heavy lorry traffic at some times, so stay alert at junctions and roundabouts.

A published stage description mentions railway lines that the way runs beside or crosses, some of the crossings without barriers. Stop, look both ways, and listen.

On the flood levee of the Tagus the path can be overgrown at some times of the year, and the older sheet says a road runs below it. Use the road when the levee is overgrown or wet.

## Rain and wet

The north is wet, as the chapter on when to go says. A proper rain jacket and a plan for wet feet matter more in the last third of the road than in the first. Wet stone paving, in old towns and on old roads, is slippery. Go slowly on descents.

## Fire and closures

In hot, dry weather, wildfire can close paths and roads. Follow any closure, diversion or instruction of the local authorities, and do not argue with a sign. Before you set out each morning, ask your host about the day's route, and about any diversion.

## When to stop

Stopping is a skill. You are not walking to prove anything. If something hurts in a way that grows and does not fade, rest it for a day, and let a doctor look at it. If you are alone on a quiet stretch, tell someone where you plan to sleep. The European emergency number is 112, in Portugal as in Spain.
