# Before you go

## The credencial

The *credencial* is your pilgrim's passport, and you need one before the first stamp. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says the official model is a cardboard card of sixteen concertina-folded pages, with boxes for the stamps, and that it has two practical purposes: access to the hostels of the Way, and proof when you ask for the Compostela. It also describes a digital credential, a phone app. Carry the paper card in any case.

In Portugal, Via Lusitana, the pilgrims' association that works on the waymarking and upkeep of the Portuguese ways, says on its site that two official models of the credencial are available: the cathedral's own and one that the association issues with the cathedral's authorisation. You can ask the association for it through its website. The regional tourism board's guide of 2023 adds that credenciais were also to be had at the Sé of Lisbon and at the two cathedrals of Coimbra. Ask before you rely on that.

Stamps, *carimbos* in Portuguese, are normally collected where you sleep, and also in parish churches, monasteries, town halls and other places, the Office says; Via Lusitana adds tourist offices. The older Confraternity sheet adds that south of Porto, where the road was little known in 2013, you may have to be inventive: post offices, parish council offices, town halls and railway stations gave stamps.

## Getting there

Lisbon airport lies ~7 km north-east of the historic centre, Wikipedia says, and has its own metro station. The Confraternity notes that you can also fly into Porto, Vigo or Santiago, and that a regular bus runs between Santiago and Porto in about four hours, so flying home from either end of the road is possible. Timetables change: check the current ones when you plan your return.

## Choosing your start

Where you begin sets the length of your walk. On the table this book follows, the road from each start to Santiago is:

- **From Lisbon:** ~617 km in 27 stages.
- **From Santarém:** ~523 km in 23 stages.
- **From Tomar:** ~461 km in 21 stages.
- **From Coimbra:** ~369 km in 17 stages.
- **From Porto:** ~242 km in 11 stages.

The Confraternity says the way becomes more beautiful after Santarém, and that the days between Tomar and Coimbra are delightful. That is a reason to start at Santarém or Tomar if you cannot spare four weeks. The app's own note suggests adding about two weeks to a start in Porto. For the whole road, 27 walking days and a rest day each at Tomar, Coimbra and Porto make about 30 days, and you need a day each to travel there and back. If you are choosing a start with the Compostela in mind, read the rule in the chapter on other ways to split the days first, and ask the Pilgrim's Office.

## Training

The regional board advises starting five months before, and building up the distance on varied ground with some hills. For this road add one thing: the length of your longest day. Four stages in a row of ~30 km or more ask a lot of a body that has not walked far in months, and the surface is hard. A few weeks of regular walking will do more than any programme, so train in the shoes and with the pack you will carry.

## Shoes and feet

Much of the road is asphalt, and tracks beside the Tagus. Look for a cushioned sole and a little room in the toe box, because feet swell as the day goes on. Break your shoes in before you leave. Two pairs of socks, changed at lunch, are a cheap defence against blisters. Treat a hot spot when you feel it, not when it has become a blister. If something hurts in a way that grows and does not fade, rest it, and see a doctor.

## Packing

Pack light, and then lighter: the board advises a pack of no more than ten kilograms. The essentials are plain: sun protection, a hat and a water bottle, because the first week has little shade; a rain jacket, because the road ends in the green north; a small first-aid kit for feet; a head torch and earplugs for shared rooms; and, as the board's list says, a sleeping bag and slippers. A phone with an offline map is a good tool and a poor single plan. Know your next town before the battery is low. Leave whatever you are unsure of at home.

## Money, language and the clock

Portugal and Spain both use the euro. Carry some coins and small notes for small purchases. Prices are not in this book; the people you meet will tell you what things cost.

Portuguese is spoken until the border. In Galicia there are two official languages, Galician and Spanish. Pilgrims need little: *bom dia* ("good morning"), *obrigado* (a man says thank you) or *obrigada* (a woman does), and in Galicia *grazas* or *gracias*. Say them with a smile and you will be understood. Remember the clock: Galicia is an hour ahead of Portugal.

## Emergencies

The European emergency number is 112, in Portugal as in Spain. Keep it in your head, not just in your phone.
