# Day 10. Be not troubled

*What if the searching is already part of the finding?*

## The passage

> Console thyself, thou wouldest not seek me hadst thou not found me. … Thou wouldest not seek me unless thou didst possess me. Therefore be not troubled. Be comforted; it is not from yourself that you must expect it; but on the contrary, expecting nothing from yourself, you must await it.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Happiness of Man with God, "The Mystery of Jesus" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

Whatever day of the road this is, it is the last day of this book, and a last day has a mood of its own. Some walkers expect to feel finished. Others feel the restlessness of the first morning again, only more tired. The mind that came with you has been on the road all the time. It was never left at home, and it is, in its way, the main thing you have been walking with.

The three short pieces above come from a meditation Pascal called "The Mystery of Jesus", most of which dwells on the night in the garden before the crucifixion. Parts of it he writes as if Christ were speaking to the soul of the reader. The opening lines are in that voice: the "thou" is you, and the "me" is Christ. We say this plainly so that nobody mistakes the speaker. It is a voice taken from the Christian story, and Pascal means it to be heard as a word to someone who is looking.

What it says is quiet. Console yourself: you would not be seeking this unless you had in some way already been found. It is the reverse of the wager on Day 8. There he told a reader to stake everything. Here a voice tells him that his search is not a sign of an absence. The restlessness, which was the whole subject of this book, is turned round and read as a kind of evidence. The thirst is not nothing. Then the last sentence: "it is not from yourself that you must expect it", and so the thing to do is to await it.

This closes a circle with the first day. There we asked whether you could sit in a quiet room, and Pascal said that most of us run from it. He did not think the answer was simply to stay in. In the same section as the thinking reed he sets two bits of advice side by side. The Stoics, he says, tell us "Retire within yourselves, there will you find your rest", and he says that is not true. Others say "Go out of yourselves, seek your happiness in diversion", and that is not true either, because sickness may come. His own answer: "Happiness is neither without us nor within us; it is in God, both without us and within us." Peace is not in the room, and it is not outside it. As Pascal means it, it comes from beyond both.

He claimed to know something of this. One night in November 1654 he wrote a short record of two hours, which, his family believed, he moved from one garment to the next and wore until he died. In it, after the word *Fire*, comes the line "Security, security. Feeling, joy, peace." Further down: "Joy, joy, joy, tears of joy." He does not say how it came, and he does not offer it as a method. It is not a recipe for a Tuesday afternoon.

There are things to say about how hard this is.

These lines cannot prove anything, and they do not try. They assume what they claim: that you have been found. For a reader who feels nothing found, they are not a comfort. They are a statement that cannot be checked. They are best read as the consolation they are, a word for someone who is already looking, and not as an argument for someone who is not.

"Expecting nothing from yourself, you must await it" can sound like an instruction to sit still. It does not sit well with the rest of the book, in which Pascal says "Labour". The two fit together only if the labour is real and the result is not a wage. Do the work. Do not think you have earned the answer.

The meditation around these lines is a great deal sterner than these three, and we have left those parts out. They are all there for anyone who reads the whole.

And the peace Pascal wrote of did not make his life easy. The preface to our edition describes his last years as years of pain and self-denial. Peace and pain were in the same house.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Something very small. To stop treating the searching as the problem. If you are still looking, for a faith, for a reason, for the next stretch of your life, then it is all right to be a person who is still looking. You can take that to a bench and sit with it. You do not have to arrive anywhere first.

## For the walk

- What am I looking for that I may already have been given?
- Stand still for one minute, on the road or on a bench, with nothing in your hands. Say, aloud or silently, in your own words: "It is all right to still be looking." Then go on. Let the minute be the whole practice, and do not try to make anything come of it.

## At the end of the day

Tonight is the end of the book, and it may or may not be the end of your road. The book cannot tell. If you are still walking tomorrow, take what follows as an interval. If you have finished, it is the last evening of something, and last evenings can be odd.

Look back along the ten days, not to summarise them but to see which stayed. The empty room. The reed in the wind. The sky that did not answer. The body that learned a routine. The mind that was always one step ahead. The heart and its reasons. The angel, the brute and the feet. The bet. The half-light. And this. One of them is probably clearer than the rest, and it is rarely the one you would have guessed in advance. That is the one to take home.

Pascal's own book was never finished. The title page of our edition carries three Latin words, *Pendent opera interrupta*, which mean something like "the works hang unfinished". It is an odd thing to say on a title page, and an honest one. A book that stopped mid-sentence is a fair companion for a road that does not end, as roads do not, at the edge of a map. You do not have to have finished anything.

If the road has ended for you today, the tiredness is a different kind. It is the tiredness of a thing being over. You might feel relief, or a flatness, or a small grief, or a good deal of nothing, and any of them is allowed. The feeling does not have to match the photographs. It will change over the next few days, as the body catches up with the fact that it can stop.

And think about the searching. Is there something you have been seeking, an answer, a faith, a way of being at home in yourself, that you have been counting as a failure because it has not turned up? Pascal's line suggests that the seeking may be part of the finding. You cannot know that tonight. But it might be allowed to stand, without a verdict.

- What am I still looking for, and can I let the looking be enough for tonight?
- Which of the ten days would I like to keep with me, and why that one?
- Whom would I like to tell something about this road?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road, the quiet, and the company I did not always notice. I am still looking, and I am less afraid of that than I was. Whatever I have found, let it be enough for tonight. Whatever I have not, let me not be troubled. Let peace be a visitor I do not have to earn.*
