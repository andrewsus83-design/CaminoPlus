# Day 9. Enough light

*Can you walk by half-light?*

## The passage

> It had not then been just that he should appear in a manner plainly divine, and wholly capable of convincing all men, but neither had it been just that he should come in so hidden a manner as not to be recognised of those who sincerely sought him. He has willed to reveal himself wholly to these, and thus willing to appear openly to those who seek him with their whole heart, and to hide himself from those who fly him with all their heart, he has so tempered the knowledge of himself as to give signs of himself visible to those who seek him, and obscure to those who seek him not. There is enough light for those who wish earnestly to see, and enough obscurity for those of a contrary mind. … I may well love total darkness, but if God keep me in a state of semi-obscurity, this partial darkness is unpleasant to me, and because I do not see in it the advantages of total darkness it pleases me not.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Happiness of Man with God, "Proofs of the Christian Religion" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

There is an hour before sunrise when you can see your feet and not the horizon. The road is a pale line a few strides long, and the rest of the world is grey. Nobody waits for full daylight. You walk by what you can see, and the next stretch shows itself as you reach it. The same is true of mist, which turns a landscape into a room, with a wall that moves back as you move forward.

Pascal wrote about a larger half-light. The "he" of this passage is God, and Pascal is explaining a puzzle that troubled him as it troubles many people: why is the evidence for God not plain? His answer is that it is meant to be neither. It would not have been just for God to appear "in a manner plainly divine, and wholly capable of convincing all men", and it would not have been just either to come "in so hidden a manner as not to be recognised of those who sincerely sought him." So the signs are tempered: visible to those who look, obscure to those who do not. Hence the sentence at the heart of the passage: "There is enough light for those who wish earnestly to see, and enough obscurity for those of a contrary mind."

Read apart from his theology, it is an observation a reader may well recognise from other parts of life. The matters that count most, whether you can trust a person, what you want to do with your life, whether anything holds, are not settled by a proof that nobody can refuse. They show themselves in the measure that you look. There is usually enough to go on, and there is usually enough doubt for anyone who wants it.

The second stretch is the part that makes Pascal likeable. He admits that he does not enjoy it. He says he might even be content with total darkness, and that the in-between, the "partial darkness", is "unpleasant to me". In another note he puts the same feeling more sharply: "seeing too much to deny, and too little to affirm, my state is pitiful". It is a good sentence to carry for a day when you are neither sure nor sure of the opposite.

Here is where it is hard.

"Enough obscurity for those of a contrary mind." Taken by itself, that sentence puts the blame for doubt on the doubter, as though the dark were only for those who wanted it. Many honest people have been in the dark without wanting it, and some have wanted the light more than anything else. Pascal knows that. In the opening of the book he writes, "I can have nothing but compassion for all who sincerely lament their doubt." And as we saw on Day 7, the only people he approves are those who "seek with tears". The earnest, in his sense, includes the ones who ache at not seeing. The sentence is best read beside those.

The picture itself is also hard: a God who arranges partial darkness so that some will stumble. It is Pascal's claim, offered in a Christian book, and it is difficult even for many Christians. You are not required to take it as yours.

And the passage cannot tell you how to see better. It says only that looking is rewarded. That may be unsatisfying, and it may also be the only honest thing to say.

On an ordinary day it comes to this: walk the stretch you can see, without demanding the whole map. You do this constantly. You set out expecting the day to be the same sort of day as yesterday, and you trust people you have never met to have told you the truth about a path. Most of living is done by half-light, and it works well enough. The practice is only to notice that, and to let the half-light be what it is without treating it as a failure.

A safety word. This is for the mind and not for the route. If you do not know which way to go at a junction, stop and check a map, or ask someone. That is the right use of the light you have.

## For the walk

- What do I need to see that I cannot see yet, and what can I see well enough to take the next step?
- At the next stretch where you cannot see the end of the path, a bend, the brow of a hill, a track going into trees, walk to the point where it opens, and notice that you could. Take the step without waiting to be certain of the one after it. If you are truly unsure of the way, stop and check. This is a practice for the mind, and not a licence to ignore a junction.

## At the end of the day

The evening has its own half-light. There is a quarter of an hour at dusk when you can still read the sky but the colours have gone. If you were out in it today, you had Pascal's lighting for free.

Think about what you had to go on without. The weather tomorrow. Whether the bed would be there. How the knee would hold. A stranger's directions, taken on trust. You acted on enough light, and the day went forward. It is worth saying that to yourself, because on a hard day it is easy to believe that nobody can walk without certainty, and the day is evidence that you can. A walker who waited for certainty about the weather, the bed and the knee would never leave the doorstep.

Then ask where you wanted more certainty than you needed. It is a strange habit. We check the forecast for a fifth time. We ask the same question of a person who has already answered it. We read the same page of the map after the route has become plain. Some of it is prudence, and a good deal of it is a nervous wish for the dark to be switched off. It is useful to know which was which.

There are two ways to end the half-light early, and both are tempting. One is a settled certainty, the other a settled cynicism. Each is a way of switching off the uncomfortable in-between, and each lets you stop looking. Pascal says plainly that he might love the total dark. Notice, without scolding yourself, where you reached for a simple yes or a simple no today, simply because the middle was tiring.

The hour is against you here. Doubt is heavier at night. The half-light seems darker than it is, and the largest questions come at the hour you are least equipped to carry them. If the dark feels heavy tonight, it may be partly the hour. The same question at six in the morning is often a smaller thing. Do not take a night's mood as a verdict on anything.

Last, think about who has been a light for you. A small one is enough: someone who pointed the way without being asked, a sign at the right bend, a message that arrived when you needed it. Light is often something that someone else has done. The same goes for how you saw the people you met. You saw a fraction of each of them, a face at a counter, a back on the path ahead. Everyone is in half-light to everyone else, and a little charity is the right response to knowing it. If you have not yet said thank you, there is still tomorrow.

- What did I have to go on without today, and how did the step turn out?
- Where did I demand more certainty than I needed?
- Who or what was a small light for me today, and have I said thank you?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for enough light to take today's steps. Forgive me for wanting the whole map. Be patient with those who cannot see, and with those who say they can. Let me rest in the half-dark, and trust it for one night. Let me wake to the morning.*
