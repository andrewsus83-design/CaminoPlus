# How to use this book

This is a small book for ten days. It is made to go with the Camino Sanabrés, and it works just as well at home, one day at a time.

Every day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, quoted exactly as the translator, C. Kegan Paul, gave them in 1885. Pascal wrote in French in the seventeenth century, and the English keeps some of its age: *to-morrow*, *thou*, *whoso*. Read the passage twice, the second time aloud if you can.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Pascal's. It starts from something you might meet on a road, an empty hour, a bench, a mist, and lets the passage open it. It says plainly what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. You will not need a notebook.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back over the day you have just lived, with the morning's passage in mind. It asks two or three gentle questions and is honest about tiredness. The questions are for lying down with.
- **A short prayer.** Original and brief. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular.

## Why this book for this road

The plan for this Library describes the Camino Sanabrés as a quiet inland road, and Pascal's *Thoughts* as the book of a restless mind meeting silence. A quiet road leaves a busy head alone with itself, and Pascal's notes keep coming back to exactly that meeting.

We have not walked the road, and we say nothing about it beyond what the app's own entry says: it begins at Granja de Moreruela and ends in Santiago de Compostela, and the app gives it no distance and no number of stages yet. Ten days is the length of this book, not a claim about the road. If your walk is longer, repeat a day or read on a rest day. If it is shorter, take the days that fit. The pairing is a recommendation, and Pascal's questions are the same on any road, or none.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

The *Thoughts* are Christian. They are notes toward a defence of the Christian religion, written by a believer for readers who doubted, and in one meditation they speak in the voice of Christ himself. People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Pascal believed to recognise what he saw about restlessness, habit, doubt and silence. Where he is hard to follow, we say so, and we have left out the pages that would need a long explanation.

## The shape of the ten days

The order is ours, not Pascal's. He left his notes unfinished, and editors have arranged them in different orders. These days follow a walk.

- **Days 1 to 3, the quiet.** A room with nothing in it, a reed that thinks, a sky that does not answer.
- **Days 4 to 6, what carries us.** Habit, the present moment, the reasons of the heart.
- **Days 7 to 10, what we are and what we risk.** Neither angel nor brute, the wager, the half-light, and a word of comfort.

Day 8 is the wager, the argument of Pascal's that people know best and that is easily misread. We read it slowly and say where it is hard. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. You are very likely reading this on that phone, which is not the quietest room in the world, so the putting away is part of the exercise. Read the evening part last, when the boots are off.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a shared kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well.

Read slowly. Pascal's notes were written in pieces, on whatever paper was to hand, and they can be read in pieces too.
