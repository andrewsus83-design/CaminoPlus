# Day 6. The heart's reasons

*What do you know that you cannot prove?*

## The passage

> The heart has its reasons, which reason knows not, as we feel in a thousand instances. I say that the heart loves the universal Being naturally, and itself naturally, according as it gives itself to each, and it hardens itself against one or the other at its own will. You have rejected one and kept the other, does reason cause your love? It is the heart which is conscious of God, not the reason. This then is faith; God sensible to the heart, not to the reason. Reason acts slowly and with so many views, on so many principles, which it ought always to keep before it, that it constantly slumbers and goes astray, from not having its principles at hand. The heart does not act thus, it acts in a moment, and is always ready to act. We must then place our faith in the heart, or it will be always vacillating. Men often mistake their imagination for their heart, and they believe they are converted as soon as they think of being converted.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Happiness of Man with God, "Various Thoughts" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

Sooner or later someone asks why you are walking. It is a plain question, kindly meant, put over a table or at a fork in the path. And a pilgrim's honest answer is seldom a list of reasons. What comes out sounds a little thin, or a little too large, or you hear yourself giving the reason that goes down best with the person asking.

Pascal would not be surprised. "The heart has its reasons, which reason knows not." The sentence is well known, and it is often taken to mean that feeling beats thinking. That is not quite what it says. For Pascal the heart is not the sentimental part. It is the part of us that takes things in at a glance, without steps: first principles, the sense that you are awake and not dreaming, the love of a person. Reason works slowly, with many views, and "constantly slumbers and goes astray". The heart "acts in a moment".

He is, remember, a mathematician. In another place he points out that nobody can prove by argument that they are awake: "We know that we do not dream, although it is impossible to prove it by reason". Reason itself rests on things it cannot prove, and he says it must "lean necessarily on this instinctive knowledge of the heart." He does not want either side to bully the other. It is "as useless and absurd for reason to demand from the heart proofs of first principles before it will admit them, as it would be for the heart to ask from reason a feeling of all the propositions demonstrated before accepting them."

The question in the middle of the passage is the one to carry. "You have rejected one and kept the other, does reason cause your love?" Think of someone you love. You did not arrive at them by argument, and a good argument against them would not stop you. That is not the same as blindness. It means love does not begin where reasons begin. Notice also the clause before it: the heart "hardens itself against one or the other at its own will." The heart is not only something that happens to us. It can close, and it can open, and Pascal holds us responsible for which.

For him, faith belongs on the same side: "God sensible to the heart, not to the reason." That is his claim, and a reader does not have to share it to make use of the rest. The part about love and first principles stands without it.

The last line is the most useful, and it is the least quoted. "Men often mistake their imagination for their heart, and they believe they are converted as soon as they think of being converted." It is a brake on the first sentence. A strong feeling is not a conviction, and a fine thought about change is not change.

A few snags, then.

The first sentence is easily used as a licence: follow any feeling, it is the heart. Pascal's last line warns against exactly that, and he does not say how to tell the two apart in this passage. There is one clue. He says that we must place our faith in the heart "or it will be always vacillating." On that reading, the heart is the part that holds. A mood that comes and goes with the weather, with a good lunch or a bad night, is probably not what he means.

Then there is the sound of the first claim, that reason "slumbers". It can be heard as an attack on thinking. It is better heard as a description of a tool that works one step at a time and needs a place to stand. Pascal says it is the heart that supplies the place.

And there is the matter of what is left for readers who do not share his faith. A good deal. The passage about the heart's reasons does not require a creed to be true of friendship, of home, of the stubborn conviction that a person is worth trusting. Only the last move, the one about God, is specifically his.

For an ordinary day, that comes to this: be honest about where your reasons run out. When someone asks why you are walking, or when you ask yourself, give the first answer that comes. Do not improve it. Then listen to it, and notice whether it was an answer of reasons or an answer of love. Neither is wrong. But it is useful to know which one you gave.

## For the walk

- What am I sure of today that I could not prove to a stranger?
- Notice one thing you like about the road: a light, a smell, the sound of your own steps on a certain surface. Do not ask why you like it. Let it be liked. If somebody asks why you are on this road, give the first answer that comes, unimproved, and hear how it sounds.

## At the end of the day

Look back for the moments that struck you without a reason. Not the postcard ones. Perhaps the way a door was opened for you. A smell from a kitchen. A stranger's laugh that made the whole hour lighter. Pascal records a friend, M. de Roannez, saying: "Reasons come afterwards, but at first a thing pleases or shocks me, without my knowing the reason". Often they do not come at all, and the thing was still real.

If you were asked today why you are walking, how did you answer? And did the answer change as the hours went by? It often does. The first one, offered at breakfast, may not be the one you would give at six in the evening, and it is worth knowing which of them feels truer now.

Then look at the other side, carefully, because the last line of the passage applies tonight more than any other night. A long day of walking makes the mind prone to verdicts. Tonight you may be quite sure that this whole enterprise was a mistake. Or you may be sure that you have understood something very large. Both can come from a body that has walked a long way and eaten too little. Pascal's warning is that imagination can wear the heart's clothes. The cure for that is not to distrust everything. It is to leave the big conclusions until you are rested, and see whether they are still there.

Not everything the heart says deserves to be believed, either. Some of it is plain prejudice: the quick dislike of a face, the certainty that a certain person will be tiresome. Pascal does not exempt those. The heart's reasons need testing too, and the testing is reason's job. The two are meant to work together.

It is also a fair evening for letting reason have a turn. Is there something you felt strongly today that you would like to test by daylight? A decision, a resentment, a certainty about another person? Put it on a shelf for the morning. If it is the heart's, it will still be there, and it will probably be steadier.

And then the other direction. Call to mind, for a minute, someone you love without needing a reason, and let yourself simply be glad of them. If it is not too late to ring or message them, you might. It need not be about anything.

Be easy with the tiredness. A tired heart is still a heart, and a tired mind is still a mind. Neither of them has to be brilliant tonight. If all this is too much to hold, hold one thing: the person you thought of.

- What moved me today without my being able to say why?
- Where did I mistake a mood for a conviction?
- Whom do I love without needing a reason, and when did I last tell them?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the things I know without proof: the ground under me, the people I love, the pull of something I cannot name. Keep me from taking my moods for my heart. Let the ones I love be well tonight. Let me sleep without arguing.*
