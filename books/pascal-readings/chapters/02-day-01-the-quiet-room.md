# Day 1. The quiet room

*What happens in you when there is nothing to do?*

## The passage

> When I have set myself now and then to consider the various distractions of men, the toils and dangers to which they expose themselves in the court or the camp, whence arise so many quarrels and passions, such daring and often such evil exploits, etc., I have discovered that all the misfortunes of men arise from one thing only, that they are unable to stay quietly in their own chamber. A man who has enough to live on, if he knew how to dwell with pleasure in his own home, would not leave it for sea-faring or to besiege a city. An office in the army would not be bought so dearly but that it seems insupportable not to stir from the town, and people only seek conversation and amusing games because they cannot remain with pleasure in their own homes. … Hence it comes that men so love noise and movement, hence it comes that a prison is so horrible a punishment, hence it comes that the pleasure of solitude is a thing incomprehensible.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Misery of Man without God, "Diversion" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

Somewhere in the first days of a road there is an hour with nothing in it. The boots are off. The washing is hung up. The phone is down to its last few per cent, and supper is a while away. You are in a small room with a bed in it, and for once nothing needs doing.

Watch what your hand does next. For most of us it moves towards something: a screen, a map you have already read, a bag that does not really need repacking. It goes before you have decided anything.

Pascal spent a good part of his notes watching people do this. He looked at the courts and camps of his day, at the quarrels and the risky ventures, and decided that one thing lay under nearly all of it: people are "unable to stay quietly in their own chamber." A man with enough to live on, he says, would never leave home to go to sea or besiege a city, if he knew how to be at ease there. We do not really want the conversation or the games. We want not to be at home.

He even pictures the grandest case, a king with every comfort, and asks what the king's household is for. They are there, he says, to "think only how to divert the king, and to prevent his thinking of self." Take away the entertainments and the most powerful man in the country is a man alone with a problem he cannot solve.

The problem, in the same notes, is darker than the sentence above. At home you are left with yourself, and when we look at ourselves attentively we find "the natural evil of our weak and mortal condition, so miserable that nothing can console us when we think of it attentively." Noise is a way of not looking. That is why, he says, a day's hunting is not about the hare: people "seek the chase and not the quarry."

There is something worth saying here about quiet roads. Quiet is not only the absence of sound. A lane with no traffic and no talk may be the first place in months where your own thoughts are the loudest thing you can hear. That can be a relief or an assault, and it is often both before lunch.

Three things are hard in this passage.

The first is the size of the sentence. All the misfortunes of men from one cause? Hunger, fever and bad weather would like a word. Read it as "a great many of them" and it still stings, but it is probably an exaggeration, and you are allowed to say so.

The second is the man "who has enough to live on". Many people leave home because they must, and a quiet room is a comfort that not everyone has. For anyone grieving or lonely, it can be the loudest place there is. Pascal is not speaking to them, and nothing in the passage tells them to sit and bear it.

The third is that you have left your room, and by a long way. A pilgrim looks like the opposite of a man staying quietly at home. But Pascal's target is not travelling. It is flight, and the same walk can be either. You can flee yourself across a whole country, one village after another. Or you can use the road as the nearest thing there is to a quiet room with a view: a body that is busy, and a mind with nowhere to go but where it is. Which one your walk is, only you can tell, and the answer may change by the afternoon.

What does this ask of an ordinary day? Very little, and it is harder than it sounds. A few minutes without a diversion. Sit on the edge of the bed, or on a bench, with your hands empty. Three minutes is plenty. The point is not to feel anything in particular. It is a small experiment: see what you reach for, and see what comes up when you do not reach. Do not try to fix it. Pascal suspects that what comes up is the very thing the noise was for. You may find it is smaller than he thought, or larger, or only boredom, which has its own interest. It is also the one exercise in this book that needs no equipment at all.

## For the walk

- What do I reach for when nothing is happening?
- For ten minutes of today's walk, go without music, podcast or screen. Choose the ten minutes now, before you set out, so that the choice is not made by a tired mind at the time. If your mind insists on running, let it, and only notice where it runs first. Then put that thought down and take the next step. Afterwards, notice how the ten minutes felt: long, short, or not like ten minutes at all.

## At the end of the day

The first evening of a road is often a quiet one, whether you meant it to be or not. The body has made its point. Whatever the day held is behind you now, and the room is small. If you can, put your feet up for this part.

If the quiet came to you today, even for five minutes, take it out and look at it. Was it empty, or only quiet? They are different things. An empty quiet feels like waiting for something to start. A quiet that is not empty feels more like a room that has been tidied: nothing needed, nothing missing. Some days you will get only the first, and that is simply the day's honest report.

Be fair to the hand that reached for the phone. It is not a vice at every hour. A message from someone you love, a map that stopped a wrong turn, a photograph of a door you liked: none of that is diversion in Pascal's sense. His word is for noise used to avoid something. Only you know which was which, and you do not have to decide tonight.

Try a rough count, without turning it into a score. Out of today's hours on foot, how many had something in your ears or in your hand? The answer may surprise you in either direction, and either answer says something about the day.

A word about tiredness. After a day on foot, quiet comes easily because the body insists on it, and it can pass for peace. It is a good thing, but it is not quite what Pascal was asking about, which is quiet when you are not worn out. Save that for a morning or a rest day. If tonight's quiet was only tiredness, that too is worth knowing.

If you are sharing a room tonight, the quiet will not be yours alone. There will be zips, breathing, the bunk that speaks whenever somebody turns over. You can still try a small version of Pascal's room: lie on your back for a minute and listen to everything in it, one sound after another, as if each were only one more thing the room contains. It is a way of staying in the room without asking it to be something else.

And if the quiet brought something up, a worry, a name, a loss, you do not have to deal with it in the dark at ten o'clock at night. Let it be noted and left until morning. If it is too heavy to carry alone, that is not a failure of the exercise. It is what friends and doctors are for. Tell someone you trust. In an emergency, 112 is the European number.

- When today did I reach for something to fill a silence, and what was it?
- Was there a minute that was quiet and not empty?
- What am I carrying that I would rather not meet, and who could help me meet it?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for this room and for the day that led me to it. Let me sit here without hurry. If something hard comes, let me not run from it, and let me not be alone with it. Thank you for the people who made the bed. Let the quiet be kind tonight, and let sleep come easily.*
