# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the ten days, or you are stopping early, or you mean to go back to a day you skipped. All of these are fine. The book is not a course, and nobody is keeping marks. It was made to be a little company on a quiet road, and company does not have to stay once the road is done.

## Ten small things

Ten small things fit in a pocket, and you have held each of them once already.

- The room: a few minutes with nothing in your hands.
- The reed: a worth that is not measured in distance.
- The spaces: wonder before explanation, and a silence you can choose.
- The automaton: one small act, done awake, and done again.
- The present: coming back to your own steps, three at a time.
- The heart: some things known without proof, and the care not to mistake a mood for them.
- The angel and the brute: the body first, then the rest of you.
- The wager: a question held open honestly, with no pretending in either direction.
- The half-light: the next step by the light you have.
- The consolation: the looking itself is allowed.

Pick one, and let the others go for now. Choose the one you can remember without looking. It will do more for you than all ten kept in a drawer.

## Going on

If you would like more of Pascal, he is easy to find. The edition we used, in C. Kegan Paul's translation of 1885, is on Project Gutenberg as ebook number 46921, and it is the same text every passage here was checked against. Our ten days come from the sections called Diversion, The Greatness and Littleness of Man (twice), Man's Disproportion, The Weakness, Unrest, and Defects of Man (twice), Various Thoughts, Of the Need of Seeking Truth, Proofs of the Christian Religion and The Mystery of Jesus. The Thoughts are short and can be read one at a time. The translator's preface and the notes at the back are not needed, and you can skip them.

A warning is fair. Much of the book is addressed to a reader who is being led, step by step, towards the Christian faith. We have left aside the long sections on prophecy, on the Jewish people, on miracles, and on the quarrel between Jesuits and Jansenists. They are his, and they will mean most to readers who share his world. If you read on, read what helps, and pass quietly over what does not.

Some of this will not have worked, and that is fair. A day that left you cold on the road may speak to you in a kitchen in March. A passage that asked for more belief than you had was not a test you failed. The wager in particular may have sat badly. If something barely touched you, put it down. Nothing has gone wrong.

## A last word

At the start of this book we quoted a single line from the *Thoughts*: "Rivers are roads which move and carry us whither we wish to go." It is an odd little sentence to find among so many large ones, and it may be the kindest. A road that carries you is a road you do not have to push. Some of the best stretches of a long walk may be the ones that go on by themselves, while the mind catches up.

A mind that cannot sit still is not a fault peculiar to pilgrims. It is the common condition, and Pascal knew it better than most. What the road offers, if it offers anything, is a little room around that mind and a little time to see what it is looking for.

Walk again, when you can. Or tomorrow, to the end of your own street and back, with nothing in your hands and nowhere to be, and see what you reach for.

*Buen Camino.*
