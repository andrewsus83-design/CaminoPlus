# Day 2. A reed that thinks

*Where does your worth come from, if not from how far you can walk?*

## The passage

> Not from space must I seek my dignity, but from the ruling of my thought. I should have no more if I possessed whole worlds. By space the Universe encompasses and swallows me as an atom, by thought I encompass it. Man is but a reed, weakest in nature, but a reed which thinks. It needs not that the whole Universe should arm to crush him. A vapour, a drop of water is enough to kill him. But were the Universe to crush him, man would still be more noble than that which has slain him, because he knows that he dies, and that the Universe has the better of him. The Universe knows nothing of this. All our dignity therefore consists in thought. By this must we raise ourselves, not by space or duration which we cannot fill. Then let us make it our study to think well, for this is the starting-point of morals.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Misery of Man without God, "The Greatness and Littleness of Man" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

By the second morning a number has moved in with you. Kilometres done, kilometres to go. It is a good number. It can be checked, it only grows, and it flatters. It also measures exactly one thing: how much ground your body has covered. The same is true of the stamps in a pilgrim's *credencial*, the passport that is stamped along the way. They are lovely, and they record where you have been. They do not record who was there.

That kind of measure is what Pascal means by space. Space is size, bulk, ground, the amount of the world you take up or cross. Set against all of it, he says, you are nothing much: "By space the Universe encompasses and swallows me as an atom." He grants that without a fight. Then comes the turn. "By thought I encompass it."

The picture he draws for this is a reed, "weakest in nature", but "a reed which thinks." The Universe, he says, does not even need to arm itself to crush a man: "A vapour, a drop of water is enough to kill him." On a hot afternoon that is plain fact, and a reason to carry more water than you think you need. But the reed has one thing the Universe has not. It knows. The Universe has the better of him, and he is aware of it, and "The Universe knows nothing of this." That awareness, small as it is, is where he puts our dignity.

It helps to be clear what he does not mean. He does not mean cleverness. The last line of the passage is not "think much" but "think well", and he calls that the starting-point of morals. As we read it, thinking well is closer to attention than to intelligence: deciding what to put your mind on, and doing it honestly. His phrase for this is "the ruling of my thought". You cannot rule what happens to you. You can rule, a little, where your mind goes. Every walker does it a dozen times a day without noticing: turning from the pain in a heel to the person walking ahead, from a grievance to the next bend, from a number to a hill.

Two things deserve a second look.

A dignity that rests on thought can sound like a good deal for the clever and a poor one for everyone else. Pascal knows the worry. A little later in the same section he writes "But what is this thought? how foolish it is!" and, a little further, "How great it is in essence, how vile in defects!" He is not boasting about the mind. What he points to is a kind of knowing that needs no cleverness: knowing that you are small, that you are fragile, that you will die. A very plain mind can know that. Even so, if the passage leaves you cold on behalf of people who cannot think straight, from illness or age or exhaustion, that is a fair reaction, and the passage itself does not answer it.

The other hard point is the bare line near the end. "The Universe knows nothing of this." The reed's nobility is lonely: a thing that knows it is dying, in a universe that does not notice. Pascal was a believer, and in other places he sets the reed inside a larger story. Here he leaves it standing in the open, and it is worth letting it stand there for a while before reaching for any comfort.

In practice, then, it asks you to stop letting the number tell you who you are. The day's worth is not its distance. A day on which you walked less far may have been the day you paid the best attention. A day when you covered a lot of ground may have been spent mostly in a mental argument with someone who is not there. Neither of those shows up on the count.

It also asks you to notice what bends. If the day is windy, find something in the verge that moves and watch how it takes the wind: it gives, and it stays rooted. Pascal chose the reed for its weakness. The part of us that thinks is not strong. It can be thrown by a hunger, a draught, a bad night. It is still the part that knows what is happening to the rest.

## For the walk

- If nobody ever asked how far I had walked, how would I measure today?
- Choose one small question you care about, and follow it to its end on one stretch of road. Do not worry at it. Follow it, as you would follow a path, one step after another, and see where it comes out. When it has nowhere further to go, stop thinking and simply walk. If you find that you have been following it for a mile, that is allowed too.

## At the end of the day

You know tonight's number already. It was in your mind before you had your boots off. Try putting it to one side for a few minutes and asking what else the day held.

A day on foot is made mostly of small thoughts. To stop for water. To remember a name. To notice you had left something behind. To change your mind about a stranger. Pascal's "think well" can be as modest as that. Was there a thought today that you are glad you had? It need not have been deep. It might have been only the moment you saw that someone was struggling and slowed down.

There is a hard half to look at as well. Where did your mind run on its own, in circles, over something you could do nothing about? Not to scold it. A reed that thinks is also a reed that worries, and the worry is part of the same equipment. The point is only to see how much of the day it took.

Be honest about tiredness, too. The reed is bent tonight, and the Universe has not even had to try. The body is weak, and the mind at the end of a day is a poor judge and a worse planner. The kindest use of it is not to make it work harder. It is to stop it passing verdicts on you. Thinking well tonight might mean nothing more than not thinking badly of yourself.

If the mind will not stop once you are lying down, do not fight it. Give it something small and finite: three things for tomorrow morning, said once, and then put down. A reed in the dark thinks louder than a reed in daylight, and it does better with a short list than with an open field.

A good deal of what is done well in a day is seen by nobody. A bag moved from the path of someone's feet, a greeting returned, a decision not to say the sharper thing. Pascal's Universe knows nothing of the reed's dying, and it knows nothing of these either. Our reading, and it is only ours, is that this does not make them smaller. The knowing was yours, and it was enough while you were doing them.

Remember the others as well. Every walker on the road is the same kind of reed. The person who passed you this morning and the one you passed this afternoon are worth the same, and neither worth is a pace. Their number tells you almost nothing about them. If you caught yourself comparing, notice it, and let it go.

And since a drop of water is part of the passage: drink some before you sleep. It is a small act of thinking well.

- Where today did I measure myself by distance, speed or someone else's pace?
- What did I give my attention to, and was it worth giving?
- What is one thought from today that I am glad to have had?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for a mind that can notice, and a body that carries it. Forgive me for counting the wrong things. Teach me to put my attention where it does some good. For tonight, let the day be enough, whatever the number was. Let the ones who walked slower and faster than I did be well.*
