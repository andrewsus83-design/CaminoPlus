# Day 5. Hope to live

*Where is your mind while your feet are on the road?*

## The passage

> We care nothing for the present. We anticipate the future as too slow in coming, as if we could make it move faster; or we call back the past, to stop its rapid flight. So imprudent are we that we wander through the times in which we have no part, unthinking of that which alone is ours; so frivolous are we that we dream of the days which are not, and pass by without reflection those which alone exist. For the present generally gives us pain; we conceal it from our sight because it afflicts us, and if it be pleasant we regret to see it vanish away. We endeavour to sustain the present by the future, and think of arranging things not in our power, for a time at which we have no certainty of arriving. If we examine our thoughts, we shall find them always occupied with the past or the future. We scarcely think of the present, and if we do so, it is only that we may borrow light from it to direct the future. The present is never our end; the past and the present are our means, the future alone is our end. Thus we never live, but hope to live, and while we always lay ourselves out to be happy, it is inevitable that we can never be so.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Misery of Man without God, "The Weakness, Unrest, and Defects of Man" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

Count, if you can, how many times your mind has been somewhere else since you set off. At the next café. At the bed you will have tonight. In the shower, which you are already enjoying in advance. In tomorrow's stage, in an email waiting at home, in a conversation from last week that you are winning this time. Your feet are on the road, and your head is mostly not. A day of walking is a long invitation to be elsewhere.

Pascal saw this in everyone, himself included: he writes "we" and not "they". We "anticipate the future as too slow in coming, as if we could make it move faster", and we "call back the past, to stop its rapid flight". Neither works, and we keep doing both. The result, he says, is that we wander "through the times in which we have no part, unthinking of that which alone is ours." The present is the only part of time that belongs to us, and it is the part we treat as a corridor.

He is not telling you to give up planning. The passage says plainly that we may "borrow light" from the present "to direct the future", and a walker who never looked ahead would be hungry and lost by evening. His complaint is about proportion. The present, he says, is treated as a means and never as an end: "the past and the present are our means, the future alone is our end." So we go through our days as a long approach to something else, and the something else never arrives as an arrival, because when it comes it is a present too, and we are already looking past it. "Thus we never live, but hope to live."

For a pilgrim this has an edge, because a pilgrimage is aimed at an end. It has a name on the map. There is nothing wrong with that. But the road to it is made of days, each of them as present as the last, and the person who walks the whole way thinking about the end has spent the whole way thinking about something that is not there.

A few things are hard here.

"The present generally gives us pain." That is not true of every morning, and it is not even what he says in the next breath: if the present is pleasant, "we regret to see it vanish away". Even the good moment is spoiled by watching it go. That may be a fair account of some temperaments and some days. It is a bleak thing to say about all of them, and he assumes that everyone is alike in this. You may already know which kind of walker you are, and you can decide how much of it fits you.

Pascal is not offering a technique, either. These days there are many schools of being present, and he belongs to none. He describes. He thinks the restlessness runs deeper than a trick of attention can reach, which is the point of Day 1. So take today's practice as a small thing, and not as a cure.

And hope is not a vice. A person who did not hope to arrive, or to see someone, or to get better, would be in trouble. Read his last sentence as a warning about the balance and not as a ban. The clause about "a time at which we have no certainty of arriving" is blunt, too. You can take it as a fright, or as a quiet reason to enjoy the plain fact that you are, so far, here.

The practice is to come back, many times. Nobody can stay in the present for a whole morning, and the aim is not to. The aim is to notice that you have left and to return. It helps to have somewhere to return to: the sound of your steps, the weight of the pack on your shoulders, the temperature of the air on your face. One more word of ordinary prudence. On any stretch with traffic, the present is not optional. Keep your eyes and ears on it.

## For the walk

- Where is my mind right now: ahead, behind, or here?
- Whenever you notice that you have left, and you will notice often, come back to your own steps. Count three of them. Use a bend in the path or a village sign as a reminder if that helps. That is the whole practice. Do not scold the mind for leaving. The coming back is the exercise, and every time you do it counts, however soon you leave again.

## At the end of the day

The day is over, and that is a rare relief. There is nothing left to anticipate until the morning except supper and sleep. For once the future is short enough that the mind can stop leaning into it. If there was a moment today when you were wholly where you were, take it out and look at it. It need not have been beautiful. It might have been a drink of water.

It need not even have been pleasant. A blister is the most present thing in the world. Pascal says the present "generally gives us pain", and a sore heel agrees with him. That does not make attention a pleasure. It only means that attention is real, and that a day is often more present than we give it credit for in the places where it hurt.

Take the pleasure you looked forward to most today, the shower, the chair, the cold drink. When it came, were you there for it, or already on to the next thing? It is a curious test, and the answer may well be "only partly". Anticipation can use up a good deal of what it hoped for.

Think about what you were always getting ahead of. For many people it is rehearsal: speeches given in the head to people who are not there, plans for a stage that will arrive in its own time. The mind is trying to be useful, and a good deal of this is kindly meant. Still, count how many of the rehearsed conversations will ever happen, and how many of today's hours went on them.

The past needs the same look. Did you call something back today, a remark you wish you had not made, an hour at home that went badly, a face? For tonight it is finished. You cannot change it by carrying it. You can only decide whether it goes to bed with you. Hand it over, in whatever way you like, to someone bigger than you, or just to the night.

Tiredness helps here, for once. A tired body makes the present compulsory: a heel, a shoulder, a hunger. You are present by default tonight, and it is fine to let that be restful. The one temptation at the end of a day is to plan tomorrow's stage in minute detail. Once is enough. The plan is made, and the plan can wait where it is until the morning.

If there is a meal tonight, try one small thing: take the first three mouthfuls with nothing else going on. No screen, no conversation, no planning. It is a modest way of being in the only place you have been all day.

- When today was I wholly where I was, even for a minute?
- What was I always getting ahead of?
- What can I leave where it lies tonight, instead of carrying it into tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for this day, exactly as long as it was. Forgive me for hurrying through the best parts of it. Hold the days I have left behind and the ones that are not yet mine. Let me rest in this one evening, which is the only one I have. Let the morning keep its own business.*
