# Day 8. The wager, read with care

*What would you risk, if you could not be sure?*

## The passage

> Let us then examine this point, and say "God is, or he is not." But to which side shall we incline? Reason can determine nothing about it. There is an infinite gulf fixed between us. A game is playing at the extremity of this infinite distance in which heads or tails may turn up. What will you wager? There is no reason for backing either one or the other, you cannot reasonably argue in favour of either. … Labour then to convince yourself, not by increase of the proofs of God, but by the diminution of your passions. You would fain arrive at faith, but know not the way; you would heal yourself of unbelief, and you ask remedies for it. Learn of those who have been bound as you are, but who now stake all that they possess; these are they who know the way you would follow, who are cured of a disease of which you would be cured. Follow the way by which they began, by making believe that they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said, etc. Thus you will naturally be brought to believe, and will lose your acuteness. … Now what evil will happen to you in taking this side? You will be trustworthy, honourable, humble, grateful, generous, friendly, sincere, and true.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Happiness of Man with God, "Of the Need of Seeking Truth" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

This is the best-known part of the *Thoughts*, and one of the easiest to get wrong, so we will go slowly.

Picture a fork in the path with no sign. You cannot work out which branch is right from where you stand. You also cannot stand there for ever, because standing there is itself a choice, and by dusk it will have made the decision for you. That picture is ours, not Pascal's, but it is close to his situation.

His question is the largest one: whether there is a God. The passage opens by granting that argument will not settle it. "Reason can determine nothing about it." Elsewhere he says outright that he does not mean to prove it by natural reasons: "Therefore I do not here undertake to prove by natural reasons either the existence of God". So the wager is not a proof. It is addressed to someone who has already concluded that reason is stuck and who would like to know what to do.

The part we have left out is the argument that gives the page its name. In it, Pascal's reader objects that the right thing is not to wager at all, and Pascal replies that this is not an option: "you are embarked in the affair." Not choosing is a way of choosing. Then he reasons like a man at a gaming table. If you stake a little and the prize is everything, "if you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing." The odds, he says, are in favour of backing the side that offers so much.

The reader concedes the arithmetic and then says what is really wrong: "my hands are tied and my mouth is gagged: I am forced to wager and am not free, none can release me, but I am so made that I cannot believe." That is where today's passage resumes. Pascal does not tell him to look for more proofs. He tells him to labour at something else, "not by increase of the proofs of God, but by the diminution of your passions." He points to people who were once where the reader is and who have taken up a way of life. The way includes practices, and the reader is told to behave as they do before he believes as they do. The reader replies with a fear, "But that is just what I fear", and Pascal answers with a question: "what have you to lose?"

In the stretch we have left out, between the second and third parts of the passage, he adds that his words are those of someone who has "thrown himself on his knees" to pray that the reader would also give his whole self. So it is not a trick played from a safe seat. Then comes the last claim: that the life this leads to is one of a person who is "trustworthy, honourable, humble, grateful, generous, friendly, sincere, and true."

Where it is hard, and it is hard in several places.

The register is the first. A gaming table, for the deepest question there is. To some readers it is cold, and to others it turns faith into an investment. Pascal seems to know how it sounds, since his objector is so uncomfortable with it.

The second is that the argument sees two sides: "God is, or he is not." Readers who see more than two live possibilities, more than one religion, or a God who would not be moved by a calculated bet, will find the odds less clear than Pascal does. The passage does not take that up.

The third is the advice: "making believe that they believed, taking the holy water, having masses said". These are Catholic practices, and to many readers they will sound like pretending. Day 4 is the background. For Pascal, custom trains the automaton, and the likeliest reading is that he is not asking for a lie. But it is fair to say that this line is hard to hear. And the objector's fear, of losing his "acuteness", is brushed aside with a question. Some readers will feel that the fear is worth more than that.

What is being asked of you? Not belief on command. If you have a faith, it asks you to be honest about whether it is held by the heart or only by habit. If you have none, it asks only that you not treat the question as settled before you have looked at it. And for a reader who finds the whole thing unconvincing, closing the book at this page is allowed. This may be the day of the ten most likely to irritate, and being irritated by it is a respectable way to spend a day.

## For the walk

- Which question do I treat as settled that I have never really looked at?
- At your next rest, hold one question open for a minute without answering it: "Is there more here than I can see?" Walk a hundred steps with it and let it stay unanswered. If it irritates you, that is information too. If you catch yourself hurrying to answer it, slow down. The point is the minute, not the answer.

## At the end of the day

This is, for a good many people, the hardest day of the ten. If you spent it irritated with Pascal, that is a reasonable use of a day, and you do not have to apologise to a man who died in 1662.

Look first at something smaller. What did you risk today on something you could not be sure of? Setting out in uncertain weather. Trusting a stranger's directions. Taking a path whose end you could not see. A long walk is made of small wagers, and nobody had proved that the bed would be there. Pascal makes the same point close by, in his own way: "how much we do on an uncertainty, as sea voyages, battles!" Most of life is acted on without proof.

Then there is the sentence the objector says: "I am so made that I cannot believe." It is an honest place to stand, and Pascal's reply does not call it a sin. Whether you can believe, or cannot, or are somewhere in between, say it plainly to yourself, with no pretending in either direction. That is the one thing the passage cannot do for you.

His question can be asked both ways. "What have you to lose?" What would you lose by looking honestly at the largest questions? And what would you lose by never looking? You do not have to answer tonight. But both are questions with real answers, and they are different answers for different people.

There is a quieter use of the passage that needs no belief at all. Its last line is a list: trustworthy, honourable, humble, grateful, generous, friendly, sincere, true. Pascal says that whoever takes the road he describes will gain in this life, at each step. Leave aside whether that is so. Run your eye down the list, and ask which of those words today earned. Not all eight. Perhaps one, in an hour you had forgotten. That is a fair thing to take to bed.

One more thing, about people. Fellow walkers answer this question every way there is: some with a settled faith, some with none, a great many in between, and quite a few who have never been asked. The man who wrote this page, by his own account, prayed for the reader on his knees. You might ask yourself how you would treat someone who answered differently from you, and how you did treat the people you met today.

Tonight is not the night to settle anything this size, and nothing here needs deciding by morning. A wager has no deadline except the one life sets, and that is a long way off from a bunk in a hostel at nine o'clock. Tiredness makes big questions bigger. If you can, let this one be a question tonight, and sleep.

- What did I risk today on something I could not be sure of?
- What would I honestly lose by looking at the largest question, and what by never looking?
- Where is the line between taking up a practice and pretending, and which side of it was I on today?

## A short prayer

*I do not know tonight what I believe, and I will not pretend to. Thank you for the freedom to ask. Let me be honest in either direction, and kind to those who answer differently. Stay with me while I do not know.*
