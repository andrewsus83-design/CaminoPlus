# Day 3. The silence of the spaces

*What do you do with a silence that does not answer?*

## The passage

> Whoso takes this survey of himself will be terrified at the thought that he is upheld in the material being, given him by nature, between these two abysses of the infinite and nothing, he will tremble at the sight of these marvels; and I think that as his curiosity changes into wonder, he will be more disposed to contemplate them in silence than to search into them with presumption. … When I consider the short duration of my life, swallowed up in the eternity before and after, the small space which I fill, or even can see, engulfed in the infinite immensity of spaces whereof I know nothing, and which know nothing of me, I am terrified, and wonder that I am here rather than there, for there is no reason why here rather than there, or now rather than then. … The eternal silence of these infinite spaces alarms me.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Misery of Man without God, "Man's Disproportion" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

On a clear night, away from street lamps, the sky is a different thing from the one you see on the way to a bus stop. There is more of it. The stars are thick enough to look like weather. If you stand still long enough, something happens that is not quite a thought: a sense of being very small and of there being a great deal of room.

Pascal went in for that feeling on purpose. In the pages just before this passage he takes the reader up first. The earth, he says, is a point beside the circle the sun describes, and that circle is itself a fine point beside the one the stars describe. He calls the whole of it "an infinite sphere, the centre of which is every where, the circumference no where." Then he turns the reader round and sends him the other way. Look at the smallest creature you know, a mite, and follow its body down through limbs, veins and drops. At the bottom, he promises, you will find a whole universe, with its own sky and its own mites, and so on without end.

"Whoso takes this survey of himself", the passage begins, and the self it means is what is left after both trips: a thing hung between two abysses, the infinitely great and the infinitely small, able to reach the end of neither. His word for the feeling is terror, and he is not exaggerating for effect, since he uses it again in the second stretch: "I am terrified".

What he proposes is not a cure. It is a posture. When curiosity turns into wonder, he says, a person will be "more disposed to contemplate them in silence than to search into them with presumption." This is the first of several silences in this book, and it is a chosen one. You stop trying to take the measure of the whole and let it be larger than you.

The famous line is the last one. "The eternal silence of these infinite spaces alarms me." It can be heard as a line about awe. Pascal wrote alarm. The sky does not speak. It goes on, in every direction, saying nothing to him, and he cannot take that calmly. Between those two silences, the one you choose and the one that is simply there, sits his question: "wonder that I am here rather than there." Straight after, he asks it in plainer words: "Who has set me here?" It is a question you may well ask in a bad hour, with a blister in mind, and it deserves its serious version too.

Just after the first stretch of the passage he gives the shape of the middle. What is man in nature? "A nothing in regard to the infinite, a whole in regard to nothing, a mean between nothing and the whole." A walker lives in that middle all day. The map of the whole country is one scale, and the stone under your boot is another. You are neither, and you move between them at the speed of a person.

Three cautions.

Do not feel obliged to feel what he felt. A sky can be frightening, or merely beautiful, or simply there, and none of these is a misreading. His alarm is his. You are free to feel awe, or nothing, or boredom.

Do not take "contemplate in silence rather than search" as a ban on asking questions. Pascal was a mathematician, and the pages around this passage are full of careful reasoning. What he is warning against is the person who sets out to understand "the origin of all that is" and is sure the whole thing can be grasped. The warning is about presumption, not about curiosity.

And do not skip over "no reason why here rather than there." There is a vertigo in the idea that your place and time are accidents. Some readers will find that bleak, and some will find it a relief, and some will answer it differently from Pascal. He answers it with his faith. You may not.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? A minute of wonder without a verdict. Look at something large, or something very small, and do not explain it. The day is full of such things, and most of them go by while we are working out what to have for supper.

This is the book's title seen from the other side. On the first day the quiet was a room you could leave. Here it is the sky, and you cannot. The nearest you can come to peace with it, Pascal suggests, is to stop arguing with its size.

## For the walk

- What would I like an answer to, and can I get through one day without it?
- At your next rest, find the smallest living thing within reach: an ant, a seed head, a beetle on a stone. Watch it for a minute. Then lift your eyes to the farthest thing you can see, a ridge or a cloud or a line of trees. Between the two, take two breaths in silence. You have nothing to say about either.

## At the end of the day

If the sky is clear tonight, and you are somewhere safe and close to a door, go outside for a minute. A garden, a terrace or a village street will do. Do not stand on an unlit road for the sake of a view. If it is cloudy, or you are too tired, nothing is lost. The same size is there above the cloud, whether or not you look. You can also take the trip with your eyes closed: the village from above, then the country, then the whole turning planet, and back down to your own bunk and the weight of the blanket. It takes less than a minute and it ends somewhere warm.

Smallness at the end of a long day is often a relief. The day's problems shrink to what they were. The argument you had with yourself at three o'clock, the weight of the pack, the thing you had to remember, all of them seem more modest under a lot of sky. If that happened to you today, it was a small kindness from a passage that was meant to alarm.

If it went the other way, and the size felt like a threat, that is his feeling too, and it is not an error. It passes, and it is usually worth a sentence to someone, a friend or the person in the next bunk, said plainly. Said aloud, such things often shrink.

There is a gentler version of his line about kingdoms: "How many kingdoms know nothing of us!" It applies to the road. You passed a great many houses today, and in each of them, from the inside, the day was the whole world. Someone was making soup. Someone was waiting for a phone call. None of them needed you, and none of them was a backdrop. They were the other half of the infinite, the half you can see.

And a word on tiredness. The body is a small thing, it has done a lot, and it wants a bed. Do not ask it for a metaphysics tonight. Questions about why we are here do not go away, and they will be as large in the morning.

The end of a day is also the hour of small bright rectangles. A minute of sky is a different use of the same eyes, and it asks nothing of them.

One more thought on the silence. It can be a refusal, or it can be a room. From outside, the two sound alike, and which one you are in may depend on who is listening and how the day has gone. It may even change while you stand there. You do not have to decide tonight which it is.

- Where did I feel small today, and was it a fear or a relief?
- What did I look at today without trying to explain it?
- What would I like an answer to, and can I let it wait one more night?

## A short prayer

*Whoever is listening, or no one: thank you for the size of the sky and the smallness of my day. Let me be quiet without being afraid. Let the questions wait until morning. Keep safe the people in the houses I passed, and the ones I have left at home. Let me sleep like something small that is not lost.*
