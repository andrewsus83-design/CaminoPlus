# Day 4. What the body believes

*What are your feet teaching the rest of you?*

## The passage

> For we must not mistake ourselves, we have as much that is automatic in us as intellectual, and hence it comes that the instrument by which persuasion is brought about is not demonstration alone. How few things are demonstrated! Proofs can only convince the mind; custom makes our strongest proofs and those which we hold most firmly, it sways the automaton, which draws the unconscious intellect after it. Who has demonstrated that there will be a to-morrow, or that we shall die; yet what is more universally believed? It is then custom that convinces us of it, custom that makes so many men Christians, custom that makes them Turks, heathen, artisans, soldiers, etc. … We must acquire a more easy belief, that of custom, which without violence, without art, without argument, causes our assent and inclines all our powers to this belief, so that our soul naturally falls into it. It is not enough to believe only by force of conviction if the automaton is inclined to believe the contrary. Both parts of us then must be obliged to believe, the intellect by arguments which it is enough to have admitted once in our lives, the automaton by custom, and by not allowing it to incline in the contrary direction.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *The Thoughts of Blaise Pascal*, The Misery of Man without God, "The Weakness, Unrest, and Defects of Man" (tr. C. Kegan Paul, 1885)

## Reflection

By the fourth morning you no longer decide to get up. You get up. The boots go on, the socks go on first, and the pack is filled in the order it was filled yesterday. Nobody argued you into any of it. Your hands know where the torch is, in the dark, better than you do. A long walk is, among other things, a very long custom: the same movement repeated until the body no longer needs instructions. What that teaches the rest of you is partly up to you.

Pascal has a name for the part of us that does this: the automaton. He says that "we have as much that is automatic in us as intellectual", and he draws a conclusion that is easy to miss. A good argument reaches only the mind. "Proofs can only convince the mind." What we hold most firmly, he says, we hold by custom, which "sways the automaton, which draws the unconscious intellect after it." His example is a modest one. Nobody has demonstrated that there will be a tomorrow, and nobody doubts it, because it has come every day so far. Our confidence in the morning is a habit.

Then he applies the point to belief, and this is where he is most practical. Once the mind has seen where the truth lies, it needs the rest of the person to follow it. "Both parts of us then must be obliged to believe": the intellect by arguments, which it is enough to have admitted once, and the automaton by custom, over and over, by "not allowing it to incline in the contrary direction."

You can try this out on something much smaller than faith. You may be quite convinced that patience is worth having, and still snap at the person rustling a bag at six in the morning. The mind agreed. The automaton had not been told. Or you may be quite sure that you should drink water through a hot day, and forget until your head aches. What gets it done is not the conviction. It is the habit of a sip at every stop, repeated until it needs no one's agreement.

Some of this is awkward, and it is better to say where.

The list in the middle of the passage reads oddly now. It says that it is "custom that makes so many men Christians, custom that makes them Turks, heathen, artisans, soldiers". These are the translation's words, from its century, and the thought underneath them can unsettle anyone. If belief comes from upbringing, is it only upbringing? Pascal does not say so. He does not say that custom proves anything or disproves it. He says that custom carries what the mind has already seen, and that without it a belief wobbles.

Habit also cuts both ways, and the passage does not say so. Elsewhere in the same notes he writes, "There is nothing we cannot make natural, nothing natural we cannot lose." A habit of hurry is trained as surely as a habit of kindness. So is a habit of complaint, or of comparing yourself with the person ahead. The automaton learns what it is given. It does not ask whether it is good.

Then there is this: a habit can be a way of not being there. A walker can do a whole day on autopilot, feet and pack and snack all in order, and arrive having noticed nothing. That is Day 1's diversion in a different coat. The test is not whether you have habits. It is whether you can do them awake.

One more thing is worth saying before it comes up. This is the quieter cousin of Day 8, where Pascal makes the same move with more force and more risk, and invites a reader to begin with practices before the belief arrives. If you are comfortable with today's passage and not with that one, it is worth noticing why.

Which leaves the question of what to do with it. Choose one small repeated act and give it your whole attention each time. The road offers plenty: putting the pack on, taking it off, stepping over the threshold of the place where you will sleep, the first drink of water in the morning. Pick one, and let it be the place where you wake up. The mind decided once. Now let the body practise, slowly enough that you are there for it.

## For the walk

- Which of my habits did I never choose, and would I choose it now?
- Pick one repeated moment today, such as putting the pack on, taking a drink or greeting someone, and do it the same careful way every time. Go slowly enough to be there for it. Do not try to feel anything. Let the repeating do the work. If you forget on the third or fourth time, begin again at the next chance, without commentary.

## At the end of the day

By evening, the habits are doing nearly all the work. Boots off, shoes on, wash, drink, find the charger. The tired body knows the sequence and runs it without asking you. That is what routines are for. They carry you when you cannot carry yourself, and it is worth a moment's gratitude to the part of you that learned them.

Now look at what else it carried today. Which habits served you? Perhaps a stretch before setting out, or a rest taken before you needed it, or a way of greeting people that makes the road friendlier. Which ones served you badly? A sharp remark that arrived before you chose it. A second look at the phone, and then a third. A grumble about the weather that had been practised for years before this road. The automaton does not discriminate. It takes whatever it has been given often enough.

You will have noticed something in how Pascal puts it, too: custom works "without violence, without art, without argument". It does not win by force. It works by being there every day, and by not fighting. That is a reason to suspect, though Pascal does not draw the moral himself, that small and steady changes may last longer than large resolutions made at night.

Pascal's picture has two halves, and tonight you might look for the gap between them. Is there something you have long been convinced of, that you should rest earlier, drink more, speak more gently, ring someone, that your habits have not yet caught up with? The mind has done its part. The gap is not a failure of belief. It is only that the automaton has not been taught.

Habits have bad days, too. A morning with a lost sock or a sore hip breaks the sequence and, with it, the calm it was giving you. The automaton is not a promise, and a routine that breaks is not a verdict on you. It rebuilds from the first act.

Which brings us to tiredness. Tonight is a poor night to reform anything. A tired mind makes bold promises and a rested one has to keep them. So do not resolve to become a different person. Choose a single act for tomorrow, small enough to do even on a bad morning, and leave it at that. The automaton learns by repetition, not by speeches. The last habit of the day sets up the first of tomorrow: water within reach, the pack half-filled, the phone put where it will not be the first thing you touch.

And if today went on autopilot from start to finish, and you cannot say what you saw, that is only information. It does not need a penance. Try tomorrow to be awake for one of its repeated moments.

- What did my body do today without being asked, and was it good?
- Which small act would I like to repeat until it comes without effort?
- Where did habit stand in for attention today?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the feet that know the way better than I do. Thank you for the habits that carried me when I could not carry myself. Forgive the ones that carried me badly. Give me one small act to make my own tomorrow, and the patience to repeat it. Let tomorrow's first act be a gentle one.*
