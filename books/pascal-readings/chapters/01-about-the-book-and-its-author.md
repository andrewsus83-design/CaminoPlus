# About the book and its author

## The man

Blaise Pascal was born at Clermont-Ferrand, in Auvergne, on 19 June 1623, and died on 9 August 1662, aged thirty-nine. His health was poor from childhood and never recovered. His mother died when he was eight, and his father, an official, taught him at home. The preface of our edition says that at fifteen his studies on conic sections were thought worthy of a hearing among the most scientific men of Paris. He was a mathematician from boyhood, and in later years of pain, the preface says, mathematics alone could calm him.

In the autumn of 1654 came what the same preface calls a second, sudden conversion. One night in November, from about half past ten until about half past midnight, he wrote a short record of what happened to him. It begins, after the date and the hours, with a single word: *Fire*. A servant found it after his death, sewn into a fold of his waistcoat. Before the year was out he had become, for a time, one of the solitaries of Port Royal, an abbey near Paris, and he wrote his *Provincial Letters* in defence of his friends there, in a long quarrel between two schools of the Church, the Jesuits and the Jansenists. The preface says the Letters put him at once among the great French writers.

## The book

After the Letters he planned a work against atheists and unbelievers: a defence of the Christian religion, addressed to people who doubted it. He never finished it. What remains are the notes: a line, a paragraph or a page at a time, set down in his last years on scraps of paper and in the margins of earlier drafts, often when he was too ill for sustained work. Some were dictated to friends, and some to a servant. One of them says how he meant to go about it: "I shall here write my thoughts without order, yet not perhaps in undesigned confusion, that is true order, which will always denote my object by its very disorder."

The notes reached print in 1670, in a French edition called *Pensées*, which means thoughts. Its editors had edited them freely, and theologians had censored them. Later editors went back to the manuscripts, and because Pascal had not placed many of his fragments, each of them had to use judgement. That is why the order of the *Thoughts* differs between editions, and why our order is only ours. This translation follows the arrangement of the French editor Auguste Molinier (Paris, 1877 to 1879), in two parts: the misery of man without God, and the happiness of man with God.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

It is a book of pieces, which suits a day of pieces: a few minutes on a bench, a rest at noon, an hour in a bunk. It is also a book about the very things a long quiet walk brings up. Why we cannot sit still. What we do with a silence. How a habit holds us up, and how a question holds on. One of the *Thoughts* is a single line: "Rivers are roads which move and carry us whither we wish to go."

## Where it is hard

Pascal is not always an easy companion. He writes at length about prophecy, about the Jewish people, about miracles and about the quarrel between Jesuits and Jansenists. Those pages were written for readers of his own century and need more explaining than a day can hold, so they are not here. They are all in the book for anyone who wants them. The preface also describes his last years as years of austerity, self-denial and alms-giving, and a reader may feel that severity in places. Where a passage is hard, the reflection says so. The translation also uses words of its century, such as *heathen* and *Turks*, and writes *man* and *he* for everyone. We have left the words as they stand in the passages. They are the translation's, and they are not ours.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is by C. Kegan Paul, translated from Molinier's text. The title page reads London, Kegan Paul, Trench & Co., and the date MDCCCLXXXV, which is 1885. The translation is in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 46921, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word. The file also holds a preface, notes and an index by the translator and editors. We quote none of that, only Pascal's own words.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. The old spellings stay: *to-morrow*, *sea-faring*, *starting-point*. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the ten passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours and they are original. Of the ten, six come from the first part, the misery, and four from the second.
