# Day 4. Three steps and a gate

*What would it take to step through a door and not look back?*

## The passage

> Thither did we approach; and the first stair Was marble white, so polished and so smooth, I mirrored myself therein as I appear. The second, tinct of deeper hue than perse, Was of a calcined and uneven stone, Cracked all asunder lengthwise and across. The third, that uppermost rests massively, Porphyry seemed to me, as flaming red As blood that from a vein is spirting forth. … Devoutly at the holy feet I cast me, For mercy’s sake besought that he would open, But first upon my breast three times I smote. Seven P’s upon my forehead he described With the sword’s point, and, “Take heed that thou wash These wounds, when thou shalt be within,” he said. … Then pushed the portals of the sacred door, Exclaiming: “Enter; but I give you warning That forth returns whoever looks behind.”
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto IX (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)
## Reflection

Some thresholds on a road are real: a gate in a wall, a bridge, the sign at the edge of a town. You step through, and the place behind you stops being yours. Others you only notice afterwards, when you realise that somewhere in the last hour you stopped being a person who has just arrived and became a person who is walking.

Dante has been asleep. He wakes on the grass with the sun more than two hours high, frightened, not knowing where he is, and finds that only Virgil is beside him. His guide tells him what happened. At dawn, while Dante slept among the flowers, a lady came who said her name was Lucia. *So will I make his journey easier for him*, she said, and she carried him up the slope while he slept and set him down here. Ahead, where the cliff seems split, is the entrance.

It is worth stopping on that. In a book about climbing, the last stretch before the gate was not climbed by the climber. Some of the way is given. You may have a night's sleep that mends more than you expected, a meal that turns a day, or a stranger who points the right way before you ask. You cannot count on it, and you cannot earn it, and yet it is there in the poem, quietly, just before the gate.

Then the gate itself: a doorway in the rock, with three steps below it and a keeper seated at the top, whose face is more than the poet can bear. In his hand is a naked sword, which throws the sunbeams back so brightly that Dante keeps lifting his eyes in vain. The first step is white marble, polished so smooth that *I mirrored myself therein as I appear*. The second is dark, burnt, uneven stone, cracked right through, lengthwise and across. The third is red as blood from an opened vein. The keeper has his feet on this last, and he sits on a threshold that looks like diamond.

The poem does not say what the steps mean. Many readers have taken them as the stages of a change of heart: first, seeing yourself plainly; next, being broken open; last, a love hot enough to burn. That is one reading, and you may find another. But a walker can use the steps without a theory. The first is a mirror, and the oddest thing about it is the phrase *as I appear*, not worse and not better. The second is the crack: the plan that does not survive the first rain, the blister that tells you what the body cannot do. The third is heat, the thing that made you start.

Dante climbs them with his guide pulling him gently on, and kneels, and strikes his breast three times, and asks for mercy. The keeper takes the point of his sword and writes seven letters on Dante's forehead, each a P. On the usual reading, the P stands for the Latin word for sin, one for each ledge of the mountain, though the passage does not say so. He tells Dante to wash these wounds when he is inside. They are given as a task, not a sentence. Then the keeper takes out two keys, one gold and one silver, and opens the door. He says he has them from Peter, and that Peter told him to err on the side of opening rather than keeping shut, as long as people fall down at his feet.

Notice that the door is more generous than the warning that follows. *Enter; but I give you warning That forth returns whoever looks behind.* It is the one line in the passage that sounds like a trap, and we do not want to explain it away. But look at what it says. It does not say that the door locks. It says that whoever looks behind goes out again. On Day 2 the two travellers sat down on a ledge and looked back at the shore, and the poem called it a delight. The gate's warning is a different kind of looking: the glance of regret, the one that wishes you were still on the other side.

On the Camino nobody is forced onward. You can stop, you can take a bus, you can go home, and nobody should shame you for it. The poem is speaking of the heart and not the timetable. The question it puts to a walker is what you are leaving, and whether you can leave it without keeping one foot on the old side.

## For the walk

- What am I leaving at this gate, and can I leave it without a look of regret?
- At the next doorway, gate, bridge or edge of a village, stop for one breath. Name, silently, one thing you are leaving behind: a worry, a plan, a version of yourself. Step through and walk to the next bend before you look back. When you do look, look at the view, not at the thing you left.

## At the end of the day

A day has more doors in it than you notice. The one you went out of this morning, the first step onto the road, the door of the place where you ate, and the door of the place where you are sitting now. Each time you passed one you were a little different on the other side.

You could do what the first step did, and look at yourself. If there is a mirror where you are staying, even a small one over a basin, look into it for a full minute. Not to judge the face, and not to flatter it. Let it show you the day as it appears: the colour, the lines about the eyes, the dust at the hairline. The step in the poem is *so polished and so smooth* that Dante sees himself exactly. See what you can bear to see.

Then the second step, the crack. Something broke or bent today. A plan, a pace, a mood, a hope about how a stage would go. Say what it was. A crack is information, and information is more useful than a sealed surface.

And the third, the red. What was warm today? Something is the reason you set out, and some days you can feel it and some days you cannot. If you could feel it today, give it its due. If not, it has not gone.

One more thing from the passage, the oldest kindness in it. Dante was carried while he slept. Think of what carried you today without your doing: the sleep, a meal that arrived on time, a word from a stranger, a stretch of road that was gentler than the map suggested. You may be too tired to be grateful, and that is all right. It is enough to notice it, as you would a hand under your elbow on a stair. If you looked back with regret at something today, it is also enough to say so, without making a case.

The keeper told Dante to wash his marks once he was inside, and washing is the sort of work that gets done in small amounts, again and again. Tonight's portion might be literal: warm water over tired feet, a face rinsed twice. It might also be one thing to let go of, a sharp word said to someone or a mean thought you had about a stranger. You do not have to rehearse it. Name it, and let the water have it.

Tomorrow will open with a door of its own, the one of the room you are in now. You might decide tonight what you will leave in it. Whatever the evening has taught you, you do not have to carry all of it out.

- When I looked at myself plainly today, what did I see?
- What did I try to leave behind, and how often did I look back at it?
- What carried me today that I did not do for myself?

## A short prayer

*Open the door a little wider than I expect. Let me see myself plainly and not turn away. Wash what needs washing, and do it gently. Carry me where my legs cannot, and let me go on without a look over my shoulder.*
