# Day 1. The shore at dawn

*What changes in your eyes when the long dark is behind you and the road is in front?*

## The passage

> To run o’er better waters hoists its sail The little vessel of my genius now, That leaves behind itself a sea so cruel; … Sweet colour of the oriental sapphire, That was upgathered in the cloudless aspect Of the pure air, as far as the first circle, Unto mine eyes did recommence delight Soon as I issued forth from the dead air, Which had with sadness filled mine eyes and breast. … The dawn was vanquishing the matin hour Which fled before it, so that from afar I recognised the trembling of the sea. Along the solitary plain we went As one who unto the lost road returns, And till he finds it seems to go in vain. … There he begirt me as the other pleased; O marvellous! for even as he culled The humble plant, such it sprang up again Suddenly there where he uprooted it.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto I (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

Before the sun is up, a coast has almost no colour. The sea is a grey sheet, the sky is a paler one, and the road is a line between them that you follow by feel. Then, in the space of a few minutes, something is added. Blue comes first, or a thin pink along the edge of the water, and the sheet turns into the sea. If you are walking when it happens, you may find you have stopped without deciding to.

That is the moment this poem begins with. Dante has just climbed out of Hell, and the first thing he tells us is a colour. It is the deep blue of an eastern sapphire, spread through the clear air as far as the rim of the sky, and with it, he says, delight begins again in his eyes, after the dead air *Which had with sadness filled mine eyes and breast*. He is a long way from any summit. He has only come up onto a beach at daybreak. But the poem treats that as the whole gift of the first morning: to see again.

He does something with the poem itself, too. He says his little vessel now hoists its sail to run over better waters, and leaves behind a sea so cruel. The cruel sea is the first part of the poem. This part is a voyage upward, and it starts from a real shore in real light.

At the water's edge someone is waiting: an old man alone, with a long beard streaked with white and a face so lit by four stars that he seems to have the sun in front of him. He challenges the two strangers. Who are they, and who has led them out of the eternal prison? Virgil, the guide, makes the younger man bow with his hand and his words, and answers for them both. This one, he says, is looking for liberty, *which is so dear*, as the old man knows, because he gave up his life for it at Utica. Readers know him as Cato.

Then the old man hands out the first work of the climb, and it is small. Go, he says, and gird this one about with a smooth rush, and wash his face, so that every stain is cleaned away. An eye clouded by any mist should not go before the first angel. Rushes grow along the foot of the island, where the waves strike. No other plant that puts out leaf or hardens can live there, he says, because it does not give way to the shocks. Where the water hits, only the plant that bends survives.

So the climb begins with a wash and a belt of reed. Not armour, and not a plan. The passage ends on the quietest detail of all: Dante picks the humble plant, and it grows again *Suddenly there where he uprooted it*.

What would it ask of you to begin like that? Most beginnings carry some show: the new boots, the early start, the story you will tell about starting. The poem begins with a washed face and a stalk of something that bends. You might take the hint at six in the morning. Wash, if there is water to hand. And let your first plan be a reed rather than a wall, something that gives when the road pushes and is still standing when the road is done.

Two things are worth saying plainly. The first is that Dante's dawn is a gift. He has not earned that sapphire; he has been brought here. Not every first morning will look like it, and the poem does not pretend so. Straight after the blue come a stern old man and a long empty plain. Look at the third stretch of our passage, where he and his guide go along the solitary plain *As one who unto the lost road returns, And till he finds it seems to go in vain*. That may be the more honest picture of the first days. You are on the right road and cannot yet tell, and until something confirms it, the walking feels like wandering. If that is your morning, you are not failing the poem. It has a line for you.

The second is smaller. Nothing here promises that the second morning will be like the first, and the poem does not promise it either. It promises only what is in the passage: a first light, a face to wash, and a plant that grows back. That is enough to start on.

## For the walk

- What is the first thing I can see clearly this morning?
- Somewhere in the first hour, stop and wash your face if there is water to hand, a tap, a fountain or your bottle. If there is not, wipe it with your hand and breathe. Then say aloud, if nobody is near, the colour of the sky or the sea. That is all. Let that colour be your whole reading for a minute, and carry the question for the hour.

## At the end of the day

The sapphire is a long way back now. Whatever colour the sky had this morning, the day has used it up, and you are sitting somewhere with your shoes off, most likely, and your feet making their first report.

Let the day come back in the order it happened. The start: did you wash, or stop, or look about you? Or was it a scramble of straps and zips and a worry about the time? Neither is a failure. It is only worth knowing how you began.

Then think of the middle of the day, the stretch the poem calls the solitary plain. Most days have one: a long straight, a road beside traffic, an hour when nothing happens and you quietly doubt that you are in the right place. Was there such an hour today, and what did you do with it? You may simply have walked on, and that counts. The man in the poem does no more. He goes on, and it only seems in vain until he finds the road.

After that, the reed. Something had to bend today. A plan changed, a stage ended sooner or later than you meant, someone walked faster or slower than you, a bed was not the bed you hoped for. The small things are the ones we resist hardest. Did you give way, or stiffen? There is no right answer tonight. It is enough to know which you did. If you gave way even once, however small, that was the plant doing what it is for.

A word about the first evening of a long road. It is often the strangest. Nothing is where it should be, and you are tired in places you did not know you had. It is easy to read that as a verdict, that you are not up to it or have made a mistake. More likely it is only a first evening, and the body is reporting on a job it has not done before. Give it a night before you judge it. If your feet are sore, see to them before anything else, and if anything worries you beyond ordinary soreness, ask a pharmacist or a doctor.

The washing is worth repeating tonight. It was the first task the old man set, and it takes half a minute: face, hands, the back of the neck. Let the dust of the road go with the water. Whatever is not dust will still be there in the morning, and you can meet it then, rested. It is a way of closing the day as the poem opened it.

And if the sky is clear and it is safe to step outside, look up for a minute before bed. You do not need to know the name of a single star. The poem will end on that word, and it is a good one to fall asleep under.

- What did I see clearly today that I would have missed in a hurry?
- When did the road feel like wandering, and what did I do then?
- What had to bend today, and did I let it?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the light that came back this morning. Wash from my face and my mind whatever I do not need to carry. Teach me to bend where I would break. Let me sleep tonight, and begin again tomorrow.*
