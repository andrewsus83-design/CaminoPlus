# Day 5. The weight on the back

*How much of what you carry is for the road, and how much is for show?*

## The passage

> “The grievous quality Of this their torment bows them so to earth, That my own eyes at first contended with it; But look there fixedly, and disentangle By sight what cometh underneath those stones; Already canst thou see how each is stricken.” … As to sustain a ceiling or a roof, In place of corbel, oftentimes a figure Is seen to join its knees unto its breast, Which makes of the unreal real anguish Arise in him who sees it, fashioned thus Beheld I those, when I had ta’en good heed. True is it, they were more or less bent down, According as they more or less were laden; And he who had most patience in his looks Weeping did seem to say, “I can no more!”
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto X (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

Lift your pack at six in the morning and it tells you the truth about yesterday's packing. The straps bite where they bit before. Something in there you have not touched since you set out is still riding on your hips. You may find yourself weighing every item in your mind, the book, the second pair of shoes, the thing you brought just in case, and deciding what you could do without.

The first ledge of the mountain is the ledge of pride, and its picture is a weight. The two travellers come out of a narrow crack in the rock onto a wide shelf, a plain *more desolate than roads across the deserts*, with a wall of white marble on the inner side. The wall is carved with scenes so alive that Dante thinks the angel of the Annunciation is about to say his greeting. There is a king dancing in front of the holy ark, and his wife watching from a palace window with scorn. And there is an emperor, Trajan, riding out with his cavalry, who is stopped by a poor widow standing at his bridle. Her son has been killed, and she wants justice. He tells her to wait until he returns. And if he does not return? she asks. Whoever sits in his place will do it, he says. What good is another's good deed to him, she says, if he neglects his own? And the emperor tells her to take comfort: he must do his duty before he moves, because justice wills it, and pity holds him back.

It is a curious image for the ledge of the proud. The great man who stops. Each of the three scenes shows someone who put their own importance down for a moment, and the carvings run along the wall where the souls will pass.

The souls themselves do not appear until Dante has looked his fill. Then he sees something coming towards them that does not look like people, and asks what it is. The torment of these figures bows them so low to the earth, Virgil answers, that his own eyes at first argued with the sight. Look hard, and *disentangle By sight what cometh underneath those stones*. They are not strange creatures. They are people carrying boulders, and each is bent according to the size of the stone. The poet compares them to the carved figures that hold up a roof or a ceiling in some old building, with knees drawn to the chest. Such a figure, he says, makes an unreal pain real in whoever watches it. And then comes the line that stays with the reader: *he who had most patience in his looks Weeping did seem to say, “I can no more!”*

The one who says it is the most patient. The strongest person on the ledge, the one who has borne it best, is at the end of what he can do. The poem gives no help to the story that toughness is the answer.

What is pride, on a road? It is not only arrogance. It is the *I'm fine* when you are not, the refusal of a lift of the arm or a plaster, the extra kilometre taken so as not to be the one who stopped, the carrying of more than you need so that nobody will think you unprepared. It is heavy for a simple reason: the effort of keeping up an appearance is a weight, and it bends you away from what is in front of you, which on this ledge is the ground. And notice that the stones come in different sizes. Each person is bent *according as they more or less were laden*. Nobody else can tell how heavy your load is. That is a good reason not to guess at theirs.

Two things need saying. First, the picture is severe, and we do not want to hide that. A reader may well flinch at stones as a penalty. One way to read it is as the image of what pride already is, and not as a punishment added from outside: a heavy thing, slow to bear, that makes it hard to see anyone else. Second, there is a good kind of pride, in a day well walked or a hard job done, and the poem is not speaking of that. Its pride is the other kind, the one that needs to be above someone.

Two small practices come out of the wall and the stones. Ask for one small help, or accept one that is offered, without a speech. And stop for one person who stands in your way, as the emperor stopped for the widow. You will not carry less because of it. You may well stand straighter.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying today in order to look strong?
- Ask for one small help or accept one that is offered: directions, a hand with a strap, a mouthful of water. Say thank you, and leave out the excuse. And if someone speaks to you while you are hurrying, stop for as long as the conversation lasts. Afterwards, notice whether your shoulders have dropped.

## At the end of the day

Shoes off, straps off. Whatever the stones were today, you have put them down for the night, and the first thing the body does is tell you how much it carried. Let it. Roll your shoulders, loosen your hips, and give the places where the pack sat a chance to breathe. If the straps have rubbed anything raw, look at it: clean it, dry it, cover it. If it is more than a rub, ask at a pharmacy.

Then look back for the *I'm fine*. Most of us said it at least once. To a person who offered something, to a companion who asked how the feet were, to ourselves at the foot of a hill. It is such a small sentence. Where did it come out of your mouth today, and what was it protecting? You do not need to scold yourself. Pride is common and it is tiring, and the first step towards putting it down is to notice where it sits.

Sort the load, too, in the mind if not yet in the pack. Some of what you carry today is the body's real need: water, a layer for the cold, a plaster. Some of it is not. It may be an item you took out and put back three times because you could not bring yourself to leave it. It may be an idea of how fast you ought to be, or a picture of how you look. Which weight was real? You do not have to decide tonight, and it is no moment for big choices when you are tired. A note to yourself is enough: that one, tomorrow, I will not carry.

There is a sentence in the passage that is not only for the proud. *I can no more.* At nine o'clock at night it is simply an accurate report, and the answer to it is sleep, not a pep talk. If you said it today, even to yourself, you were not failing at the road. You were telling the truth about a body that has done a day's work.

Look round the room, too, if you are among other people. Everyone has put their stones down, and no two loads were the same. You cannot see the size of another person's day, only its effect: the way someone sits, or the care with which they take off a boot. If somebody near you looks more bent than you would expect, a small kindness costs little. A plaster, a glass of water, a seat moved along a bench.

Think, finally, of the person who stopped you today, or the one you stopped for. A greeting, a question, a person who needed a hand with a map. Was it a nuisance or was it the best part of the day? The emperor in the carving is not made smaller by the widow.

- Where did I say I was fine today when I was not?
- What am I carrying that is for the road, and what is only for show?
- Who stopped for me today, or whom did I stop for?

## A short prayer

*Let me put down what was never mine to carry. Teach me to ask, and to take what is offered without a speech. Make my back honest and my load small. Let whoever is bent beside me be lifted too.*
