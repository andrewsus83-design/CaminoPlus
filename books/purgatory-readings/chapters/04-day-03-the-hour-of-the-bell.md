# Day 3. The hour of the bell

*What does the evening do to a heart that has left home?*

## The passage

> ’Twas now the hour that turneth back desire In those who sail the sea, and melts the heart, The day they’ve said to their sweet friends farewell, And the new pilgrim penetrates with love, If he doth hear from far away a bell That seemeth to deplore the dying day, When I began to make of no avail My hearing, and to watch one of the souls Uprisen, that begged attention with its hand. It joined and lifted upward both its palms, Fixing its eyes upon the orient, As if it said to God, “Naught else I care for.” “Te lucis ante” so devoutly issued Forth from its mouth, and with such dulcet notes, It made me issue forth from my own mind. And then the others, sweetly and devoutly, Accompanied it through all the hymn entire, Having their eyes on the supernal wheels.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto VIII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)
## Reflection

Late in the afternoon, a walking day changes its mind. The morning's resolve has been spent, the pack has been on for hours, and the end of the stage is near enough to think about but not near enough to touch. It is the hour when a mind with nothing to do turns for home: a kitchen, a voice, a particular chair. An hour ago you were not thinking of it, and now you can think of nothing else.

Dante knew that hour and wrote one of the best-loved sentences in the poem about it. It was the hour, he says, that turns back the desire of those who sail the sea, and melts their hearts on the day they have said farewell to their sweet friends. It is the hour that pierces the new pilgrim with love, if he hears from far away a bell that seems to grieve over the dying day. Two travellers are named: the sailor on his first day out, and the new pilgrim. Both have left something, and dusk shows them how much.

The word that matters is *new*. Not a pilgrim of years, but one in the first days, before the road has become a home. And the poem does not call the feeling a weakness or a fault. It does not tell him to ignore it. It says only that this is the hour: it comes at a certain time of day to those who have set out, and a bell from far away is enough to start it. You may never learn who is ringing it, or for what. It reaches you anyway.

What happens next is the part we hardly ever quote. Dante is in a small flowery valley at dusk, among a company of souls, and the poem turns from his own heart to theirs. One of them rises, joins his palms and lifts them, fixes his eyes on the east, and says to God, in words the poet puts in quotation marks, *Naught else I care for*. Then he sings an evening hymn, which the poem names only by its first Latin words, *Te lucis ante*, so devoutly and with such sweet notes that Dante is carried out of his own mind. The others join in, sweetly and devoutly, through the whole hymn, with their eyes on the heavens above them. Afterwards they look up in silence, *pale and humble*, as if waiting. Later in the canto two angels come down to guard the valley against a serpent that will arrive at night. Evening has its dangers, and the poem posts a watch.

It is a small, brave thing. At the very hour that melts the heart, someone stands up and sings, and the others follow.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? First, to know the hour. If you know that the late afternoon is when the mind goes home, the feeling is less alarming. It is not a sign that the walk is wrong, or that you are weaker than the others, who may well be feeling it too. Second, to let the heart melt a little on purpose, and to name who is in it. Third, to give the feeling something to do. In the poem it is a hymn, sung together. For someone else it could be a message home, a pot of tea, a look at the sky, or three minutes of silence. Longing wants a shape. Without one it tends to sit in the chest and grow.

There are two honest doubts. The first is that a hymn is not everyone's answer. We are not asking you to sing one, and nothing in this book depends on it. The point carries over to any kind of ending of the day: do something with your hands or your voice or your attention that says the day is closing, and say it in company if you can. The second doubt runs the other way. A bell can start a spiral. The hour at the end of a stage is a poor time to decide whether to go on, because tiredness and longing make a very persuasive pair. Decide that after food and a night's sleep, when the light is better and the same question weighs less.

And *Naught else I care for* is a splendid sentence, though perhaps not one to say aloud at six in the evening to your blistered feet. It is a direction more than a report. The soul who says it has put everything down but one thing, and the day that is ending is what put it down for him.

You are reading this in the morning, and the hour is a long way off. Put it in your pocket, and let it be there when the bell rings.

## For the walk

- When the pull of home comes this afternoon, what will I do with it?
- Notice the first time today that your mind turns for home. Say silently who or what it is. Walk three minutes with that person or place in mind, and then bring your attention back to the road. If you hear a bell, stop for as long as it rings, wherever you are, and let it be the whole of what you do.

## At the end of the day

If the bell hour came today, you have probably already met it, and if it did not, you were lucky or busy. Either way, you are now at the end of the day, which in this poem is the time when a whole company sings together.

Start with what the hour held. When did your mind turn for home, and what was in it? Perhaps a person, perhaps a room, perhaps something small, like the light through a particular window or the sound of a kettle. Notice that it was probably not what you would have guessed. Longing is a poor liar. It tells you quickly what you are carrying.

Then ask what you did with it. Did you push it down and walk faster? Did you sink into it and stop seeing the road? Or did you hold it for a few steps, as the poem's sailor and pilgrim hold it, and let it be true? Nobody gets this right the first time. The aim is only to notice the shape of your own habit, so that tomorrow's bell is a little less of a surprise.

Homesickness is a hard word, and not an accurate one. What you felt was love with a distance in it. That is not a flaw in the walking. It is evidence that there is something to go home to, and for some people that is the quiet news of the day. If there is nothing at home that pulls at you, that is true too, and worth a gentle thought. Someone might have been missing you all the same. Perhaps tonight is a good night for a short message, or a few lines in a notebook for someone who will read them later.

After the singing, the souls in the valley looked up in silence and waited. You can do something like that before you sleep. Sit for five minutes with nothing to read and nothing to do, and let the day settle. You may be too tired to feel anything much, and that is allowed. Strong feelings are louder when you are weary, and the same thought at noon would have weighed less.

The poem also posted a watch over its valley, so it is fair to ask what yours might be. The thing that comes at the end of a day is rarely a serpent. It is smaller: an old argument replayed, a worry about tomorrow built out of nothing, an hour lost to a screen. You might decide tonight, in advance, which of those you will not let in. A short list is enough, and it is easier to keep when you wrote it while you were calm.

One practical thing: deal with the small jobs first, such as socks, water and the phone on its charger. A tired mind that has things to do is less easily carried off by a bell.

- When did my mind turn for home today, and what did it hold?
- What did I do with that feeling, and what could I do with it tomorrow?
- Who might like to hear from me, and what would I say to them in one line?

## A short prayer

*For the ones I miss, thank you. Keep them well tonight, and keep me honest about what I feel. Let the bell at the end of the day be a kindness and not a wound. Give me a quiet mind and an easy sleep.*
