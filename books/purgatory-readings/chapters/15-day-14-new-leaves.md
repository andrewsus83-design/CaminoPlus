# Day 14. New leaves on an old tree

*What will I leave at the first river, what will I take from the second, and what will I carry home?*

## The passage

> In front of them the Tigris and Euphrates Methought I saw forth issue from one fountain, And slowly part, like friends, from one another. … I will too, if not written, at least painted, Thou bear it back within thee, for the reason That cinct with palm the pilgrim’s staff is borne. … If, Reader, I possessed a longer space For writing it, I yet would sing in part Of the sweet draught that ne’er would satiate me; But inasmuch as full are all the leaves Made ready for this second canticle, The curb of art no farther lets me go. From the most holy water I returned Regenerate, in the manner of new trees That are renewed with a new foliage, Pure and disposed to mount unto the stars.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto XXXIII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)
## Reflection

Roads end in ordinary places. If yours finishes in Santiago de Compostela, as the Norte does, you will arrive in a city where ordinary life is going on around you, and you will be a little dusty and a great deal tired, with a whole month's worth of feeling and nowhere particular to put it. If you are reading at home, the equivalent is closing a book. Either way, the question of the last day is the same. What goes with you, and what stays?

The poem's last canto answers in pictures. Dante and Beatrice and the others come to a shady place at the edge of the garden, where two streams rise from a single spring and *slowly part, like friends, from one another*. He has been told about these waters already. One, called Lethe, takes away all memory of wrongdoing. The other, called Eunoe, gives back the memory of every good deed done. He has already drunk from the first. Now Beatrice tells the lady of the garden to lead him to the second and revive what is half-dead in him.

Hold that picture of the rivers. One source, two directions. A thing that parts slowly, like friends. It may be the best image in the poem for the last day of a pilgrimage, because it is true of the people you have walked with. You came along the same stretch for a while, and now you will go different ways, and nobody has to be wrenched apart. The poem's friends part slowly. You can let yours.

Then Beatrice does something unexpected. She has been speaking in riddles for a good while, and she tells Dante to take what he has heard back with him. If it cannot be written, she says, then at least painted: *I will too, if not written, at least painted, Thou bear it back within thee, for the reason That cinct with palm the pilgrim's staff is borne.* The pilgrim who comes home brings his staff wound round with palm, as a sign of where he has been. The Way has its own token, the scallop shell, which many people wear or carry on a pack. The poem's point is wider than a keepsake. She does not ask for an essay. A picture will do, one image that holds the whole, brought back inside you.

After that, the poet does something rarer. He stops. If he had more space for writing, he says, he would sing a little of the sweet draught that never leaves him satisfied. But the leaves made ready for this canticle are full, and *The curb of art no farther lets me go.* He stops because he has run out of pages, not because he has run out of things to say. A road ends the same way, at the measure it was given and not at the point where wanting stops. You may find that you want to go on, and that is the sign of a good road, and not a failure of the walking.

And then the final sentence: *From the most holy water I returned Regenerate, in the manner of new trees That are renewed with a new foliage, Pure and disposed to mount unto the stars.*

Read it slowly. He returned, like a tree. A tree is not a new tree each spring. It is the same one with new leaves. That may be the most truthful thing in the poem for a person coming home. You are not replaced. You are renewed, and a good deal of what is old in you is still standing. And he is *disposed* to mount, which is a long way short of having arrived. The poem ends with a readiness, not an arrival. And the word it ends on is *stars*, the same sky that was over the shore on the first morning, when four of them stood over the old man at the water's edge.

Three honest doubts. The first is that nobody finishes a road *pure*. A walker comes in with blisters and mixed feelings, and some of what was heavy on Day 1 is still heavy on Day 14. The poem's claim is the poem's own. You are allowed to read it as a wish, and a good one. The second is that the two rivers do not ask you to forget everything. The first washes away what is bad, and the second keeps what is good. If the good fades first, as it can, the second river is the one to look for. And the third is that the last day may be flat. Nobody has said it will arrive like the sapphire dawn of the first morning, and if it does not, nothing has gone wrong.

So the day has three small tasks, all of them light. Choose one thing to put in the first river. Choose one good thing to take from the second. And choose one picture to carry home.

## For the walk

- What will I leave at the first river, what will I take from the second, and what picture will I carry home?
- On the last stretch of your walk, or the last quiet minutes of your reading, choose three things: one thing to forget, such as a grudge, a blister or a worry; one good thing to remember; and one image of the road. Say each once, in a sentence, aloud if nobody is near. Then simply walk.

## At the end of the day

This is the last evening of the book, and it may or may not be the last evening of your road. If you are still walking, the poem would not mind your reading this tomorrow, or in a week. If you are at the end, it is the right night to be a little quiet.

Start with the rivers. Is there something you would like to put in the first one? It does not have to be heavy. It may be a small resentment about a bed, or the memory of a day that went wrong, or the picture of yourself at the bottom of a hill. Say what it is, and let the water take it. The poem does not ask for much. It only asks you to be willing.

And the second. Of everything that happened, what do you want to keep? The poem's drink is one that restores the memory of the good that was done, whoever did it. Some of it was yours, and some was other people's: the stranger with the directions, the person who held a door, the host who left a light on. Make a little list, in your head or on paper. These are what you will tell people about, and they are easily lost if you do not collect them tonight.

Then the people. Who have you walked beside, and how will you part? The poem's rivers go slowly, like friends. If there is someone whose company you would like to keep, there may be a way to say so before you go your own ways. If there is someone you simply liked, a word of thanks is a good thing to leave. Parting does not have to be a wound.

And be honest about how you feel. The end of a road, or a book, often brings something other than joy: flatness, a kind of grief, restlessness, sometimes nothing at all. All of those are ordinary. The poem's last line is a readiness, not a fanfare. You have done what you have done, and tomorrow will start without any ceremony.

If you can, go out for a minute and look at the sky. The poem ends on its stars. It is a good place to put your attention, and a good place to lay it down.

- What do I want to leave at the first river, and what do I want to keep from the second?
- Who have I walked beside, and how can I part from them like a friend?
- What one picture of this road would I carry home, even if I could not write it down?

## A short prayer

*For the road, the people and the days, thank you. Take from me what I should forget, and keep for me what was good. Let me go home as the same tree with new leaves. Turn my face to the stars, and let me rest.*
