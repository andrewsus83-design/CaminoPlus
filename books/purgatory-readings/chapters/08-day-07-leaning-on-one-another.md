# Day 7. Leaning on one another

*Who is holding up whom, and who has noticed?*

## The passage

> And even as I asked, behold the third, Saying: “Love those from whom ye have had evil!” … Covered with sackcloth vile they seemed to me, And one sustained the other with his shoulder, And all of them were by the bank sustained. Thus do the blind, in want of livelihood, Stand at the doors of churches asking alms, And one upon another leans his head, So that in others pity soon may rise, Not only at the accent of their words, But at their aspect, which no less implores. … “O brother mine, each one is citizen Of one true city; but thy meaning is, Who may have lived in Italy a pilgrim.”
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto XIII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

Look at a rest stop in the middle of a morning. A low wall in the shade, a row of packs leaning against it, and people leaning against the packs. Nobody has arranged it. Shoulders touch because the wall is only so long and the shade only so wide, and for ten minutes nobody minds. It is one of the plainest sights on any long road, and it is a picture the second ledge of the mountain can make something of.

The ledge of envy is bare. The poem says there is no shade and no carving, only the dull blue-grey of the stone. Virgil stops, uncertain, and does something unexpected for a guide: he turns to the sun and asks it to lead. *Thou warmest the world, thou shinest over it*, he says, and the two travellers cover a mile in no time. Then voices go past them in the air, unseen, calling out examples of generous love. The third of them is a command, and it opens our passage: *Love those from whom ye have had evil!* Virgil tells Dante that this ledge punishes envy, and that the lashes of its whip are made from love.

Then they see the souls. They are sitting against the cliff, in coarse cloaks the colour of the rock, so that at first the stone seems to have people in it. The poet compares them to blind beggars at the doors of churches, asking alms, with one resting his head against another's, so that pity will rise in the passer-by, *Not only at the accent of their words, But at their aspect, which no less implores.* These souls prop each other by the shoulder, and the bank props them all.

They are blind for a reason the poem gives without softening. An iron wire runs through their eyelids and sews them shut, as a hawk's are sewn to keep it still. Dante, passing, is uneasy. He sees them and they cannot see him, and it feels like an outrage, so he turns to Virgil, who knows what he wants before he says it and tells him to speak, and to be brief.

He speaks to them. If any of them is Italian, he asks. And a voice answers, gently, with the sentence we have in our passage: *O brother mine, each one is citizen Of one true city; but thy meaning is, Who may have lived in Italy a pilgrim.* It is a quiet correction that is also a rebuke to envy. Everyone here belongs to one true city, the voice says. What you mean is someone who lived in Italy as a pilgrim. So a whole human life is a pilgrimage, and a country is only a stretch of the road. In the poem's own faith, the city is heaven. For another reader it may simply mean the one human road.

Dante goes towards the voice, and the soul he finds there, a woman from Siena, goes on to say something terrible about herself, in a few lines we do not quote: that she was happier at another's misfortune than at her own good luck. It is a very exact description of envy. It is not hunger for what the other person has, but a flicker of gladness when they lose it.

On a road, envy is small. It is the person with the lighter pack, the knees that do not hurt, the last bed taken by someone who came in a minute earlier. It is another walker's photographs, or his ease with the language, or the friends he seems to have found already. It lasts a few seconds, and then we are annoyed with ourselves for having felt it. It is embarrassing, and the embarrassment makes it hard to look at.

The poem's answer is odd, and worth sitting with. It does not take the envious and make them stronger, or better at winning. It shuts their eyes, so that they cannot look sideways, and then it makes them lean on each other. The cure for the sideways look is to need someone, and to be seen needing. A group that is propping itself up has little room for a contest.

Two places in this passage are hard. First, the wire is a grim image, and a reader may reasonably think it too much. We would say only that the poem's pictures of punishment are mirrors of the fault: the eyes that were always looking at others are now closed. Second, *Love those from whom ye have had evil* is a hard command. It is not advice on forgiving someone who has asked. It is a voice from the air, and the poem does not pretend it is easy. On a road it may be small: the person who pushed in at the water tap, or snored all night. You are not asked to feel anything. You are asked, perhaps, to wish them well once.

The day's task, then, is to lean and to be leant on. To take the shoulder that is offered, and to be one. And when the sideways look comes, to answer it with a small, silent good wish for the person you are looking at.

## For the walk

- Whose good luck do I find hard to watch today?
- When envy twitches, give that person one silent good wish, in a sentence of your own, and mean it as much as you can. If you are able, do something small for someone: hold a gate, share a map, pass the water. Notice whether the twitch has gone, and do it again the next time it comes.

## At the end of the day

There is a place in the poem where a whole ledge of people are propped by the person beside them and by the wall. Tonight, take a moment to find the wall in your own day. Who held you up? It may have been a companion, or a stranger with directions, or a shopkeeper who said something kind. It may have been a bench, or a tree trunk, or the plain fact of a road that other people have walked before you.

And who did you hold up? Almost everyone has been somebody's shoulder today without knowing it: a word at the right moment, a share of something, a place made on a bench. You do not need a list. It is enough to know it is true.

Now the sideways look. Did you meet it today? It is worth looking at it calmly now, at the end of the day, because it is smaller than it seemed. Think of one moment when you envied someone. Look at what it was really about. The envy is often a pointer, and it points less to the person than to something in you that wants attention. The lighter pack points to a wish for ease. The faster pace points to a fear of being less able than you hoped. Once you see that, you can be kinder to both people, the one you envied and the one who felt it.

There is something else the poem notices, in Dante's own shame. He walks past people who cannot see him, and he finds it awkward to look at them. It is a good instinct. Today, did you look at anyone who did not know it: a stranger asleep on a bench, someone limping, a person talking quietly into a phone? There is a courtesy in looking away. There is another in looking, and seeing whether a hand is needed.

Think, last of all, of the people in the room tonight, or at the next table. They are all on their way to the same city, and some of them you will never see again. If you can, say one good word to somebody before the light goes out. It costs nothing, and envy is hard to keep up against it.

Turn it round, once. Somebody may have looked at you today with that small twitch: for your lighter boots, your companion, your easy stride, a bed you were lucky to get. It is hard to imagine from the inside, because you know what the day cost you. A little care costs nothing, and the best answer to a sideways look is to be easy to be near.

If envy was loud today, note that it is louder when you are hungry or tired. That is not an excuse, only a fact, and a reason to eat and sleep.

- Whose good luck did I find hard to watch today, and what did it show me that I want?
- Who held me up today, in any way at all?
- Whose shoulder could I be tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*Keep my eyes from the sideways look. Let me lean without shame and be leant on without complaint. Bless the ones I envied today, and give me what I truly need. Make me glad at other people's luck, even if I learn it slowly.*
