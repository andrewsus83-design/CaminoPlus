# Day 9. Quick, quick

*Is the trouble my legs, or the warmth of my love for the road?*

## The passage

> The love of good, remiss In what it should have done, is here restored; Here plied again the ill-belated oar; … Full soon they were upon us, because running Moved onward all that mighty multitude, And two in the advance cried out, lamenting, “Mary in haste unto the mountain ran, And Caesar, that he might subdue Ilerda, Thrust at Marseilles, and then ran into Spain.” “Quick! quick! so that the time may not be lost By little love!” forthwith the others cried, “For ardour in well-doing freshens grace!” … So full of longing are we to move onward, That stay we cannot; therefore pardon us, If thou for churlishness our justice take.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Cantos XVII and XVIII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)
## Reflection

There is a stretch near the end of most walking days that has nothing wrong with it, and is still the hardest. It is the last third of a stage. You are not injured and you are not exhausted. You have simply gone flat. A bench with a view becomes twenty minutes, then forty. The next kilometre does not look like anything you want. The poem has a word for this condition, and it gives a surprising definition of it.

It comes at nightfall on the mountain. Dante and his guide have climbed the stairs to the fourth ledge, and as the sun goes down Dante finds that his legs have simply stopped. *O manhood mine, why dost thou vanish so?* he says to himself, and the stairs end, and they halt like a ship that has reached the shore. Then he asks Virgil what fault is purged here, and Virgil replies, and the answer is the first lines of our passage. The love of good, he says, that was slack in what it should have done is here restored. Here the oar that was taken up too late is plied again.

Notice the definition. Sloth in the poem is not tiredness, and it is not a slow body. It is slack love: a good thing loved too faintly, so that the rowing is put off. The fault is in the warmth, not in the legs.

What follows is nearly comic. A crowd comes running along the ledge, all of them, a great multitude in a hurry. Two at the front cry out as they run, and what they cry are examples of haste. The first is Mary, who in the Gospel goes in a hurry up into the hill country to see her cousin. The second is Caesar, who attacked Marseilles and then ran into Spain to win a war. Behind them, others call: *Quick! quick! so that the time may not be lost By little love!* and then *For ardour in well-doing freshens grace!*

Dante's guide stops them, politely, and asks the way up. One of them calls over his shoulder to follow them and the opening will be found. They are so full of longing to go on, he says, that they cannot stop, and he begs pardon in case it looks like rudeness. And off they go. Behind them, near the end of the crowd, two more shout their warnings, about people who gave up before the end of a long journey and settled for a life without glory.

Look at what drives them. It is not a whip. Their cry is against *little love*, and the reason they run is that they are *So full of longing*. The speed is a symptom, not an order. They go fast because they want to, as someone hurries towards a person who is waiting for them. Hurry here is a sign of warmth.

So the flat patch of an afternoon may be cold love more than tired legs. You have forgotten why you want to be here, or you have never quite said it. The remedy is not a faster pace. It is a reason.

We should say plainly what the passage does not mean. It is not an order to hurry on the Camino. The road is long, and rushing in the first week is one of the quickest ways of failing it. Earlier in the poem the same travellers sit down on a ledge, and it does not object. In the poem, *quick* means willing. Your pace is yours.

Nor is tiredness the same as sloth. A body that has walked for eight hours is rightly spent, and the poem does not rebuke it. Dante himself stops at dusk with his legs, in the poem's phrase, in truce, and Virgil lets him. How do you tell the difference? Here is one test, and it is only one. If someone you loved were waiting at the end of the next kilometre, would you find the legs? If you would, the legs are better than you thought, and the trouble is warmth. If you would not, rest.

There is a last honest doubt. Warmth does not come on request, and the poem knows that, because its runners are given their longing and do not manufacture it. But you can give the walking a reason, and a small reason is enough. A person, a view, a destination, the next village's name, the colour of the sea at the next bend. Plying the oar again does not have to mean pulling hard. A slow step with some love in it may be worth more than a fast one without.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Find one reason for the next kilometre, and say it. And if you must stop, stop for a reason, and stand up for one too.

## For the walk

- Is this tiredness, or has my love for the road gone lukewarm?
- At the first flat patch of the afternoon, give the next kilometre a reason: a person to walk it for, or something particular to look for, a bird, a doorway, the colour of the water. Carry that reason to the end of the kilometre, then choose another, or rest.

## At the end of the day

Somewhere between lunch and the end of the stage there was probably a slump, and tonight is a good time to look at it kindly. When did you go flat, and where were you? Remember the place, if you can. And what happened? Did you stop? Did something move you on: a person, a hill, a bar, a bell, the plain thought of a bed?

The test from this morning is worth trying on the afternoon in hindsight. Was it your legs, or was it your love? Legs give signs of their own: heaviness that does not lift, a particular ache, a trembling. Love gives different ones: boredom, a dull what is the point, a reaching for the phone, a wish for the day to be over. You will come to know your own signs, and it may take a few evenings. Neither is wrong. Only the reading of them is worth getting right.

Before you decide which kind of flat it was, check the dull basics. Had you drunk enough, and eaten since the morning? Was there a damp sock, or a stone in a boot? A great deal of lukewarmness turns out to be a missing glass of water. The poem's definition is a deep one, but the first step in telling love from legs is to feed the legs.

Look for the warm moments, too. The runners in the poem are cheerful in a ragged way, and you may have had a few such moments yourself, even on a flat day. A coffee at the right time, a view that stopped you, a moment of talk, a dog that came to say hello. Note two or three of them. They are the fuel the next day will run on, and they are easy to forget by morning.

Think of anyone whose pace pulled you along today: a companion, or someone a little ahead on the path whom you followed for a while without ever speaking. The runners in the poem carry one another along without a word. Such a person may never know what they did for you. You can thank them in your mind.

Did hurry pass itself off as love today? It is easy to confuse the two. Moving fast to get to the end is not the same as wanting to be on the road. The poem's runners go fast for love. If you went fast for any other reason, you may have missed some of what was there.

And on the days when there was no warmth at all, and you walked on from habit alone, that is allowed. Turning up, again, without ardour, is a form of love of its own. The poem has a place for the quick, and it does not despise those who take their time.

Lay the oar down now. It will be there in the morning.

- When did I go flat today, and was it my legs or my love?
- What gave the next kilometre a reason?
- What, however small, made me glad to be on this road?

## A short prayer

*Warm what has gone cold in me. Give me a reason for the next kilometre, and a slow step with some love in it. Forgive the hours I only waited for the day to end. Let me lay the oar down tonight, and take it up again in the morning.*
