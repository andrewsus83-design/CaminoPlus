# Day 2. Hard at the beginning

*What if the climb is hardest now, and kinder later?*

## The passage

> Spent with fatigue was I, when I began: “O my sweet Father! turn thee and behold How I remain alone, unless thou stay!” “O son,” he said, “up yonder drag thyself,” Pointing me to a terrace somewhat higher, Which on that side encircles all the hill. These words of his so spurred me on, that I Strained every nerve, behind him scrambling up, Until the circle was beneath my feet. Thereon ourselves we seated both of us Turned to the East, from which we had ascended, For all men are delighted to look back. … And he to me: “This mount is such, that ever At the beginning down below ’tis tiresome, And aye the more one climbs, the less it hurts. Therefore, when it shall seem so pleasant to thee, That going up shall be to thee as easy As going down the current in a boat, Then at this pathway’s ending thou wilt be; There to repose thy panting breath expect; No more I answer; and this I know for true.” … And as he finished uttering these words, A voice close by us sounded: “Peradventure Thou wilt have need of sitting down ere that.”
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto IV (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

There is a particular hill on the first days of a long walk. It is not the steepest you will meet. But the legs have not yet learned the work, the pack has not found its place on your back, and the top is out of sight behind a bend. You can stand at the bottom and feel the whole road piled up in that one slope.

Dante is at that point. The passage opens with him *Spent with fatigue*, and he does what tired people do: he asks his guide to stop, because otherwise he will be left behind and alone. Virgil does not make a speech. He points to a ledge a little higher up, the one that circles the whole hill, and tells him to drag himself up as far as that. The words spur Dante so hard that he strains every nerve and scrambles after him until the ledge is under his feet.

The guide's move is neat. He does not offer the summit, which is out of sight and would be crushing. He offers the next ledge, which is near enough to reach.

Then they sit, facing east, back the way they came. The poem gives its reason in a short line: *For all men are delighted to look back.* Walkers know this at once. You reach a bend and turn, and there is the place you slept, small and level and already part of a story. It is one of the few free pleasures of a climb, and Virgil, who hates to waste time, allows it.

Dante then asks what every tired person asks: how far is it? And Virgil gives the best-known promise in the poem. The mountain is made so, he says, that at the beginning, down below, it is tiring, and the more one climbs, the less it hurts. When going up feels as easy as going down a river in a boat, you will be at the end of this path, and there you can rest your panting breath. Then, a little drily: no more answer, but he knows it to be true.

Virgil gives no distance. He gives a rule and a sign. You will know it by how it feels, and that is all he says. It is an honest kind of encouragement. It does not say that the end is near. It says that the effort changes.

Dante is not left to enjoy it in peace. A voice close by says, *Peradventure Thou wilt have need of sitting down ere that.* You will probably want to sit before then. It comes from behind a great rock they had not noticed, where some people stand in the shade as if through idleness. One of them, who looks worn out, is sitting with his arms round his knees and his face down between them. Dante knows him, and calls him Belacqua, and a little laughter comes to his lips at the slumped figure. He asks whether the man is waiting for an escort or has fallen back into his old habit. The answer is a question that the whole poem has to face: *O brother, what's the use of climbing?*

Two things stand side by side here, and the poem lets both stand. The mountain gets easier, and a man who has settled into the shade has a point, because climbing is hard and nobody is making him. Virgil does not sneer at him, and he does not stay. Dante bends near and speaks with something like affection, and then goes on. Resting is not the fault. The two travellers have just sat down themselves. What the poem notices in the figure behind the rock is the settling: knees drawn up, face hidden, a question that has quietly become an answer.

What does this ask of an ordinary day? Three small things. Choose the nearer ledge. When the top is out of sight, pick the next bend, the next tree, the next waymark, and let that be the whole task. Turn round at the top of it and look back. And sit down on purpose, for a few minutes, so that you are not caught by a rock and a stranger's voice.

We cannot promise the road will ease. Hills do not read poetry, and a road of hills does not promise to flatten. What the poem claims is about the climber. You may find that the first hill is the one that feels like the whole road, and that a hill in the second week is one you almost forget. That is a hope, not a rule, and the body keeps its own timetable. A sore body is also not the same as an injured one. Pain that sharpens, swells or does not ease is a matter for a pharmacist or a doctor, not for poetry. In an emergency, call 112.

## For the walk

- Which ledge, rather than the summit, is my next one?
- When the top is out of sight, choose something near, a bend, a tree, a waymark, and walk to it as if it were the whole task. When you reach it, turn round and look back for three breaths before going on. Do this three times in the next hour. If you need to sit down, sit on purpose and give it a count of a hundred, then stand.

## At the end of the day

The second evening has its own character. The novelty of the first has worn off, and the stiffness that was waiting for you has arrived. Nothing is wrong. It is only the hill, now in the body.

Tonight you can do what the two travellers did on their ledge: sit and look back. Try it in the mind. Start from where you are, and run the day backwards, bed first, then the last stretch, then the middle, then the early morning, as if you had turned round at the top of a slope. Pick out three places. They need not be beautiful. A bar, a field edge, a bend in a lane will do. Notice how small the morning looks from here, and how much of it is already behind you. If you have a map or a list of the stages, run a finger along the stretch you walked today and then along tomorrow's. The first is solid, drawn by your own legs. The second is only a line. It is a quiet pleasure to watch the ground turn from one into the other.

Then think about the hills. There was probably one that looked like the whole road while you stood below it. How did it go? Did you give yourself a nearer goal, or keep your eyes on the top? And was there a moment like the one behind the rock, a point when you wanted to stay where you were and let the day go on without you? Be fair to it. A rest is a good thing, and the poem does not mock it. Tonight is a good time to tell which kind you took. The kind you take is short and has a reason, and afterwards you stand up. The kind that takes you lasts longer than you meant and has no reason except that standing up is hard. Neither is a sin. Only the second is worth knowing about.

If today was easier than yesterday, be glad of it without making it a promise for tomorrow. If it was harder, that is not a bad sign. Bodies adapt at their own speed, and nobody can set it for them.

There is one more thing to do with the question from behind the rock, *what's the use of climbing?* Tonight it may sound less like Belacqua's voice and more like your own. Give it an honest answer, in a sentence, for nobody but yourself. You may not have a reason yet. That is allowed, and a reason can arrive after the start. If you do have one, say it once, quietly, and let it be among the last things you think of before you sleep. If you do not, let the question stay open. It will keep until morning, and it is not a verdict on the day.

So let the legs be heavy on the mattress. Heavy legs are what the day was for. Drink some water, eat something, and let sleep be the last ledge of the day.

- Which near ledge got me through today, when the top was out of sight?
- When I turned and looked back, what did I see?
- Was my rest today a pause or a settling, and how can I tell?

## A short prayer

*Be near me on the steep part. Give me a ledge I can reach, and the sense to sit when I should. Let my legs be heavy and my sleep be deep. In the morning, let me stand up without being asked twice.*
