# About the book and its author

## The poet

Dante Alighieri was born in Florence in 1265 and died in 1321. He was a poet, and he also took part in the public life of his city. In 1302 Florence sent him into exile, and he did not go home again. He wrote his great poem during those years of exile, over roughly the last dozen or so years of his life. The file we quote from gives only his name, so these few facts are general knowledge. We say "roughly" where a figure is not exact, and we have not tried to say more than we know.

## The poem

The poem's own title was simply the *Comedy*; later readers added *Divine*. It has three parts: the *Inferno*, the *Purgatorio* and the *Paradiso*. In each, the poet is the traveller, so the "I" in the passages is Dante's. In the first two he has a guide, the Roman poet Virgil, who also wrote the *Aeneid*. In the Italian, each of the three parts ends on the same word, *stelle*, which means stars. The *Purgatorio* does, as you will see on the last day.

This book uses only the middle part. It has thirty-three cantos, which is what the poem calls its chapters, and it begins just after the first part ends: the poet and his guide have come up out of the dark onto a shore, and in front of them stands a mountain. From the shore and the foot of the slope, they climb to a gate, then up seven ledges round the mountain, then to a garden at the top. The poem says they can climb only by day. You do not need to have read the first part. Everything you need is said in the day you are reading.

The poem belongs to the medieval Catholic imagination, and the mountain is its picture of purgatory, a place where souls headed for heaven are made ready. That belief is the poem's own, and we neither explain it nor argue for it. We read the poem as a pilgrim's climb, and we keep to what it shows.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

It is a poem about walking. It has stages and a guide, a hard first stretch and a kinder one later, a night when no one can go on, and a goal that is a long way off and then, quite suddenly, is not. It keeps comparing itself to pilgrims: the word *pilgrim* turns up in five places in this translation, and every one of the five is in a passage in this book. The poet also admits, in his own voice, that he is tired, afraid and slow to understand, which is good company for a long road.

It is a hard poem too, and a poem of the fourteenth century. It is full of Dante's neighbours and enemies, and in places it is severe about what people have done. The politics we have left out. Some of the punishments are strange, and where a passage is strange we say so, and leave you to make up your own mind. We chose the passages that speak most plainly to someone on a road.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, the American poet, who died in 1882. His translation of the whole poem was published in 1867; that date is general knowledge, since the file does not state a year. He wrote in unrhymed English lines, in groups of three. Because he died long ago, the translation is in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 1002, under the title *Divine Comedy, Longfellow's Translation, Purgatory*, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

The file begins with a list of contents with a short summary of each canto. We have not used any of it. We quote only the poem itself, and none of the file's other matter.

What we changed in the passages is nothing, with three small exceptions. The lines of verse are joined with a space, so the line breaks are not shown. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. And where a speech goes on past the edge of our passage, we cut at a full stop and leave out any quotation mark that opens or closes beyond it, so that none is left hanging. The old spellings and endings are kept as they are. The fourteen passages come from sixteen of the thirty-three cantos. The choice of the passages and their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original.
