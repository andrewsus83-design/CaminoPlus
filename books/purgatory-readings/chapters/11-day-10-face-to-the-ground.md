# Day 10. Face to the ground

*What are my eyes fastened on, and what am I missing while they are?*

## The passage

> On the fifth circle when I had come forth, People I saw upon it who were weeping, Stretched prone upon the ground, all downward turned. “Adhaesit pavimento anima mea,” I heard them say with sighings so profound, That hardly could the words be understood. … Even as our eye did not uplift itself Aloft, being fastened upon earthly things, So justice here has merged it in the earth. … I on my knees had fallen, and wished to speak; But even as I began, and he was ’ware, Only by listening, of my reverence, “What cause,” he said, “has downward bent thee thus?” And I to him: “For your own dignity, Standing, my conscience stung me with remorse.” “Straighten thy legs, and upward raise thee, brother,” … Err not, fellow-servant am I With thee and with the others to one power.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Canto XIX (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)
## Reflection

On a rough track you have to look at your feet. The stones are loose, the roots are waiting, and a twisted ankle is a long way from anywhere. Looking down is simply good sense, and a day's walking is mostly done that way. But there is another kind of looking down, which has nothing to do with the path. It happens when the eyes are fixed on the pack, the phone, the count of kilometres, the cost of the next bed, the thing you wish you had bought. It is easy to spend a whole afternoon there, and to arrive with no memory of the hills.

The fifth ledge of the mountain is built on that kind of looking. Dante and Virgil climb up onto it and find people lying flat on the ground, face down, weeping. They are saying a line in Latin from the Psalms, *Adhaesit pavimento anima mea*, which means roughly, my soul clings to the ground, and they sigh so deeply that the words can hardly be made out. When Virgil asks the way, they tell him to keep to the outside. Dante goes up to one of them, and the voice that answers is an old one.

He says, in Latin, that he was a successor of Peter, which is to say a pope. He held the office for a little more than a month, and in that time he found out how heavy the great cloak is on whoever keeps it out of the mud. Every other burden, he says, seems a feather beside it. And he discovered that life is a lie: that there was nothing further to climb, and that the heart is not at rest there. Until then, he says, he had been a wretched soul, parted from God and wholly avaricious.

Then comes the explanation of the ledge, and our passage gives its first lines. Even as our eye did not lift itself, being fastened on earthly things, so justice here has pressed it into the earth. A little later he adds that, because avarice had put out our love for every good, so that action was lost, justice now holds them bound by the feet and hands, until it pleases the Lord to let them go. The punishment is the habit made visible. They looked at the ground all their lives, and now they look at nothing else.

Notice one phrase: *whereby was action lost.* In the poem, avarice does more than make a person grasping. It stops them. Someone who clutches things gets nothing done with them. The hand that closes around a possession cannot be used to do anything else, and the same is true of the eyes.

Then comes the part of the story that moves the reader. Dante, who is alive and who has just heard a pope, falls on his knees. He wants to speak, but before he can, the soul hears his reverence by listening alone and asks him why he bends. Dante tells him: because of your dignity, my conscience stung me for standing. The reply is the line this day turns on. *Straighten thy legs, and upward raise thee, brother.* And then: do not make the mistake. He is a fellow-servant, he says, with Dante and with the rest.

A man who held one of the highest offices of his world, flat on his face and bound hand and foot, tells a living stranger to stand up. There is no rank in this place, the poem suggests, and he has learned what it was worth.

What might this mean for a walker? The road is full of things to hold: the gear, the phone, the money, the bed. You need them, and the poem is not against bread or boots or a careful count of what you can afford. It is against being bound. A useful way to tell the difference is to ask whether what you hold is serving the walking or the walking is serving it. And the pope's words are a gift to anyone who has felt small beside someone more experienced, better equipped or simply more confident. The person in the next bunk is a fellow-traveller. So are you.

Three doubts. The picture of lying bound and face down is a stark one, and some readers will find it harsh. We read it as the poem's way of showing the habit, not as a verdict on the people who have it. The second is that counting is not always greed. A walker who budgets carefully is looking after themselves and the people at home. The line lies between counting to live and living to count. The third is that the poet is blunt about rank and about the Church of his own day, and we leave that politics alone. What matters here is a man who learned to say *fellow-servant*.

The poem's remedy is simple. Look up. Stand. And treat the next person as an equal.

## For the walk

- What is my gaze fastened on right now?
- Each time you pass a bench, a gate or a bridge, lift your eyes to the farthest thing you can see and hold them there for three breaths. Once today, greet someone as an equal: look at them, say good morning in whatever language you have, and ask them for nothing.

## At the end of the day

Evening is when we count. The kilometres, the cost of the day, the number of days still to come, the weight of what is left in the pack. It is natural, and it can be useful. So do the counting, and then put it down. Let the figures be what they are for ten minutes, and then close the book.

What held your eyes today? Think back through the hours and look for the places where your head went down for something that was not the path. A screen, a worry about the money, a plan for the next stage, a quiet replay of something you said. None of them was a crime. But ask what you missed while you were looking. There may have been a view, or a face, or a bird on a wall. You cannot get it back, but you can know that it was there.

The pope said that all other burdens seemed a feather beside the cloak. It is worth asking what your cloak is. For some people it is a job, for some a family role, or a reputation, or a sense of being the one who sorts everything out. A road is a place where you can put it down for a while. Nobody here knows your title, and most of what you do is walk. Did you notice the lightness today? If not, it may be because you were still carrying the cloak out of habit.

Think about rank in the smaller sense. Where did you treat someone as above you or below you today? Perhaps a person who seemed to know more, or a host, or a stranger who was not very well dressed. The question is not whether you were rude. It is whether, for a moment, you forgot that you were fellow-travellers. If you remembered, what changed?

You are going to lie down tonight, and it is worth noticing that it is not the lying down of the poem. Their rest is a restraint. Yours is a choice, and an earned one. It is a gift to be able to lie down on purpose, to a clean sheet, and to know you can stand up in the morning.

One last thing. Give something away tomorrow, however small: a spare plaster, a piece of fruit, a few minutes of attention. Open hands are hard to bind.

- What held my gaze today, and what did I miss while it did?
- Where did I treat someone as above or below me, and what changed when I did not?
- What is one small thing I could give away tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*Lift my eyes from what I hold to what is far off. Loosen my hands and my feet. Let me count what I need and no more. Teach me to stand up, and to call everyone I meet a fellow traveller.*
