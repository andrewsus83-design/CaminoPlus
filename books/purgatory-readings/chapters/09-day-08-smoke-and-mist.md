# Day 8. Smoke and mist

*What do you do when anger takes the view away?*

## The passage

> Darkness of hell, and of a night deprived Of every planet under a poor sky, As much as may be tenebrous with cloud, Ne’er made unto my sight so thick a veil, As did that smoke which there enveloped us, Nor to the feeling of so rough a texture; For not an eye it suffered to stay open; Whereat mine escort, faithful and sagacious, Drew near to me and offered me his shoulder. E’en as a blind man goes behind his guide, Lest he should wander, or should strike against Aught that may harm or peradventure kill him, So went I through the bitter and foul air, Listening unto my Leader, who said only, “Look that from me thou be not separated.” … “Thee will I follow far as is allowed me,” He answered; “and if smoke prevent our seeing, Hearing shall keep us joined instead thereof.” … Remember, Reader, if e’er in the Alps A mist o’ertook thee, through which thou couldst see Not otherwise than through its membrane mole, How, when the vapours humid and condensed Begin to dissipate themselves, the sphere Of the sun feebly enters in among them, And thy imagination will be swift In coming to perceive how I re-saw The sun at first, that was already setting. Thus, to the faithful footsteps of my Master Mating mine own, I issued from that cloud To rays already dead on the low shores.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Cantos XVI and XVII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

Mist does something strange to a road. The next marker vanishes, then the next bend, and the world shrinks to a few metres of path with a wall of white on every side. Sounds seem nearer. You find yourself staying close to whoever is ahead, or you stop talking, or you slow down without being told to. If you have ever been caught in cloud on a hill, you know the particular quiet of it.

Anger does the same thing, only on the inside. In the middle of it you cannot see the other person, or the day, or the way out, and the world is the size of the grievance. The third ledge of the mountain is the ledge of the angry, and the poem's picture of it is smoke.

The passage begins with a fierce description of darkness. Neither the darkness of hell, Dante says, nor a night without a single planet under a poor, cloud-choked sky, ever made so thick a veil as this smoke, or one so rough to the feeling. It does not even allow an eye to stay open. So Virgil, faithful and wise, draws close and offers his shoulder, and Dante goes after him as a blind man goes behind his guide, to keep from wandering or striking something that might harm or kill him. All Virgil says is: *Look that from me thou be not separated.*

Inside the smoke there are voices, and they are singing. In the lines we skip, they all sing the same words in the same measure, a prayer for mercy and peace, so that harmony seems to hold among them even though no one can see anyone. Then a voice asks who this is that cuts through their smoke as if he still reckoned the days by the calendar. It belongs to a man who says his name is Marco, a Lombard, who loved the world's excellence, and who agrees to go with them as far as he is allowed. And he says the sentence that is the heart of this day: *and if smoke prevent our seeing, Hearing shall keep us joined instead thereof.*

If you cannot see, listen. It is a very simple instruction and a rarely used one. Sight is the sense we trust, and anger shuts it first, so we blunder on trusting it. The poem proposes a different thread: a voice, a presence, a sound that stays beside you. Marco goes on to speak of the world's troubles, and we will not follow him. But he stays with the travellers while the smoke lasts, and that is what the passage is for.

Then the poet turns to you. *Remember, Reader, if e'er in the Alps A mist o'ertook thee.* He is addressing a walker, someone who has been caught on a mountain. He asks you to recall how it felt when the thick vapours begin to thin and the sun enters among them weakly, and he says your imagination will be quick to understand how he first saw the sun again. He does not describe it. He trusts yours. And look at what he saw: the sun was already setting. They come out of the smoke into rays *already dead on the low shores*.

It is not noon after the anger. It is a late, low light, and it is enough to walk by. That is honest. Some anger clears at midday, and some only as the day is closing, and you cannot hurry it. You can only keep your hand on the shoulder and wait for the vapour to thin.

Three limits on all this. First, the poem does not say that every anger is wrong. Elsewhere on this ledge it shows quiet examples of gentleness. The ledge is for the anger that has become blind, which is another matter. Second, there is no cure here for the real wrong that may lie behind a person's anger. A grievance can be just and the smoke still smothering. Third, you cannot always stay close to a guide. Sometimes there is nobody, and you are in the smoke with your own thoughts. Then the thread may be your own voice, saying one short sentence aloud, or the plain count of your own steps.

On the Camino, the causes are small and ordinary: a missed turn, a snorer, a person ahead who walks at the wrong speed, a bar that has run out of what you wanted, your own tired body. By mid-afternoon you may be in it already.

So, today: notice when the view narrows. Stop talking, for a hundred steps if you can. Listen for something that holds: the sea, a bell, a footstep behind you, the person beside you. And let the late sun count as the sun.

## For the walk

- When my view narrows, what can I still hear?
- When you notice irritation rising, stop talking and keep silent for a hundred steps. Listen: to your feet, your breath, the wind, water, a bell, a voice in the distance. Speak again only if the words are still needed. If you are walking with someone, tell them in advance that you will do this, so that the silence is not a message.

## At the end of the day

If you met the smoke today, you will know it by the smell it leaves on the evening. Even when the vapour has gone, a day that had anger in it keeps some of the sourness. So open the window, or sit near a door, and let a little air in.

Begin with the cause, and keep it small. Name the thing that did it, in one or two words. A person, a delay, a pain, a sound. You may find that it was not what you were angry about at the time. Fatigue, hunger and thirst are cheap sources of fury, and a surprising number of rows are settled by a sandwich. That is not an excuse, only a useful thing to know.

Then think about what you did inside it. Did you speak, or go silent, or walk faster? Did you hold on to someone, a companion or a voice, or did you try to find the way alone? In the poem the guide offers his shoulder and the only instruction is to keep in touch. Whose presence kept you joined to the day, even when you could not see well? It may have been very quiet. A person who said nothing and walked beside you has done what Virgil did.

Anger at yourself is also smoke. A wrong turn, a forgotten item, a decision you regret: these cloud the eyes in the same way, and they are harder to talk your way out of, because nobody else is to blame. If that was your smoke today, the poem's answer is the same: stay close to what is good in you, and listen to it. Be as gentle with yourself as you would be with someone you were guiding.

And if you said a sharp word to somebody, or kept a cold silence that was meant to be heard, the end of the day is a good time to mend it. A sentence is enough: that you were short with them, and are sorry. If you were right, and still unkind, you can be sorry for the unkindness alone. If you cannot do it tonight, do it in the morning, before the road has started to set.

If you tried the hundred steps of silence this morning, think about what you heard in them. For most people it is something ordinary, such as their own feet and breath, a gate, birdsong, a distant engine, and it is usually more than they expected. That small, steady sound is the thread the poem is talking about, and it is always there, even on a day when you did not need it.

If there is a sunset where you are, and it is safe to look at it, go and see it. The poem's late sun is a good one to end with.

- When did my view narrow today, and what did I do about it?
- Whose voice or presence kept me joined to the road?
- Is there a sharp word I could soften with a single sentence?

## A short prayer

*When the smoke comes, let me hear a voice. Keep a hand on my shoulder and a guard on my tongue. Let the sun come back low and gentle, and let me walk by it. Forgive what I said in the dark, and teach me to be quiet sooner.*
