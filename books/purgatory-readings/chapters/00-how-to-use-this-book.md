# How to use this book

This is a small book for fourteen slow days. It is made to go with the Camino del Norte, the road that runs from Irun to Santiago de Compostela, ~820 km. You read one day at a time, whether you are walking or at home.

The road is far longer than the book. If you want one day for each walking day, the book will run out early and you can read it again. If you would rather make it last, read a day every second or third day, or keep one for a rest day. Nobody is counting.

## Why this poem for this road

Dante's *Purgatorio* is the story of a climb. A poet and his guide come out of the dark onto a shore at dawn and begin to walk up a mountain, one stage at a time: a gate, seven ledges that wind round the slope, and a garden at the top. Each ledge has its own weight to put down, and the way is steep at first and kinder higher up. The poem begins by the sea, as this road does.

The Norte is a long road of sea and hills, and it too is taken in stages. The poem notices things any walker will know: the first hard hill, the hour when the heart turns for home, a row of people leaning on each other, a night when you cannot go on, a guide who has done all he can.

## A day, in two parts

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from the poem, in the exact words of Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's translation. It is old-fashioned English (*thou*, *doth*, *o'er*), which slows the eye down. Read it twice, the second time aloud if you can. Where a stretch is skipped inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. Longfellow wrote in lines of verse, grouped in threes; here the lines are joined with a space, so the breaks are not shown. The words and the punctuation are his.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Dante's. It starts from something on the road and lets the passage open it. It says what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. Please read on a bench or in a café, not while you walk along a road.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have lived, with the morning's passage in mind, and two or three gentle questions. You do not need to write anything down.
- **A short prayer.** Original and short, and not Dante's. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish.

## A poem of its time, and who it is for

The *Purgatorio* is the middle part of the *Divine Comedy*, written in the early fourteenth century. It belongs to the medieval Catholic imagination: its mountain is the poem's picture of purgatory, as that faith described it, and its people pray, sing hymns and speak as believers. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. We do not explain that belief or argue for it. We read the mountain as a picture of a climb, and the people on it as walkers with something to put down.

People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here. You do not need to share the poet's faith to know what he noticed about tiredness, comparison, anger and the wish to go on. You do not need to have read the poem's first part. Many of the souls on the mountain are people of Dante's own day, and where a name matters we explain it in a clause.

## The shape of the fourteen days

The days follow the climb, in the order of the poem.

- **Days 1 to 3, the shore and the foot of the mountain.** Setting out, the hard beginning, and the hour of the evening bell.
- **Day 4, the gate.** A threshold, three steps and a warning not to look back.
- **Days 5 to 11, the ledges.** Pride (two days), envy, anger, sloth, greed and appetite, one after another.
- **Days 12 to 14, the top.** A wall to pass and a night on the stairs, a guide who lets go, and the garden where the climb ends.

The shape is a guide, not a timetable. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a shared kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well.

Read slowly. The poem was meant to be heard, one line at a time.
