# Day 13. Crowned and let go

*What if the guide has done his part, and tells you to lead?*

## The passage

> And said: “The temporal fire and the eternal, Son, thou hast seen, and to a place art come Where of myself no farther I discern. By intellect and art I here have brought thee; Take thine own pleasure for thy guide henceforth; Beyond the steep ways and the narrow art thou. Behold the sun, that shines upon thy forehead; Behold the grass, the flowerets, and the shrubs Which of itself alone this land produces. Until rejoicing come the beauteous eyes Which weeping caused me to come unto thee, Thou canst sit down, and thou canst walk among them. Expect no more or word or sign from me; Free and upright and sound is thy free-will, And error were it not to do its bidding; Thee o’er thyself I therefore crown and mitre!” … Eager already to search in and round The heavenly forest, dense and living-green, Which tempered to the eyes the new-born day, Withouten more delay I left the bank, Taking the level country slowly, slowly Over the soil that everywhere breathes fragrance.
>
> — Dante Alighieri, *Purgatory* (*The Divine Comedy*), Cantos XXVII and XXVIII (tr. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow)

## Reflection

At some point on a long walk you notice that you have stopped checking. You know what a waymark looks like, you can read the lie of the land, and you trust your feet to find the line of the path. The guidebook is in the pack, the arrows are friendly, and you simply walk. It happens gradually, and then there is a day when you notice that it has happened.

Dante's guide has been with him since the first canto of the poem. At the top of the stairs, with the last fire behind them, Virgil turns and fixes his eyes on him, and makes a speech. It is the farewell speech of a teacher, and the strange thing is how brief and how plain it is. *The temporal fire and the eternal*, he says, Dante has seen, the fire that ends and the fire that does not, and he has come to a place where Virgil himself can see no further. *By intellect and art I here have brought thee.* That is what Virgil is in the poem: human reason and human craft, and a good deal of both, which have carried a man this far and cannot carry him any further.

Then comes the line that startles: *Take thine own pleasure for thy guide henceforth.* Dante is beyond the steep and narrow ways, Virgil says. Let him look at the sun on his forehead, and at the grass, the flowers and the shrubs that this ground grows by itself. Until the beautiful eyes arrive, the eyes whose weeping sent Virgil to him in the first place, he may sit down, and he may walk among them. Those eyes belong to the lady from heaven who sent Virgil, and readers of the poem have long taken her to be Beatrice.

*Expect no more or word or sign from me.* Dante's will, says Virgil, is free, upright and sound, and it would be an error not to follow it. And then the last line, the one that gives the day its title: *Thee o'er thyself I therefore crown and mitre!* A crown is a king's and a mitre is a bishop's. Virgil is saying that Dante has authority over himself.

The second half of the passage is one of the loveliest walks in the poem. Dante, eager to explore, goes alone into the heavenly forest, which is thick and living green and which softens the new day for his eyes. He leaves the bank and takes the level ground *slowly, slowly*, over soil that breathes fragrance everywhere. In the lines that follow, a soft wind bends the branches all one way, and the birds do not stop singing in the treetops. Nobody tells him where to go.

Three things in this deserve a moment. The first is the guide's limit. Virgil does not pretend. *Where of myself no farther I discern.* A good guide knows where their sight ends, and says so. What the guide leaves you is yourself. The most generous thing a guidebook, a friend or a teacher can do is to become unnecessary.

The second is the line about pleasure, which is easy to misread. It does not mean doing whatever you like, and it should not be taken as a licence on the first day of a walk. On the poem's terms, Dante's wants have been trained by the whole climb, and the question of what he wants and what is good have drawn together. The line describes where the climb arrives, not where it starts. On a long road you may find, by the second or third week, that what you want, a bench, a coffee, a silence, a hill, and what is good for you lie closer to one another than they did at the beginning. That is only a possibility, and nothing here depends on it.

The third is the *slowly, slowly*. After the stairs, the level ground. The poem could have hurried through it, and instead it repeats the word. On a road, the easy stretch is where the temptation is to speed up because you can. The poem suggests the reverse: the flat, kind country is the one to take slowly.

There is a loss in it too. Virgil is still beside Dante when this canto ends, listening with a smile, but he no longer steers. A little later, in Canto XXX, Dante turns to him as a child runs to its mother, and finds he is gone: *Virgilius, sweetest of all fathers*. The poem lets that be as abrupt as such partings are. It is worth knowing that the freedom here has a price, and that some of it is silence.

If you have had a guide on this road, of any kind, you may find today that you are more alone than you expected, and also that you are fine.

## For the walk

- Where could I trust myself today without checking?
- Choose one small decision, which side of the street, where to rest, what pace, and make it from what you want, without asking anyone or looking anything up. Then, when the way is level and kind, slow down. Walk a hundred steps *slowly, slowly*, and see what the ground and the air have to say.

## At the end of the day

Begin with thanks. Who or what has guided you on this road? A book, a friend, a stranger, a waymark, the voice in your head that says *left here*. The yellow arrow is a particular kind of guide: it never explains, and it never tires, and it only points. You may never have thanked any of them. Do it now, in your mind, one by one. It takes a minute, and it is a way of closing the account. If one of them is a person, a single sentence to them tomorrow will do.

Then think about where you decided things yourself today. It need not be anything grand. Where to stop, whether to take the longer way round, whether to talk or to be quiet. Look at how it felt. Was it easy and clear, or did you want someone to tell you? And how did it turn out? If it was good, notice that. If it was not, notice that too, without scolding. The poem trusts a will that has been through something, and you have been through some days.

The poem has Virgil say that Dante could sit down or walk. Notice which you did today. Both are the same freedom, and neither is more virtuous.

Silence is the other thing Virgil gives, and it is not the same for everyone. A day with no voice to tell you what to do might have felt lonely, or it might have felt like air. Either is a fair report. If it was lonely, the poem does not call that a fault. A road has its lonely stretches, and the freedom to be alone comes with them.

And the slow part: was there a level stretch today, and did you take it slowly? If you hurried, there may be another one tomorrow. The flat road is a gift that does not come as often as one would like.

Try a small experiment before you sleep. Ask yourself what you want, right now, this minute, and answer honestly: a glass of water, a hot shower, silence, a voice on the phone. Do it, if you can. It is a very small crowning, and the poem's trust in a trained will can only be tested in small things like this.

A last, quiet thought. The road is coming to its end, or the book is, or both. Some of the people you have met will go another way soon. It is early to say goodbye, and there is no need to do it tonight. But it is not too early to keep a little attention for them. Notice one person today whose company you would not like to lose, and tell them so, if it feels right, in a sentence.

- Where did I decide something today from my own wanting, and how did it turn out?
- Who or what has been my guide on this road, and have I thanked them?
- Where could I have gone more slowly, and what might I have seen?

## A short prayer

*For every guide I have had, the loud ones and the silent ones, I am grateful. When the words stop, teach me to trust what the road has taught me. Let my wanting be honest and my steps slow, and let me sleep.*
