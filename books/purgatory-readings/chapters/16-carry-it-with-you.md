# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the fourteen days, or you have stopped early, or you mean to go on slowly. All three are fine. This book gives out no certificate. It was meant as company on a road, and a road has no use for a book once the road is done, except to be remembered kindly.

So only one question is left, which is what to take down the mountain with you.

Not everything. Nobody carries a whole poem. Choose three small things.

- **A line.** Something Dante said that stayed in your head. It might be *And aye the more one climbs, the less it hurts.* It might be *Hearing shall keep us joined instead thereof*, or *Hungering at all times so far as is just*, or just two words, *slowly, slowly*. Write it on a card, or on the inside of something you will see every morning.
- **A ledge.** The mountain has seven, and each is a weight: pride, envy, anger, sloth, greed, appetite, and, at the top, a love that ran too hot, which this book only passed through. You may recognise one of them as yours. The poem does not ask you to be finished with it. It asks you to know which one you carry, and to notice when it is heavy.
- **A name.** The one you would say at the wall of fire. A person, or a reason, or a place. Keep it where you can reach it, because the stretch you are afraid of tends to turn up when you are tired.

If you can keep all three for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from any book.

## How to go on

Go back whenever you like. The days are short and were made to be read more than once. A day that did nothing for you at the shore may say a great deal on a hard hill later on. Read the evenings alone for a week. Read a single passage aloud on a bad morning and nothing else. Or read the days in the order the weather gives you, not the order of the poem.

You might also read Dante himself. This book has used a small part of one third of a long poem, and every word of it, in this translation, is in the public domain. The *Purgatorio* is in the same translation, from Project Gutenberg, ebook number 1002. The whole poem, with the *Inferno* before it and the *Paradiso* after, is there as ebook number 1004. Some people read a canto at a time and stop when something catches. That is how it was meant to be read. Long stretches of it are about the people and the quarrels of the fourteenth century, and you may skip them without shame.

And be honest about what did not work. Some days will have rubbed. Some passages will have been strange or hard, or asked for more than you could give. That is allowed. A book of this kind is not a test. If a day did not speak to you, nothing has gone wrong. Leave it, and take what did.

## A last word

Dante wrote this poem far from home, in the years when he could not go back. He ends it with no triumph. He says the pages are full, and that he came back from the water renewed, like trees with new leaves, ready to rise. Not arrived. Ready.

That may be all that a road can honestly give. You do not have to know where the next stretch leads. You need a next step, some patience with yourself, a name to say when you are afraid, and now and then a hand on the shoulder at the right moment.

Walk again. Next year, if you can. Or tomorrow, round the block, with your phone in your pocket and your hands open.

*Buen Camino.*
