# Cantabria: Kobaron to Unquera

## The character of these days

Eight days of small bays, estuaries and old fishing towns, with two boats on offer and a few very long days. A Cantabrian walking guide to this stretch gives nine stages. This book merges the second and third, and it follows the rest. The national tourism site calls the Cantabrian road easy walking, with plenty of road and track.

The lengths come from that guide, "Caminando por el Patrimonio" (2018), which the Camino Lebaniego website hosts. Its stages follow every loop of the official route, such as the detour to Santoña and the walk round the whole bay of Santander. They add up to ~225 km, and the guide says ~250 km. The national tourism site counts only ~170 km, in seven or eight stages. Where boats or shorter ways exist, the real day can be much shorter than the figure, so read the figures as the longest honest version. The route description comes from Cantabria's 2015 definition of the Way, published in the official state gazette.

### Day 9: Kobaron to Castro Urdiales (~21 km)

The Cantabrian route officially begins at El Haya and passes Ontón. The guide counts ~21 km from El Haya to Castro Urdiales, so add a little for the walk from Kobaron. You climb a mountain road for more than ~6 km, drop into the valley of Otañes and follow an old railway line for ~3 km to Santullán. The way then goes by Sámano into Castro Urdiales. It is the first of the "Cuatro Villas de la Costa", the four medieval coastal towns of the Hermandad de la Marina de Castilla, and it has a Gothic church of Santa María, a castle and a hermitage perched on a crag.

### Day 10: Castro Urdiales to Laredo (~35 km)

A very long day. The guide breaks it in two, at El Pontarrón in Guriezo, ~12.9 km from Castro Urdiales, with ~22.5 km to Laredo. The road goes on minor roads by Allendelagua, Cerdigo and Islares, where the official text records the ruins of a pilgrims' hospital. After El Pontarrón it crosses Liendo and the ridge tracks of the Sierra de la Vida, then drops into Laredo, the second of the four towns. Its church of Santa María de la Asunción has a fifteenth-century carving of St James as a pilgrim. Decide in advance where you will break this day.

### Day 11: Laredo to Güemes (~35 km)

Another long day, with the second boat. From Laredo's seafront a boat crosses the bay to Santoña. The alternative is on foot by Colindres, over the bridge at Treto that replaced the barge that once crossed the Asón, and by Escalante. Either way, Santoña is part of the route: it was where pilgrims landed after embarking at Laredo, which the documents of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries show, according to the official text. Everyone then walks back to Argoños. Beyond, a coastal and an inland branch lead to Bareyo, with its twelfth-century Romanesque church of Santa María. From there an inland road runs to Güemes, and a coastal one by Ajo to Galizano.

### Day 12: Güemes to Santander (~40 km)

The guide's ~40 km is the walk round the whole bay. The official text offers a boat from Somo to Santander as an option, and it describes the way round on foot by Pedreña, the shore path, El Astillero and the streets of Maliaño or Guarnizo. With the boat the day is far shorter, but check its times. Santander's cathedral grew from a collegiate church that had a chapel of St James in the fourteenth century, and the official text records several pilgrims' hospitals.

### Day 13: Santander to Santillana del Mar (~40 km)

The longest day in Cantabria, much of it on minor roads and tracks beside the motorway and the railway: Santa Cruz de Bezana, Boo de Piélagos, the river Pas (crossed in the Middle Ages by the "Barco de Mogro", a ferry), Mar, Requejada and Barreda. The official text names these places, and this book does not say where to stop, so ask the app. Santillana del Mar has its Romanesque collegiate church with its historiated cloister capitals. The national tourism site notes that access to the original Altamira cave, at Santillana del Mar, is heavily restricted, and that a replica can be visited at the Altamira museum.

### Day 14: Santillana del Mar to Comillas (~25 km)

The road goes by Oreña and Cigüenza to Cóbreces, where the abbey of Santa María de Viacoeli stands, and down to the beach of Luaña. Then come Ruiloba and Concha. Comillas is known for its monumental nineteenth- and twentieth-century architecture, including the palace of the Marqués de Comillas and Gaudí's Capricho, as the official text says. It also had a pilgrims' hospital before the sixteenth century, founded with a papal bull.

### Day 15: Comillas to San Vicente de la Barquera (~12 km)

A short day. You cross the ría de la Rabia, pass the beach of Oyambre, and reach the Puente de la Maza, successor to a late medieval bridge, over the ría of San Vicente. On the far bank are the ruins of a Franciscan convent of the fifteenth century, and then the medieval town, with its rock castle and its church of Santa María de los Ángeles, which keeps a carving of St James the pilgrim. San Vicente is also where the Camino Lebaniego begins, to the monastery of Santo Toribio.

### Day 16: San Vicente de la Barquera to Unquera (~16 km)

The road goes by La Acebosa and the tower of Estrada to Serdio, where a pilgrims' hospital once stood. At Muñorrodero the Lebaniego way forks away. Your road crosses the ría of Tina Menor by a bridge to Pesués, where there was a hospital beside the hermitage of Santiago. A path beside the railway leads to Unquera, and then you cross the river Deva into Asturias.
