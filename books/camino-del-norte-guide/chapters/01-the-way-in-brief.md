# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino del Norte, also called the Camino de la Costa, begins at Irun, across the Bidasoa river from Hendaye in France. The walk starts at the international Santiago bridge. It crosses four regions: the Basque Country, Cantabria, Asturias and Galicia. Asturias describes it as the continuation of the road that runs through the west of France, the "Camino de Soulac".

In Galicia the road leaves the sea at Ribadeo and goes inland, by Mondoñedo, Vilalba and the monastery of Sobrado dos Monxes. At Arzúa it meets the Camino Francés, and the Xunta de Galicia adds that the Camino Primitivo, which joined the Francés at Melide, is also on that road. The three routes walk the last ~38 km together.

## How long is it?

The app's catalog gives ~820 km. Other sources do not agree with it, or with each other:

- Wikipedia says ~817 km from Irun, and the Confraternity of St James says ~825 km "depending on route chosen".
- The Asturias tourism pages give ~815 km for the whole Camino de la Costa.
- Spain's national tourism site says the road is ~800 km long, but its own figures for the four regions (~175 km, ~170 km, ~280 km and ~220 km) add up to ~845 km.
- The Spanish Wikipedia counts ~828 km from Hendaye to Arzúa, and ~867 km to Santiago.
- The Cantabria guide and the Spanish government's 2015 announcement of the UNESCO listing give ~936 km for the listed route.

They start in different places (Hendaye, Irun or Hondarribia) and count different variants. Plan on ~820 km and expect it to feel longer. The thirty-seven days of this book add up to ~867 km, because they follow the official stage lists with all their loops.

Stage counts vary as much. The four regional lists have eight, nine, thirteen and eight stages: thirty-eight in all. The national tourism site recommends 8 or 9, 7 or 8, 9 to 11, and 8 to 10 for the regions, which is 32 to 38 days. This book follows thirty-seven walking days, named by their stage towns. With rest days, count five to six weeks.

## Why the north?

The Basque Government says many researchers consider the coast road one of the oldest pilgrim routes to Compostela, with evidence of pilgrims in the first decades of the ninth and tenth centuries, on roads in use since long before. It was the safer way, it says, compared with Navarre, then threatened by Muslim armies. Asturias says the same of its own part: when the kings of the eleventh and twelfth centuries promoted the Camino Francés, the coast and the Primitivo stayed busy because they ran through lands that were already Christian. The Confraternity of St James adds that much of the route follows the line of an old Roman road, the Via Agrippa.

It was also a road for those who came by sea. The Xunta says pilgrims came by land or by sea from England, Flanders, Germany and Scandinavia, and that the route came into prominence in the late Middle Ages, when sea pilgrimages were at their height. Asturias writes that in the thirteenth century the road thrived on pilgrims who started at Irun and on those who landed at the ports of Bermeo and Bilbao. Cantabria's government, when it defined its stretch of the Way in 2015, wrote that the Way has been in use for more than twelve hundred years, and it records pilgrims' hospitals in many of the towns the road crosses.

In 2015 UNESCO added the northern routes, among them this one, to the Camino Francés, which had been listed in 1993.

## Who walks it now

Wikipedia, reporting the Pilgrim's Office statistics, gives the Norte a share of about 4 per cent of pilgrims in 2024. That is far fewer than the Francés. The Confraternity of St James calls it a good alternative for a longer and slightly quieter first Camino, and Wikipedia says it is less travelled because of its changes of height.

## What it feels like

The Confraternity describes the shape of it. The first week in the Basque Country takes you from the coast into a hinterland of hills and woods. After Bilbao the road returns to the sea, with beaches, seaside towns and the roads that join them. Between Avilés and Ribadeo you are often by the sea, with few hills. At Ribadeo you turn inland and the country becomes more undulating, and the highest point of the route, ~710 m, comes shortly after Miraz. It is easy to miss, because the slope is gentle and nothing marks it.

Asturias calls the road a kind of opposite of the Camino Francés. Where the Francés crosses mountains and then the dry plateau, the Norte passes the frontier and runs beside the Cantabrian Sea. Because the road follows the coast, it crosses many rivers, and the Confraternity says there is more walking on tarmac than on the Francés. The next chapter says what that means for you.
