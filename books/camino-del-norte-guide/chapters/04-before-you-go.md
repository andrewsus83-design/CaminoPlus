# Before you go

## Your credencial

The credencial is the pilgrim's passport, and the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says how to get one. There is an official model, a card of sixteen concertina-folded pages. You can request it in person at the Office, or from institutions the cathedral has authorised, such as parish churches, associations of friends of the Way, pilgrim hostels and confraternities. In Spain and abroad some associations are authorised to issue their own, and there is also a digital credencial, as an app. Stamps are collected mostly where pilgrims sleep, the Office says, but also in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, town halls and other places.

At the start of the road, the Basque Government's brochure says the simplest way to get a credencial is at Irun's pilgrim hostel or at a church in the town. Hours change, so check. Get it before your first night, and have it stamped every day.

## Getting to Irun, and home from Santiago

Irun sits on the border, across the Bidasoa from Hendaye in France. Wikipedia notes that its railway station is a major break-of-gauge point, where the French and Spanish railways meet, so a journey from France may mean changing trains at the border. The airport named for San Sebastián stands at Hondarribia, which is Irun's neighbour on the estuary, and the Basque brochure starts its first day from either town. From Hendaye you can walk across the Santiago bridge and begin.

At the other end, the last day passes Lavacolla, near Santiago's airport, as the Xunta de Galicia notes. Timetables change, so plan the way home with them in front of you, not at the last minute.

## Training

Walk, with the pack you will carry, and on hills if you can find them. The Asturias tourism pages advise starting a few months before, with comfortable shoes you have worn before. Test your shoes on at least one long day, in the rain. Start at an easy pace and keep it: this road is long.

## What to carry

Official advice differs on weight. The Asturias pages say the pack should not pass ten kilos. The national tourism site says seven kilos and not more than a tenth of your body weight. Aim for the lower figure. Both lists agree on the essentials: sun cream, a waterproof jacket, a basic first-aid kit, a torch (a head torch is useful) and light sandals for the evening. The Asturias pages also call a walking stick indispensable. On a coast with this much rain, add a waterproof cover for the pack, and a dry bag for one dry set of clothes.

The Basque Government's brochure adds advice for the whole road: carry your identity document, your credencial and some money or a card, and take care with valuables. Carry enough food and water for each day and a first-aid kit. Choose shoes and clothes for the forecast, and keep your phone's battery charged. Save your maps before you set out, with the CaminoPlus app or any other, because signal is not guaranteed.

## Shoes and feet

The national tourism site suggests waterproof hiking boots, half a size larger than usual, already worn in. On a wet road, shoes that dry fast and a spare pair of socks matter a great deal. Treat a hot spot at once, before it becomes a blister.

## Waymarks

Follow the yellow arrows and the scallop shells. The Confraternity says the road is very well marked in Galicia and the Basque Country, and sometimes confusing in Cantabria and Asturias. On some coastal sections the yellow arrows share the way with the red-and-white bars of a long-distance footpath (a GR route), and on some routes arrows marked "bici" are for cyclists. The Basque brochure gives one rule that holds everywhere: follow the official marks, and if you meet yellow arrows that point to a different line, do not follow them.

## Three boats

The Confraternity says there are typically three places on this road where you take a boat: at Pasaia (Pasajes), across the bay from Laredo to Santoña, and from Somo to Santander. At Pasaia the official route itself uses the boat. At the other two, Cantabria's official definition treats the boat as an option and describes the way round on foot. Boats run to their own timetables and seasons, and the weather can stop them. *Check before you go*, and have a plan for the day the boat does not run.

## Language

Spanish is understood everywhere. In the Basque Country you will see signs in Basque as well, and names such as Donostia for San Sebastián. In Asturias, official pages give place names in both Spanish and Asturian, and in Galicia the Xunta uses Galician. A few words of Spanish make every evening easier.

## Money and phone

Carry some cash and a card, as the Basque brochure advises. Beyond that, this book gives no prices: they change, and the app and the people at the door are the right source.
