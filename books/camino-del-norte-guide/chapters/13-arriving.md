# Arriving

## The last streets

On the last day you leave Arca for Santiago, past Lavacolla and up to Monte do Gozo, ~380 m, where pilgrims first saw the cathedral. The Xunta de Galicia then describes the way in: a descent of about 5 km through the San Lázaro quarter, the Porta do Camiño, and the pedestrian streets and squares of Casas Reais, Cervantes and Acibechería, to the cathedral. Wikipedia says its construction began in 1075 and that it was consecrated in 1211. It is a Romanesque church with later Gothic and Baroque parts, and the Baroque façade on the Obradoiro square, completed in 1740 by Fernando de Casas Novoa, has behind it the Romanesque Pórtico da Gloria of Master Mateo, dated 1188.

2027 is a Jacobean Holy Year. The Holy Door is opened only in Holy Years, and the Xunta says that in a Holy Year the alternative way into the cathedral is the Holy Door at A Quintana. Pilgrims' Masses are held in the cathedral, and Wikipedia says the botafumeiro, the great censer, is used at all of them in a Holy Year. Their times change, so ask on the day.

## The Compostela

The Compostela is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules. The Office owns the rules, and they can change. This book says what the Office says, and sends you to the Office in any doubt.

- The Compostela is for those who come to the tomb of the Apostle and follow the routes of the Way of St James on foot, by bicycle or on horseback.
- It asks for at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred kilometres by bicycle, shown by the credencial, stamped along the route.
- On foot or on horseback, that means at least a hundred continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago that the Office recognises. The final stage must include at least the last one leading into the cathedral.
- You may walk the Way in stages, if they follow in chronological and geographical order. A pilgrim who walks only the minimum distance must get the credencial stamped at the start and at the end of each stage, with the date.
- A pilgrim who has already walked part of a recognised route outside Spain needs no less than seventy kilometres in Spain.
- The Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special and exceptional cases.

The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.

From Irun the road is ~820 km, far more than the last hundred kilometres the Office asks for.

## At the Pilgrim's Office

You will need your stamped credencial. To collect the Compostela you take a ticket with a QR code, which lets you follow your place in the queue, and the Office says that in times of great influx it cannot guarantee same-day collection. Its page says the Office is closed on 25 December and 1 January. Procedures and hours change, so look at the Office's own website before you arrive, and do not plan a tight connection for the same day.

Whatever the Compostela means to you, it is a record of a road you walked, not a measure of it.

## The next day

Do not leave on the morning after you arrive. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it rewards a second day.

- **Rest.** After thirty-seven days your legs have earned it.
- **See the cathedral without a queue behind you.** Sit in a square and watch the others arrive.
- **Think about what comes next.** If you want to keep walking, the Camino to Finisterre and Muxía, ~90 km, goes on from Santiago, and has its own book in this series, *To the End of the Earth*.
- **Plan your way home** with the timetables in front of you, not at the last minute.
