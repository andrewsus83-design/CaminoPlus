# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

The app's catalog rates the Camino del Norte 4 on a scale of 1 to 5. It rates the Camino Francés 3, the Camino Primitivo 4 and the Camino Inglés 2. The Norte earns its 4 differently from the Primitivo, which is short and steep. Here the difficulty is length: five or six weeks of walking, with hills that never quite stop, and weather that can wear you down more than the climbs do.

The road does not feel the same from one region to the next. The Basque Government says the first stretch, in Gipuzkoa, was far from a comfortable walk, because the Oiartzun, Urumea, Oria, Urola and Deba rivers each break the coast, and the path must climb out of one valley and into the next. Spain's national tourism site calls the road through inland Bizkaia one of the most rugged and hardest on the whole Camino, with mountain paths. Of Cantabria it says the opposite: easy walking, with plenty of road and track. The Xunta de Galicia rates its part of the route medium. Its first Galician stage, out of Ribadeo, is the only one it rates high.

## What to expect underfoot

The Confraternity of St James says the road has more walking on tarmac than the Camino Francés, with some good sections that make the journey worthwhile. Because it runs parallel to the sea it crosses many rivers, and it says you will often not be in sight of the sea for many kilometres. Waymarking is very good in Galicia and the Basque Country, it says, but occasionally confusing in Cantabria and Asturias.

Pilgrim lodging is enough, it adds, but not as plentiful as on the Camino Francés, and quite a few places open only in summer. That makes the weeks at either end of the season harder to plan, and it is one more reason to read "When to go".

## Who it suits

It suits someone with five or six weeks to spare, or someone who will walk it in parts: it divides naturally by region, and each of the four chapters of days in this book is a real walk of one to two weeks. It suits someone who likes the sea beside the path and a different town every evening, and who does not mind that the sea is sometimes hidden behind a motorway. It suits those who can walk in the rain, because on this coast you should expect some.

It is a good second Camino for someone who has walked the Francés and wants something quieter, and, by the Confraternity's account, a possible first one for someone who wants something longer. Beginners can walk it. They should start gently, and for the first days they should expect the Basque hills.

## Who should choose another

If you have two weeks or less, a shorter Camino fits better. *The English Way* in this series covers a walk of six days, and *To the End of the Earth* covers the road from Santiago to the sea.

If you want the great social road, with the largest number of pilgrims and the most lodging, the Camino Francés is that road. *The French Way* in this series covers it.

If you want a short, steep and demanding walk, look at the Camino Primitivo. *The Original Way* covers it, and the chapter on other roads in this book explains how the Norte and the Primitivo are joined.

If you dislike road walking, or the thought of a boat crossing worries you, read the days of the first three regions before you decide. Parts of the coast are tarmac.

## A word on honesty

This book was written from public sources and not from a walk. It can tell you what the official pages say about a day. It cannot tell you how a day felt. For that, ask people who have walked it, and use the CaminoPlus app for the live answer on beds, boats and weather.
