# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergues, and what to expect

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: bunks in a shared room, a place to wash clothes, and a stamp for your credencial. The national tourism site explains how they work. Some public hostels ask for a donation to cover cleaning and upkeep, and private ones cost a little more. They cannot be booked, and places go first come, first served, with pilgrims on foot ahead of those on horseback and those on bicycles. They usually open around midday. They ask for silence after nine or ten at night, and you leave in the morning by eight or nine.

The Pilgrim's Office adds the spirit of it. Hostels that receive no subsidy are kept going, in austerity, by the contributions of pilgrims, and groups with a support car are asked to find other lodging. The host of a hostel is a *hospitalero*.

On this road, the Confraternity of St James says, pilgrim lodging is enough, but not as plentiful as on the Camino Francés, and quite a few places open only in summer. The Basque Government says the same: plan the next day's stage. The Asturias pages say the region has enough lodging to let you stop when your feet tire, and the Confraternity adds that there are many campsites, many of them in Asturias with tents to hire for pilgrims. Which towns have a bed this month belongs to the CaminoPlus app and to a phone call.

## Sharing a room

A room of bunks asks for a little good manners. Pack your bag the night before, and leave the room quietly at dawn. Use earplugs and an eye mask, because someone will snore. Keep your pack off the floor and your things in one place, and shake out your sleeping bag in the morning. If you see signs of bedbugs, tell the host at once, and wash and dry everything on a hot cycle before it goes back in your pack.

## Eating

The national tourism site says some places offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask. Each region has its own dishes, and the official pages name some of them:

- **Basque Country.** Pintxos, the small bites eaten at the bar, in Bilbao and San Sebastián, and cider, with a cider-house season that, the Basque brochure says, runs from January to April in places such as Oiartzun, Astigarraga and Hernani.
- **Cantabria.** The national tourism site names anchovies from Santoña and velvet crab from Noja.
- **Asturias.** Fabada, a bean stew, the cachopo, and desserts such as *frixuelos* and rice pudding, with local cider.
- **Galicia.** Lourenzá's beans, Mondoñedo's almond cake, Vilalba's San Simón cheese and Arzúa-Ulloa cheese, and the almond Tarta de Santiago.

Drink water often and eat before you are hungry. The Basque Government's brochure asks you to carry food and drink for each stage, which matters on days that pass few villages.

## Rest days

Take them. The road is long, so plan rest days into the weeks. The cities are natural places for one: San Sebastián, Bilbao, Santander, Gijón and Avilés, and Asturias says Gijón and Avilés each ask for an unhurried walk through their streets. Santillana del Mar and Mondoñedo are small towns with old centres. Do not turn a rest day into a second walk. Wash, sleep, look at your feet, and eat something local.

*Check before you go*: opening seasons, boat times and local festivals change from year to year.
