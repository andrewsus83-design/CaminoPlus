# Body, weather and safety

## Feet

Most of the trouble on a long walk starts in the feet. Wet feet blister, so change socks at lunch, air your feet whenever you stop, and treat a hot spot the moment you feel it. Shoes should be worn in, and half a size larger than usual, as the national tourism site advises. If a blister or a swollen foot will not settle, rest, and see a doctor or pharmacist before it becomes an infection. Do not walk on through pain that gets worse.

## Rain, cold and wet

The Confraternity of St James says the weather can run from fine summer days to very damp and cold autumn ones. Carry a waterproof jacket, which both the national tourism site and the Asturias pages recommend, and one dry layer in a dry bag. Wet stone, wet leaves and wet tarmac are slippery, especially on descents and steps, so shorten your stride and use poles if you carry them. In cold rain, stop before you are chilled and put on the dry layer. When the weather turns, stopping early is a sound decision.

## Road and path

The Confraternity says there is more walking on tarmac than on the Camino Francés, and the Xunta de Galicia warns that on the way to Arca the road crosses the N-547 several times, and asks for special care with the traffic. Where you must walk on a road, face the oncoming traffic, keep to the edge, and do not walk it in poor light. The Basque Government gives a rule that keeps you from getting lost: follow the official waymarks, and do not follow yellow arrows that point to a different line.

## Sea and cliffs

The path runs close to the sea in places. Asturias describes blowholes that throw sprayed sea into the air on high-tide days. Stay well back from edges and wet rock when the sea is rough, and keep children and dogs close. Some paths cross beaches, and boats do not run in every weather. Have a plan for the day a boat does not run.

## Heat and sun

The Confraternity says the very hot, dry conditions found further south are unusual on the coast, but heat is not unknown: the record at Santander's downtown station is about 40 °C. Start early on a hot day, wear a hat, use sun cream, and drink before you are thirsty. The Basque Government asks you to carry food and drink for each stage, and you should carry more on the longer days of Cantabria.

## Walking alone, and getting help

The sources differ. The Basque Government's brochure says it is advisable to walk in company, and the national tourism site says it is completely safe to walk alone. Whichever you choose, tell someone your plan and keep your phone charged. Note that signal is not guaranteed everywhere.

The European emergency number is 112. Call it for any real danger, an injury you cannot manage, or a person who has not arrived. Know the name of the nearest village. For anything that worries you, such as chest pain, a fever, a swollen leg or a wound that will not heal, see a doctor. This book gives no medical advice beyond the ordinary: water, rest, an early start, and listening to your body.

## When to stop

Stopping is not failing. Stop when the weather is dangerous, when you are ill or injured, when you are too tired to see the path, or when a boat has not run and the alternative is long. A day lost can be made up.
