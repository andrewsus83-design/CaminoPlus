# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino Sanabrés starts at Granja de Moreruela, a village in the province of Zamora, in Castilla y León, on the Vía de la Plata. The Zamora tourism board writes that behind the village church you take the direction marked Ourense, saying goodbye to the Vía de la Plata, which goes on towards Astorga. It adds that for most walkers the pilgrimage does not begin here: it continues.

From there the road crosses the River Esla, follows the valleys of Valverde and the Tera, climbs into the mountains of Sanabria to Puebla de Sanabria, and crosses the Padornelo pass to Lubián, the last village in Castilla y León. It enters Galicia near A Canda, in the province of Ourense, goes by A Gudiña, Laza and Vilar de Barrio to the city of Ourense, and then by Cea and Dozón to Lalín, in the province of Pontevedra. At A Laxe, in the parish of Bendoiro, the Camino de Invierno joins it, and the two roads go on together over the River Ulla, where you enter the province of A Coruña, to Santiago.

## Which road is this?

The official pages do not agree on its name.

- The Xunta de Galicia calls it the Vía de la Plata or Camino Mozárabe, and its pages on the road never use the word *Sanabrés*.
- The Junta de Castilla y León calls it the Camino Mozárabe-Sanabrés.
- The Confraternity of St James calls it the Camino Sanabrés.
- The CaminoPlus catalog calls it the Camino Sanabres.

It is one road. *Sanabrés* means "of Sanabria", the mountain district around Puebla de Sanabria.

The Vía de la Plata is a different and much longer road. The CaminoPlus catalog lists it from Seville, at ~1,000 km, and the Confraternity says walkers take six to eight weeks. The Xunta says its name has nothing to do with silver: it comes from an Arabic word for a wide paved road. This book covers only the part the Sanabrés follows from Granja de Moreruela, and the app has the Vía de la Plata as its own route.

## How long is it, and how many days?

The app's catalog gives this road a start and an end but no distance and no number of stages. The CaminoPlus website lists it as ~370 km and 15 to 17 stages. The official sources agree once you add their parts together.

- The Junta's guide, in an edition of 2010 updated in 2011, gives ~373 km from Granja de Moreruela to Santiago, ~149 km of them to Lubián.
- The Xunta gives the part it describes as ~222 km by Laza and ~253 km by the longer way through Verín. Its first stage is titled Lubián to A Gudiña.
- The Junta's figure to Lubián and the Xunta's length by Laza make ~372 km. The sixteen days of this book add up to the same.

Stage counts vary. The Zamora tourism board suggested eight stages from Granja de Moreruela to A Gudiña in a leaflet of 2010, and the Xunta lists nine in Galicia by Laza, starting with Lubián to A Gudiña. Together, without counting that one twice, they make sixteen. This book names each day by those stage towns, and the chapter on the long days shows where a day can be split. Plan on ~370 to ~375 km.

## Why it exists

The Junta's guide says this was not a Jacobean road in origin. It grew out of a link between the old kingdoms of León and Galicia, and the royal chronicles show kings on it: Alfonso IX in the spring of 1225, and in 1506 Philip the Handsome, who met Ferdinand the Catholic at Remesal after travelling from Santiago to Ourense and Puebla de Sanabria. The guide cites a pilgrim's account of the middle of the eighteenth century, and names pilgrims' hospitals at Santa Marta de Tera, Rionegro del Puente, Verín, Monterrei and Ourense.

The Xunta tells it from the pilgrim's side. In the second half of the thirteenth century, after the Christian conquest of Córdoba and Seville, it says, pilgrims from Andalucía and Extremadura began to use the Vía de la Plata. Some went on to Astorga and joined the Camino Francés. Others took the diversion by Puebla de Sanabria and A Gudiña, and went by Laza or Verín to Ourense and Santiago. The Zamora tourism board says walkers took it to shorten the road.

The modern road is a revival. The Junta's guide, in 2010, says volunteers and Jacobean associations recovered it, and that it had lately gained attention. So it is an old way, with an uncertain pilgrim pedigree, and a young life as a Camino.

## What it feels like

The Zamora tourism board describes country that changes by the day: Castilian plain, rolling land around Tábara, the valleys of Valverde and the Tera, the most fertile in the province, and then the mountains of Sanabria, a landscape you do not leave until the Apostle's tomb. The Junta's guide says the road lacks the crowding of the older, more traditional roads, and invites a slow walk. The Confraternity says you can expect a relatively quiet and peaceful arrival in Santiago.

Quiet does not mean silent. The Junta says the old natural entrance to Galicia, the Portillas, was bypassed when new roads, the Rías Baixas motorway among them, took a straight line. Several of the Xunta's stage pages put the national road N-525 beside the path, and the Zamora board's leaflet counts the crossings of the motorway. You will cross them and walk beside them.
