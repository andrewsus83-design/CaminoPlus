# Before you go

## The credencial

The credencial is the pilgrim's passport, a folded card of stamps. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says it identifies the pilgrim, gives access to the hostels of the Way, and is the proof you show when you ask for the Compostela. It is issued, the Office says, by a parish church, a confraternity, a diocese, an association of friends of the Way, or another Christian institution connected with the pilgrimage. The Office's pages list confraternities and associations in Spain and worldwide, so start there. If you are coming off the Vía de la Plata, you will already have one.

Stamps are normally collected where pilgrims sleep, but the Office says you can also get them at parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. On a road with long gaps, remember that list. The Office also mentions a digital credential, an app. Read the Office's page before you rely on one.

## Getting to Granja de Moreruela, and home from Santiago

Granja de Moreruela is a village: the Junta's guide counts 313 people, and Wikipedia 337 in a census of 2004. The sources read for this book say nothing about public transport to the village. The nearest city is Zamora, and Wikipedia says Zamora has a railway station on a high-speed line and a bus station with services to nearby villages and larger cities. Ask in Zamora about the connection and check it in the month you travel, because timetables change. The Zamora board's leaflet of 2010 gave Zamora to Granja de Moreruela as two stages, so you may prefer to walk in.

Ourense has a railway station, which Wikipedia places in a western suburb, across the Miño from the main town. Santiago's airport is ~12 km east of the city, and the city has a railway station. Do not plan a tight connection for the day you arrive: you will want to see the Pilgrim's Office and the cathedral first.

## Training

The Xunta's advice is to start training some months before, with walks that grow gradually in time and difficulty, and to plan the stages to suit what you can do. "The Camino is not a race", it says. It also asks for steady warm-up and stretching on the way, with attention to the calves.

On this road that advice has teeth, because three days are ~34 km or more and the high ground comes in the middle. In the weeks before you leave, practise ~20 km on two or three days in a row with the pack you will carry, and a longer day of ~30 km. Include hills, downhill as well as up: your knees and toes will feel the descents.

## What to carry

The Xunta's advice is plain. Keep the pack to what is essential, and never above 10 kg. Wear boots or shoes you have already worn, and the Xunta prefers trekking or mountain boots with a hard sole, with sandals for the evenings. Wear well-fitting socks, cotton and seamless in the Xunta's advice, with a second pair of wool socks if you wear boots, and keep them clean. Its small first-aid kit has what you need for your feet and skin: needles, gel dressings, plasters, bandages, small scissors, sun cream and moisturiser. It asks for about two litres of water a day.

Add what this road needs. The plain and the Galician descents are sunny, so carry a hat and sun cream. The mountains can be cold and wet in any season, so carry a warm layer and a waterproof that covers the pack.

## Money, language and maps

Carry some cash as well as a card, and do not assume that a small place will take cards: ask. Spanish will serve you; in Galicia, place names on the Xunta's pages are in Galician, which is close to Spanish.

Save an offline map before you leave home. The Confraternity says the Vía de la Plata is generally well signed with yellow arrows, but the Zamora board's leaflet of 2010 noted poor waymarking at a few points, and waymarks change. The Xunta's route page offers a KML file of the way, a format many map apps can read. The CaminoPlus app is built to work without signal. It is still in development, and what it holds for this road is described at the end of this book.

## Booking ahead

The app's own note for this road says services are thin, so plan your nights ahead. In Galicia the Xunta's public hostels take no reservations, so for a bed there you simply arrive. For any other place, ask in the app, and phone or message the day before.
