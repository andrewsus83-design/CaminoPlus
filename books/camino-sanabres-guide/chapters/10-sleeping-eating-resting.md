# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Where you will sleep

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: shared rooms, bunk beds, a shared bathroom and sometimes a kitchen, and a *hospitalero*, the host, who stamps your credencial. The Pilgrim's Office says the credencial gives access to the hostels of the Way, so keep it with you.

In Galicia the Xunta runs a public network of hostels, begun in 1993. Its rules, as it states them now, matter on a quiet road. Places are filled as pilgrims arrive, and no reservation is taken. The stay is one night. Priority goes first to pilgrims with physical limitations, then to those on foot, then on horseback, then by bicycle, and last to people travelling with a support car. Rules change, so read the Xunta's page before you go.

The Office adds that hostels with no subsidy are kept going, in austerity, by pilgrims' contributions, so leave what you can and leave the place clean. It also asks groups travelling with a support car, or by bicycle, to find other shelter than the pilgrim hostels.

This book names no hostel and says where none is. Hostels open and close, and a wrong line costs a tired person dearly. In Zamora the villages are small, some with a dozen people. The Junta's guide of 2010 says the road has hostels and services enough to be entirely workable, while the Confraternity of St James asks for extra care in planning. So work out your nights first, then your days, and confirm the night before.

## Living with other people

- **Be quiet early and late.** Pack your bag the night before, and use a torch, not the ceiling light, when you leave in the dark.
- **Keep your boots and pack off the beds.** Some hostels ask for boots to be left at the door, so ask.
- **Check your bunk before you unpack.** If you find bites or bugs, tell the *hospitalero*, and if you can, wash and dry your clothes on a hot cycle.
- **Earplugs and a sleep mask** are the usual answer to snoring and early lights.

## Eating

A *menú del peregrino* is a set meal for pilgrims, offered in some places. This book gives no prices and names no restaurant: ask where you sleep.

The sources describe food that belongs to the country. Wikipedia says Zamora is known for pulses, sheep's-milk cheese and the honey of Sanabria. The Junta's guide names home-cooked stews of veal, lamb, kid and chicken around Tábara, and for Sanabria its beans, its mountain beef and an octopus dish. It describes the *magosto*, the chestnut feast, with chestnuts roasted, boiled with milk or bathed in honey. The Xunta says San Cristovo de Cea is famous for its wheat bread, and the Junta that the monks of Oseira make cheese, honey and liqueurs.

The practical point is the gaps. Buy lunch the day before, fill your bottles at every village, and do not trust a village on the map to have anything open when you arrive. On the high Verea Sur, the Xunta says, the villages are half abandoned.

## Rest days

A rest day is part of a plan, not a failure of it. Puebla de Sanabria comes at the end of the fifth day, with its castle, its old streets and the lake nearby, and Ourense at the end of the eleventh, with its hot springs. Santiago comes last. The chapter on arriving says why to stay a second day there.

## In the evening

Wash your feet and look at them before you sleep. Then spend ten minutes on tomorrow: how long it is, where you will eat lunch, where the water is, and where you will sleep.
