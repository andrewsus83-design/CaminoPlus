# Day 2. An unwelcome errand

*What if the job you least want is where the day is actually lived?*

## The passage

> That he had been lately sent into Burgundy, to buy the provision of wine for the society, which was a very unwelcome task for him, because he had no turn for business, and because he was lame and could not go about the boat but by rolling himself over the casks. That however he gave himself no uneasiness about it, nor about the purchase of the wine. That he said to GOD, *It was His business he was about*, and that he afterwards found it very well performed. … So, likewise, in his business in the kitchen (to which he had naturally a great aversion), having accustomed himself to do everything there for the love of GOD, and with prayer, upon all occasions, for His grace to do his work well, he had found everything easy, during fifteen years that he had been employed there.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Second Conversation (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

A road is mostly chores. Nobody puts that on the postcard. There is the pack to repack, the water to find and fill, the socks to wash in a sink and wring out, the heel to see to, the bed to make up from a sleeping bag or a sheet. There is the long middle of the afternoon, which has to be walked whether or not you feel like walking it. The lovely parts are the minority, and the chores are what is left when the lovely parts have gone by.

Brother Lawrence had chores too, and by the account we have, he did not choose them. The notes of the Second Conversation say that he had been sent to Burgundy to buy the community's wine, and that he found it a very unwelcome task. He had no head for business. He was lame, and the only way he could get about the boat was by rolling himself over the casks. (The notes are in the third person, as if reported to someone else, and the "society" is his religious community.)

Then comes what he did about it, as the note-taker has it. He gave himself no uneasiness, and he told God that it was His business he was about.

Look at how small that move is. The word "business" turns up for two different things here: the trade he had no head for, and the errand that was God's. It would be a neat joke if it were meant as one, and it is a neat idea even if it was not.

The casks are still there. He is still lame. The wine still has to be bought, and he does not claim to have found a talent he lacked. The notes say it went well, and they are the only evidence we have. What changed is not the job. It is whose errand it is. Not "my wretched duty" but "His business". And an errand that belongs to someone else loses much of what makes a dull errand heavy: the commentary about whether you are good at it, whether it is fair, whether it is beneath you.

The kitchen is the same lesson at a slower speed. He had, the passage says, "naturally a great aversion" to it. He did not come to love it, or at least that is not what the notes claim. What they say is that he did everything there for the love of God, asked for grace to do it well, and found everything easy, in the fifteen years he had been at it. Easy is not the same as liked. You can find a thing easy and still not care for it, the way a road gets easier once you stop arguing with it.

There are three places where this is hard.

The first is the word "easy". It sounds too good, and it is a report from fifteen years in. It says nothing about the first week, or the first month, or about the man he was before, who in the First Conversation calls himself "a great awkward fellow who broke everything". Do not hold your own first week to a standard set by someone's fifteenth year.

The second is that handing a task over does not take the ache out of it. Your feet will still hurt. Lawrence is not saying that a heavy pack becomes light, or that a hated job becomes a pleasure. He is saying that a thing can be done whole instead of grudgingly, and that the difference matters even when the thing is no easier.

The third is the phrase "His business". It helps if God is the one you mean. If that is more than you can honestly say, there is a plainer version nearby: the errand in front of you is simply the thing you are doing, and you have only this one chance to do it. The grumbling about it, and the planning of better errands elsewhere, are a second job nobody asked you to take. Putting that second job down is most of what he is describing.

So what does it ask of an ordinary afternoon on a road? Take one chore, the dullest on offer, and do it all the way through. Not as a lesson, not for the sake of becoming a better person. Because it is the part of the day you would otherwise spend somewhere else, and the day has only so many hours. There is a quiet pleasure waiting at the far end, too: the moment a chore is finished and does not need doing again.

## For the walk

- Which part of today am I only trying to get through?
- When the dullest task comes up (the laces, the repack, the queue, the refilling of the bottle), do it as if it were the only thing in the day. Slowly, with both hands and your whole attention. When it is finished, say "done" to yourself, and let it go. If you cannot decide which chore to choose, take the first one that turns up. It will do.

## At the end of the day

If the place you are staying has a sink, it is probably busy. Socks, a shirt, the second shirt, something you do not want to look at too closely. Hands in cold water, soap of uncertain purpose, a wringing that squeezes out half of what it should. It is a very small chore. It is also, if you let it be, a good place to think about the passage.

Go back over the day and find the part you fought. There will be one. It might have been a stretch of road with nothing to look at, a hill that arrived at the wrong time, the daily business of packing, or a conversation you did not want. Ask what the fighting cost. Often it costs more than the thing itself: the hill took an hour, and the argument with the hill took the hour and the morning.

Then look for the opposite, however brief. Was there a moment in the day when you did one thing with all of yourself? Not necessarily a good thing. Tying a lace, pouring water, closing a gate, looking properly at a village. If you cannot find one, that is information, not failure, and tomorrow offers plenty of chances. Nobody learns to say "His business" by tomorrow lunchtime.

There is a small pleasure in a finished chore that is easy to overlook. The sock is on the line, the bottle is full, the pack is closed. These "done" moments are the real milestones of a day on foot, and they come far more often than the kilometres do.

The evening has its own errands, and they are the same kind: wash, hang, eat, charge the phone, lay out tomorrow's clothes. They are not in the way of the rest. They are the rest, taken one small job at a time.

It is worth noticing the chores that were not a fight. The first drink of the day that tasted of something. Unlacing the boots. Hot water on tired feet, if you had it. Nobody offers a prize for these, and they are some of the best minutes a day on foot can give. There is a tiredness that comes from the fight and a tiredness that comes from the work, and tonight you may be able to tell them apart. The second is the cleaner of the two.

Is there a chore you have been putting off since the first morning? The heel you have not looked at properly, the message you have not answered, the bag you keep meaning to sort. Name it tonight. Naming it costs nothing, and it is half of doing it.

Be gentle about tonight. This is not the evening to optimise anything. If the washing will not dry, it will dry on the pack. If the chores are not finished, they will be there in the morning, and they will be exactly the same size.

The questions for tonight:

- Which task today did I try to get through instead of being in it?
- Was there a moment, however short, when I did one thing with all of myself?
- What chore will tomorrow bring, and how could I meet it without a fight?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the plain work of this day. For the pack and the water and the wet socks, for the miles I only put up with. Let tomorrow's errands be Yours, and let me be in them. Give my hands something to do and my mind something to rest on. Good night.*
