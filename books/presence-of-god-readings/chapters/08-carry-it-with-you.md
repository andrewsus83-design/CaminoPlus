# Carry it with you

Six days is not long, and the book does not ask to be finished. You may have read all of it, stopped at the flat day, or read only the evenings. All of that is fine. This was meant as company on a short road, and a road has no use for a book once it is over, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep. Not everything. Choose three small things, and keep them light.

## What to keep

- **A line.** Something Lawrence said, or something said about him, that stayed in your head. It might be the plainest one: *Let us begin, then.* It might be "You tell me nothing new", from the day your mind ran off, or the old man's cheerful admission that he is always saying the same thing. Write it on a card and put it where you will see it.
- **A turn.** One of the small movements: a breath at a bench, a pause before a chore, a return after you have drifted, a gate closed behind you. Pick the one that cost least and helped most, and do it again tomorrow.
- **A task.** Choose one thing in your week that you do not like, and decide to do it whole, the way he went over the casks. Not for ever. Once, this week.

If one of the three is still with you in a month, the book has done what it was made to do.

## Reading Brother Lawrence himself

Go back to him whenever you like. The book we drew on is short, about eleven thousand words, which is an hour or two of reading, and the Letters especially can be taken one at a time, in order, over a few evenings. It is in the public domain, in the same edition we used, at Project Gutenberg as ebook number 13871. Read a letter, and stop when something catches. That is how it was meant to be read.

You will find in it much that is not in these pages: the long self-reproaches, the joys, the letters about sickness. Some of it will not suit you, and some of it may. Read it as you would a letter from a friend of a friend, taking it for what it is, one man's way of living in the company of God, set down in the seventeenth century.

It is fine, too, if little of this fitted. A day may have asked more than you had, or offered less than you hoped. A passage may have seemed strange or beside the point. Leave those, and keep the one that helped.

## A last word

Brother Lawrence was a footman who broke things, a soldier, and then a man in a monastery kitchen who was sent to buy the wine. By his own account he found some of that work unwelcome, and at the beginning he spent his hours of prayer in the failing and returning that this book has been about. He did not present himself as a master. As far as the letters show, he thought of himself as someone who had found one small thing that worked and kept repeating it.

That may be the most useful thing to take from him. You do not need to be strong, or certain, or finished. You need a next step, a little patience with yourself, and now and then a remembrance, however brief, that you are not walking alone.

Walk again when you can. And before that, tomorrow, go once round the block at the pace of the road, and see what you pass.

*Buen Camino.*
