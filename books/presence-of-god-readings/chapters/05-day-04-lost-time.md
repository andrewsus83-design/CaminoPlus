# Day 4. Lost time

*What do you do on the day when nothing is felt?*

## The passage

> I do not say that therefore we must put any violent constraint upon ourselves. No, we must serve GOD in a holy freedom; we must do our business faithfully; without trouble or disquiet, recalling our mind to GOD mildly, and with tranquility, as often as we find it wandering from Him. … Be not discouraged by the repugnance which you may find in it from nature; you must do yourself violence. At the first one often thinks it lost time, but you must go on, and resolve to persevere in it to death, notwithstanding all the difficulties that may occur.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Sixth Letter (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

Sooner or later there is a stretch where nothing happens. The path runs straight, or the village you were counting on is shut, or the weather is the colour of dishwater. The excitement of the first morning has worn off, and the end is still too far away to taste. You are walking, and you feel precisely nothing. Not bored, even. Flat. A voice that sounds like your own asks what exactly you think you are doing here.

On a road of about a week this is roughly where the middle falls, though it may arrive on any day, and it comes whether or not the scenery is good. This is the day for Lawrence's Sixth Letter.

Read the passage twice and you will find two moods in it. The first is gentle. There is to be no "violent constraint". We are to serve God "in a holy freedom", do our work faithfully, and call the wandering mind home "mildly, and with tranquility". The second is firm. Do not be discouraged by the reluctance you find in yourself. "At the first one often thinks it lost time, but you must go on."

The two sit side by side in the same letter, and he does not stop to reconcile them. A fair reading is that he is gentle about how and firm about whether. You do not whip yourself. You also do not stop because the feeling has stopped. Everything he writes assumes that the practice will, for long stretches, seem pointless, and that the pointless-seeming hours are part of it and not a sign that it has failed.

Notice, too, "we must do our business faithfully". Business again, as on the second day: the plain work in front of you. On a road the business is walking, and it can be done faithfully on a day when it is done without a feeling.

The word worth stopping at is "lost". Lost against what? Against a measure: a feeling, a result, a picture of how the practice ought to be going. The hours seem lost because the measure is not being met. Read that way, "go on" means keep walking, and let the measure go quiet.

He is not speaking from a place of ease. In the Second Letter he says, "for the first ten years I suffered much", and in the First Conversation, the note-taker has him telling of "dryness, or insensibilities and irksomenesses" in prayer, a gift of a word for the grey hours of a long afternoon. A man who tells you that the beginning is hard is not selling you a feeling. He is telling you what the middle looks like from the inside, and that it is survivable.

There are two places where this passage needs care, and one of them is about your body.

The first is the phrase "you must do yourself violence". It is not one we would choose today, and it should not be read as an order to push through pain. "Go on" is about the practice, the small returning of the attention, and about the feeling that it is a waste of time. It is not about your knee. If a foot, a joint, a head or a chest is telling you something that worries you, stop. Rest. Ask someone. See a doctor if it does not settle. If it is an emergency in Spain, the number is 112. Nothing in this passage asks you to walk on an injury for the sake of a mood.

The second place is the feeling itself. Sometimes "lost time" is a true report. The day may be flat because you are depleted, or underfed, or in need of a rest day, or because the road has stopped being the right thing for now. The passage is about the particular dullness of a practice that is working in the dark. It does not forbid you to notice that a day is simply a bad one, and to treat it kindly.

He also, in the First Conversation, reads such hours as ways "by which GOD tries our love to him". That is a claim about God's purposes, and it will land differently on different readers. You do not have to accept it to take the sense of it: that a flat hour is not the same as a wasted one, and that what you do inside it still counts.

So what does it ask of a flat afternoon? Not enthusiasm. Only the next small thing, done gently. One foot, then another. A look at what is actually in front of you. And no verdict on the hour until it is over.

## For the walk

- What can I do with an hour in which I feel nothing?
- When the thought "this is lost time" turns up, pick a fixed thing ahead of you, a post, a gate, a bend, a tree, and walk to it without any commentary. Then pick another. Keep to an ordinary pace, and do not try to cure the flatness by hurrying. Do not judge the hour until the evening. If it helps, make the hour smaller: not "until the next village" but "until that gate". Ask nothing more of the afternoon than the gate, and eat something while you are at it.

## At the end of the day

A flat day is hard to describe at the end of it, because nothing happened. There is no scene to tell. Just a lot of time, and your feet at the bottom of it.

Check the body before anything else. A flat afternoon is sometimes only a body asking for food, water, salt, sleep, or a sit-down it has been refused for hours. If you have not eaten properly, eat. If you have not drunk, drink. The feeling of futility can be astonishingly sensitive to a sandwich. This is not a trick. It is a reminder that you are an animal as well as a pilgrim, and the animal has views.

Then look at the grey stretch. When did it start? Often it has an edge: a hill, a lunch missed, a bad night, an unwanted message. When did it ease, if it did? Sometimes a flat spell ends so quietly that you could not say when. You look up and the light has changed and you are walking differently. It is worth noticing whether that happened, and what you were doing when it did. If it did not ease, and you are still in it tonight, that is allowed. Lawrence's ten years were not a week. A single day of greyness is nothing to be afraid of.

The passage says "persevere in it to death", and by that Lawrence means for life, not for the next week. Tonight you need only persevere until morning.

Make a short list in your head of three small things that were fine today. Not good, only fine: a wall with the sun on it, a nod from a stranger, the moment the stone came out of the shoe. Flat days carry small good things, and they are easy to miss because you were looking for large ones. Fine is underrated. A week is built mostly of it.

Some of the people around you tonight may have had the same afternoon. Nobody announces it. There is a quiet company in a room of people who have each been through a flat day and are not going to mention it.

And ask whether the time was truly lost. Think of what you did anyway. A kilometre walked without joy is still a kilometre. A courtesy offered in a flat mood is as real as one offered in a glad one, and perhaps costs a little more. If you cannot find anything, then you did what some days are for. You went on.

If anything hurt today in a way that surprised you, look at it now, in good light, and decide about tomorrow. A rest day is an ordinary part of a road, and so is asking someone for help.

Then rest, properly, without making it another task. Lawrence's two moods apply here as well: be gentle about how, and firm about whether. Tonight that means sleeping.

The questions for tonight:

- Where today did I think "this is lost time", and what did I do with the thought?
- What did I do anyway that I am quietly glad of?
- What does my body need tonight, and have I given it?

## A short prayer

*Nothing to report. The road was long and I felt little. Be with me in the flat hours as You are in the bright ones. I will not pretend. Let me go on gently, and rest well, and wake without a verdict.*
