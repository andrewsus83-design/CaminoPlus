# Day 6. The same thing again

*What is worth carrying home?*

## The passage

> How can we pray to Him without being with Him? How can we be with Him but in thinking of Him often? And how can we often think of Him, but by a holy habit which we should form of it? You will tell me that I am always saying the same thing. It is true, for this is the best and easiest method I know; and as I use no other, I advise all the world to do it.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Ninth Letter (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

The last stretch of a road is the one you can least picture. You will have pictured the arrival, perhaps: the long approach, the first sight of the city, the end. It is much harder to picture the feeling of it, which may be a mixture of relief and a small, strange loss. A body that has spent days on its feet does not know what to do with a stopped afternoon. A mind that has been given a single task, to get to the next place, finds itself without one.

Which brings us to the question the last day has to carry. What now? And what, if anything, of this goes home?

Lawrence's answer, in the Ninth Letter, is almost comically plain. He builds a little staircase of questions. How can we pray to God without being with God? How can we be with God but by thinking of Him often? How can we think of Him often, but by forming a habit? Then, before anyone can say it, he says it for them: "You will tell me that I am always saying the same thing." And he agrees. It is true. It is the best and easiest method he knows, and he uses no other.

Take the first word out of the staircase and it still stands, which is worth seeing. How can you attend to anything without being with it? How can you be with it without thinking of it often? How can you think of it often except by habit? That is our paraphrase, not his, and it is not what he means. He means God. But it shows how little the shape depends on the name, for a reader who is not ready to use it.

There is something endearing in an old man admitting that he has only one tune. And there is a lot of sense in it. The six days of this book have been the same thing said in six kinds of weather. A little remembrance, on the first morning. The same remembrance inside a chore. The same turning back, after the mind has run off. The same going on, when nothing could be felt. The same attention, spent on a straw. And today, the habit that holds them together. If you have kept anything at all, it is probably only one of these, and it is probably the one that was easiest to repeat.

A road is the same thing again, too. You do not walk a whole road. You walk one step, and then one step, and the road is only what that comes to. Lawrence's method is the same arithmetic applied to attention: a small turning, over and over, until it is less of an effort than not turning. In the Fourth Conversation the note-taker has him saying that "we should not wonder if, in the beginning, we often failed in our endeavors", and that in time "we should gain a habit". Habit is the bridge between the road and the rest of your life.

At the front of the same letter there is a line to hold on to as well. He writes of someone who "would go faster than grace", and adds that one does not become holy all at once. You could say it of a walker who sets out too fast, or of a reader who tries to keep too much. One of the things the road teaches, if you let it, is the pace at which a person can actually go on.

There are three honest difficulties.

The first is "the best and easiest". Easiest to describe, perhaps. Day 4 of this book knew better: it is not an easy practice to keep when nothing comes of it, and in the Fourteenth Letter he says himself that "the beginning is very difficult". Take the word as his enthusiasm, not as a promise.

The second is "I advise all the world to do it." This book does not. It offers one man's way of keeping company with God to anyone who would like to try it, and it is content if you take a single line and leave the rest. In this letter Lawrence is a Christian writing to a fellow member of a religious community, and he is not writing to us. What a reader of another faith, or none, does with his advice is their own affair.

The third is the difference between a road and home. A road holds you. The arrows are painted, the day has a direction, other people are walking the same way, and the whole of your attention is permitted to be on one thing. Home has none of that. A habit that was easy on the road may feel absurd beside a kettle on a wet morning. Be ready for it to fade a little in the second week. Be ready to begin again, which is, after all, the same thing again.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Only that you choose one. And hang it on something you already do, so that you do not need to remember it.

## For the walk

- What is the one thing from these six days I would be glad to still be doing in a month?
- Choose a place you pass every day at home, a doorway, a kettle, a bus stop, a step, and decide that it will be your bench. Whenever you reach it, stop for one breath. If you are still on the road as you read this, walk the last quarter of an hour in silence, and let the last thing you notice be the first thing you take home.

## At the end of the day

Tonight can be quiet. Not every ending needs a ceremony, and some of the best ones are no more than a person sitting on the edge of a bed with the light off, saying nothing in particular.

If the road has ended for you, or ends tomorrow, you may feel a little flat, and that is no surprise. A purpose that has filled days does not leave without a gap. The body will need a few days to believe it can stop. Do not ask yourself tonight to say what it all meant. A walk of this kind is not a thing that can be summed up on the evening it finishes, and most of what it has done will not show for a while.

Think back over the six days instead of over the one. Was there a morning on which something small turned up that you would have walked past on the first day? Was there a moment that surprised you with its quiet? No conclusion is required. A list will do: the things that stayed.

If you walked beside people for a day or two and are about to part, a goodbye said properly is a small kindness, the same size as the straw. You may never see them again. A name, a handshake, a "thank you for the company" costs a minute and is remembered longer than the conversation.

Then ask which of the six you would most like to read again. It does not have to be the best one. It can be the one that stuck in your teeth. A book of this kind is better read twice, and a passage that annoyed you the first time is often the one with something in it.

And think of the place at home that you chose for your breath. Picture it, the doorway or the bus stop, and picture yourself stopping there. Not a speech, only a pause. If the picture makes you smile, that is a good sign. If it makes you wince, choose a gentler place.

Tomorrow, if you are not walking, there may be a strange morning in which the body wakes at its road hour and finds nothing to do. Let it. Decide in advance that you do not have to decide. The first day without an arrow is a day for slowness.

You do not need to resolve anything. You have been walking, and that is plenty. If you want a line for the evening, the Sixth Letter has one that Lawrence gives to a friend who had put off starting: "it is better late than never." It is an easy thing to say to yourself at the end of a road.

The questions for tonight:

- What did I notice on this road that I would have walked past on the first morning?
- Which of the six days do I most want to read again?
- Where, in an ordinary week, could I stop for one breath?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road, and for these six days. For the feet that carried me and the mind that kept coming home. Keep what is worth keeping, and let the rest go lightly. Be near on the ordinary days. I will begin again.*
