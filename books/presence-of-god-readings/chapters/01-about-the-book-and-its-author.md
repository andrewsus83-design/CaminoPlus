# About the book and its author

## The man

*The Practice of the Presence of God* is the work of Brother Lawrence, though not in the way most books are the work of their authors. The title page of the edition we use calls him Nicholas Herman, of Lorraine. The preface says he had been a footman and a soldier, and that he was admitted as a lay brother among the barefooted Carmelites at Paris in 1666, and was afterwards known as Brother Lawrence.

Two of those terms need a gloss, and the gloss is ours, not the edition's. A lay brother is a member of a religious community who is not a priest and who does its practical work. The barefooted Carmelites are the Discalced Carmelites, a reformed branch of the Carmelite order, whose reform began in Spain in the sixteenth century.

The edition tells us a little more, and that is all we claim. The First Conversation reports him saying that at about eighteen, in winter, the sight of a tree stripped of its leaves, and the thought that leaves and fruit would return, gave him a lasting sense of God's power and care. He had been footman to a treasurer, and by his own account, as reported, "a great awkward fellow who broke everything". Later he worked in his community's kitchen and was sent out to buy its wine. The preface says he died at the age of eighty, and in the Seventh Letter he writes "I am almost eighty". The year usually given for his death is 1691. The file we have does not state a year, so treat that date as coming from outside the book.

## The book

Lawrence did not write this as a book. The title page calls it "Conversations and Letters", and that is what it is. There are four Conversations, which are another person's notes of what he said, and fifteen Letters, which the edition presents as his own, to correspondents the book mostly does not name. Most notes in the Conversations begin with the word "That", because they are reported speech, a continuation of "He told me that". We call the person who took them the note-taker. The preface says the Conversations are "supposed to have been written by M. Beaufort", a Grand Vicar, an official who helps a bishop run his diocese.

A note in the edition, on the last letter, records that he took to his bed two days later and died within the week. So the book as we have it was put together by others, after his death.

One more detail. A stretch at the end of the Fourth Conversation carries a footnote saying that its particulars are collected from other accounts of him. Every passage in this book comes from the four Conversations and the Letters, never from that stretch or from the preface. (This chapter does quote the preface and the title page, and says so each time.)

## Why it suits a pilgrim

It is not a book about a destination. It is a book about how to go about a day: how to begin, what to do when your mind runs off, how to do a task you dislike, what to make of a flat hour. It comes in short pieces, which is how a walker reads, and it is a book of attention, which a road tends to ask for whether or not you offer it.

The unsigned preface, which speaks for the edition and not for Lawrence, says he "lived his Christian life through as a pilgrim" and "as a steward and not as an owner". The title page calls the publisher "Publishers of Evangelical Literature", so a Catholic lay brother is presented by an evangelical house. Read the preface's blessings and comments as the edition's, not his.

It is also a hard book in places, and a seventeenth-century one. He calls himself wretched, often. From the Eleventh Letter on he writes of sickness and pain as gifts from God, which is hard to read when you are in pain, and we have not tried to defend or explain it. All of that is left out. We chose the passages that speak most plainly to someone on a road.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages comes from an old print edition, "Translated from the French" as its title page says. The file names no translator and prints no year, so under each passage we credit an anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition (the company of New York, Chicago and Toronto), and we do not guess a date. It is on Project Gutenberg as ebook number 13871, and every quotation here was checked against that text, word for word.

We treat the translation as public domain. Lawrence died in the seventeenth century, and the edition is an old print with no modern editor named and no modern copyright notice in the file. Project Gutenberg offers it as a print edition not protected by United States copyright. For other countries we can say no more than that the translation is old and anonymous.

We changed nothing in the passages. We kept the old spellings, the capital letters the print uses for GOD and LORD (the old way of setting the divine names, not shouting), and the "That" at the head of a note. We left out the small footnote numbers. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the six passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours and they are original. Four of the six passages come from the Letters and two from the Conversations, one of those drawing on two.
