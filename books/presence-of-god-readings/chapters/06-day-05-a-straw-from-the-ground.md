# Day 5. A straw from the ground

*What small thing are you about to walk past?*

## The passage

> That he was pleased when he could take up a straw from the ground for the love of GOD, seeking Him only, and nothing else, not even His gifts. … That we ought not to be weary of doing little things for the love of GOD, who regards not the greatness of the work, but the love with which it is performed.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Second and Fourth Conversations (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

There is something at the side of every path. A glove that fell out of a pocket. A gate that was left open behind somebody. A strap that has twisted on another walker's pack, which they cannot reach. A plastic straw in the verge, bleached by the weather. A person looking at their feet. At walking pace you pass dozens of these in a morning, and walking pace is fast enough to miss every one.

Brother Lawrence's example is the smallest object he could have chosen. The notes of the Second Conversation say he was pleased when he could pick up a straw from the ground for the love of God. Not a rescue, not a sacrifice. A straw. In our day the straw is more likely to be plastic, and the verge is where you will find it.

The second half of the passage, from the Fourth Conversation, supplies the principle. God, he says, "regards not the greatness of the work, but the love with which it is performed." In plain words: nobody is keeping a scale of large and small. A thing done with care is the size of the care.

That matters more on a road than it might at home, because nearly everything you are given on a road is small: a plaster, a pointing hand, a word of warning about the next hill. Nearly everything you give will be small too. If small things count for nothing, almost all of what passes between walkers is wasted. By Lawrence's account they count for exactly what the love in them is worth.

There is a kind of freedom in the example he chose. Nobody could be proud of picking up a straw. A deed that small cannot be a boast, and a deed too small to be noticed is hard to do for the sake of being noticed.

Be honest about what this passage is and is not. Lawrence's examples are a straw, a kitchen, a cask of wine. He does not, in these lines, tell his reader to be kind to the people on the road. That step from the straw to the neighbour is ours, and it is a short one. In the Ninth Letter he does write that "we ought to help one another by our advice, and yet more by our good examples", and near its close the Fifteenth Letter says, "Let us pray for one another." What the passage gives you is the shape of an act, small, attentive and unadvertised, and the road offers a good many people to practise it on.

The harder half is the straw's, and it hides in the last words of that sentence: "seeking Him only, and nothing else, not even His gifts". The sense seems to be that he did not do the small thing for the glow. His "gifts" appear to mean the sweetness and consolation that can come with prayer. He did the small thing for no reward at all, including that one. Put into walking terms: the kindness that is waiting for the other person's face to light up, or for the thank-you, or for the tidy warm sensation of being a good pilgrim, is not quite what he describes. That is a high bar, and most of us will not clear it by lunchtime. It is still worth knowing where the bar is.

There are two more difficulties.

One is that small kindness can be a performance. A road is full of people, and it is easy to find yourself being a helpful walker in front of an audience. The check is simple, if uncomfortable: would you still do it if no one at all could see? The closed gate, the carried rubbish, the greeting to the person who will not look up. Those are the straw. The lent charger announced to the whole room is something else.

The other is that small things can be a way of avoiding a large one. Picking up straws is a comfortable occupation. If there is a call that should be made, an apology that is owed, a decision waiting at the end of the road, a morning of kindly tidying will not discharge it. The passage does not say otherwise. It speaks of little things as worth doing well, not as a way round the day's larger business.

What does it ask of an ordinary stretch of path? To look up from the middle distance. To notice the one small thing within reach, and to do it, once, without a word. If it is a dropped glove and its owner is out of earshot, the glove can be set on a wall or a post at eye height, where they may look if they come back. That is the whole of the act. Then to carry on, a little lighter, and not tell anyone, including yourself.

## For the walk

- What small thing am I about to walk past?
- Do one small thing today that no one will see or thank you for. Close a gate. Carry a bit of rubbish to the next bin. Say *Buen Camino* to someone who is watching their feet. Then do not mention it, to anyone, or in your own head afterwards. Let it stay where you left it, like the straw. It may feel slightly foolish. That is a good sign, since it means the thing was small. If nothing offers itself, look for the second thing. And if someone does something small for you, let them, and say thank you.

## At the end of the day

Kindness is one of the first things to go at the end of a long day. By the time the bed is found and the boots are off, there is often very little left in the tank for strangers, and the person asking if the other bunk is taken can receive a shorter answer than they deserved. If that was you, it was not a character flaw. It was a long day of walking.

Start with what you were given. A direction offered without being asked. A bit of tape, or a plaster. A seat shifted along a bench. Someone holding a door, or pointing out a puddle. These things arrive so lightly that they are easy to receive without noticing, and the day goes by with a good deal of unpaid thanks outstanding. If you can remember one, you could say thank you to it now, in your head, to a face you may not see again. It is a small payment and it goes a long way.

Think, too, of what you used today without knowing who made it possible. The path under your feet. The arrow that kept you right. The bed you are lying in, with its blanket and its pillow. Someone made each of those, or mended them, or kept them going, and you will probably never meet them. A road is built, all the way along, out of small acts by people who are not there. It might be good to be glad of them for a moment.

Then what did you pass? Think of the straws that were at the edge of your day. Not to scold yourself, only to find out what stopped you. Sometimes it was hurry, and sometimes it was shyness. Sometimes it was a plain fatigue that left nothing to give, and that deserves the same tenderness as anything else. Often it was only that you did not see. Seeing is a skill, and it gets better with use.

Lawrence's "not even His gifts" can be a quiet evening question. Was there a moment today when you did something kind and noticed that you were waiting for it to be returned? You do not need to answer. It is enough to be able to notice.

Receiving is a kindness too. A person who lets you help them, or who takes the offered plaster without a speech, has given you something, and it is worth remembering that you can give it back by receiving well.

If the day left you nothing to give, then nothing was owed. Some days you are the person being carried, and that is as much a part of a road as the carrying.

Then, if you are up to it, set one small act for the morning, and tell nobody. Not a grand intention. A straw. If the morning gets in the way, it will keep.

The questions for tonight:

- What small kindness did I receive today without saying thank you?
- Was there something small I passed by, and what stopped me?
- What is one thing I could do tomorrow that no one will see?

## A short prayer

*For the small kindnesses I was given and did not notice, thank you. For the ones I passed by, forgive me. Make my hands quick and my attention kind. Let me do one little thing well tomorrow, and not need to be thanked. Rest.*
