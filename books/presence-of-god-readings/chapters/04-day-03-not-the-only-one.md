# Day 3. Not the only one

*When your mind has run off, how do you walk back?*

## The passage

> You tell me nothing new; you are not the only one that is troubled with wandering thoughts. Our mind is extremely roving; but, as the will is mistress of all our faculties, she must recall them, and carry them to GOD as their last end. … Let it be *your* business to keep your mind in the presence of the LORD. If it sometimes wander and withdraw itself from Him, do not much disquiet yourself for that: trouble and disquiet serve rather to distract the mind than to re-collect it: the will must bring it back in tranquility.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Eighth Letter (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

At some point today you may well walk past a turn. The waymark was there, a yellow arrow on a wall or a post, and you were somewhere else: inside an argument from last spring, or composing a reply you will never send, or deciding what to eat. A few hundred metres on, something is off. The path has no arrows. You stop. You turn round. Nothing is lost but ten minutes and a small amount of dignity, and you walk back to the place where you stopped paying attention.

The mind does this all day, and Lawrence knew it well. The edition we use heads this letter with a note, "Concerning wandering thoughts in Prayer", and the letter itself is a reply to someone who has confessed to the problem. Look at how it begins. "You tell me nothing new." Is there a kinder way to meet a person who is ashamed? He does not sigh, or quote a rule. He says: you are not the only one. The mind roves, and it roves in everybody.

Then he gives what we would call his method, and it is gentler than the word suggests. The will, he says, is "mistress of all our faculties", and "she" must recall them and carry them to God as their "last end", the place everything is meant to arrive. For Lawrence that is God; for a walker it can be as plain as where you are going today. (In his day the faculties were the powers of the mind, such as memory and understanding, and the will was the part of you that chooses.) The picture is of a person calling back a flock, not of a guard with a stick. And notice that the calling is done "in tranquility". The last sentence of the passage is the one worth underlining: "trouble and disquiet serve rather to distract the mind than to re-collect it."

In plain words: the scolding is a second wandering. When you find that you have drifted, the drift is not the thing that takes you furthest from where you meant to be. The self-reproach is. It adds a lap of the same field.

The phone is a very efficient wanderer. It can take your mind to eleven places before the next village. This is not a sermon about phones. Lawrence had none, and his mind roved like anyone's. The phone only makes the roving easier to do and harder to notice.

He did not preach this from a height. In the Second Conversation the note-taker reports that at the beginning he often spent the time set aside for prayer "rejecting wandering thoughts, and falling back into them." A man whose whole book is about attention spent whole sittings failing at it. In the Third Conversation he is said to have gone a good while without thinking of God and not to have disquieted himself, but to have owned up and returned, "with so much the greater trust in Him". The return is not a penalty. It is the practice itself.

It is hard in two places.

The first is the sentence "the will must bring it back". Some days it cannot. The mind is held by something heavy: grief, an illness at home, a dread about what is waiting when the road ends. Then the wandering is not a lapse. It is weather. On those days the passage is not an instruction, only a permission: do not add reproach to a weight you are already carrying.

The second is that not all wandering is waste. Lawrence is speaking of one particular practice, keeping his mind in God's presence, and the passage does not tell you to stop thinking about your life. On a long walk a good deal of what the mind does is sorting and remembering and rehearsing, and that may be the very work you came out to do. A walking mind is allowed to walk. What the passage offers is a way back for the moments when you wanted to be somewhere and were not. You are the only one who can tell which moments those are.

One more word, since the letter is about God: "the presence of the LORD" is the place Lawrence wants his mind to return to. If that place is not one you can name, the turn still works at its simplest. You come back to the road under your feet, which is also a place, and is also always there.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? That you treat each return as a small success and not a small defeat. The arrow was missed, the arrow was found. The finding is the walking.

## For the walk

- Where did my mind go in the last ten minutes, and can I bring it back without a word of blame?
- Whenever you notice you have drifted, to the phone, a worry, a tune that will not stop, let the noticing count as a success. Touch the strap of your pack or take one slow breath, and come back to the path. Count the returns, not the drifts. If it helps, give each return a one-word name, such as "back". By evening you may have more than you expect. If you lose count, that is only another drift, and you know what to do with those.

## At the end of the day

By evening the mind is as tired as the legs, and it wanders further. That is worth knowing before you grade yourself. A mind at nine at night is not the mind that set out at eight, and it would be unfair to ask the same of it.

Think back over where it went today. Some of the places will be forgettable: a stretch of road spent with a stuck song, a long argument with a person who was not there. Others will have been returned to, over and over, like a tongue to a sore tooth. A particular worry. A particular face. If there was something your mind kept going back to, it may not have been a distraction at all. It may have been trying to tell you something you did not have time to hear while you were walking. Tonight, with the light low and nothing to do, you could give it a minute of the attention it was asking for. Not a solution. Only a hearing.

Then think about how you spoke to yourself when you noticed you had strayed. There is a tone people take with their own minds that they would never take with a friend: *there you go again.* Lawrence offers another. *You are not the only one.* If you can hear the second voice, even faintly, try saying it once, to yourself, as you would to someone you were fond of.

And what helped you come back? It is useful to know. For some people it is a sound, a bell or a dog or the scrape of a stick. For some it is a step that landed badly. For some it is a person who said good morning. The things that call us back are often small and often outside us, and a road is full of them. You may find that you have a few that work for you.

The same letter has an image for the end of a day like this, and it may fit better than any advice. Lawrence tells his reader to hold themselves before God "like a dumb or paralytic beggar at a rich man's gate". ("Dumb" in his English means unable to speak.) A person who cannot speak and cannot move can do nothing but be there, and being there is, as we read him, the whole of the business. If you have no words tonight and no energy, you are well placed. You may be nearer to the gate than you were at noon.

If sleep will not come and the mind is running, do not fight it. Treat each time you notice as a return. You are practising in the dark, and it counts as much as it did at noon.

Do not sit up long with this. A tired mind will not sort itself out in the dark, and there is a lot to be said for sleeping on it.

The questions for tonight:

- Where did my mind keep going today, and what was it asking for?
- How did I speak to myself when I noticed I had drifted?
- What helped me come back: a sound, a step, a person, a breath?

## A short prayer

*I wandered today, and here I am. No speeches. Take what was scattered and gather it gently. Let me be as patient with my mind as You are. Tomorrow I will lose the way again, and again find it. Stay near.*
