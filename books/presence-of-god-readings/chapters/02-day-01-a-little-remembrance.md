# Day 1. A little remembrance

*What is the least you need to set out in good company?*

## The passage

> He requires no great matters of us; a little remembrance of Him from time to time; a little adoration; sometimes to pray for His grace, sometimes to offer Him your sufferings, and sometimes to return Him thanks for the favors He has given you, and still gives you, in the midst of your troubles, and to console yourself with Him the oftenest you can. Lift up your heart to Him, sometimes even at your meals, and when you are in company: the least little remembrance will always be acceptable to Him. You need not cry very loud; He is nearer to us than we are aware of. … Every one is capable of such familiar conversation with GOD, some more, some less: He knows what we can do. Let us begin, then. Perhaps He expects but one generous resolution on our part. Have courage.
>
> — Brother Lawrence, *The Practice of the Presence of God*, Seventh Letter (anonymous translation from the French, Fleming H. Revell Company edition)

## Reflection

On the first morning the list in your head is probably long. Water, the charger, tape for the heel, whether the shoes will do, whether the first day is too long or too short. A pack that was sorted last night gets sorted again at the door. There is nothing wrong with that. A road is a lot to begin, and a list is how the mind holds a lot.

Brother Lawrence has a list too, and it is shorter than any you will write today. It comes from a letter to someone the book does not name, someone who seems to be in trouble, since the letter opens with "I pity you much." And it begins with what is not being asked. The "He" of the passage is God, whose name this old print sets in capitals. God, says the letter, "requires no great matters of us". A little remembrance now and then. A little adoration, which you may read as a moment of plain wonder or thanks. A word asked for, an ache offered, a thank-you, a bit of comfort taken. Lift up your heart, he says, even at a meal, even in company. There is no need to shout. God is nearer than we know.

Notice the verbs in that list: to ask, to offer, to thank. You will probably do all three before lunch without calling them anything. You ask the hill to end. You put up with a heel. You are grateful for a patch of shade. Lawrence only suggests that these small movements of the heart have somewhere to go, and that going there takes no more than a moment.

Then, after a line we will come back to, comes a sentence that is kind to everybody. Every one is capable of this familiar conversation, "some more, some less". The letter does not say how much. It says only that God knows what we can do, and then it says the best thing a letter can say to a person on a doorstep: *let us begin, then.*

This suits a first day for two reasons.

The first is scale. The first day of a road is when resolutions are biggest. You will walk every step in silence. You will stop at every church. You will give up the phone. By the third day a resolution of that size has met a blister. A resolution of the size Lawrence asks, one small remembrance, now and then, can survive a blister, not because it is easy but because there is so little of it to break.

The second is place. The lines hidden by the ellipsis say that to be with God you need not always be at church. You may make a small chapel of your heart, a word he gives in its old form, an *oratory*, and slip into it from time to time. On a road that passes churches which may be shut, and long stretches that pass none at all, that thought travels well. The chapel is not a building you reach by evening. You carry it.

Where is it hard? At the very first word. "Him" is God, and Lawrence is not offering a way to feel calm. He is describing a friendship with someone he is sure is there. If you are not sure, or are sure of the opposite, you can read this as a stranger's kind letter and keep what fits: the smallness, the permission to begin badly, the idea that attention is something you can give a little at a time. Do that knowing it is a borrowing.

It is hard, too, that the letter offers no cure. "In the midst of your troubles" is as far as it goes. The remembrance is company in the trouble, not a door out of it. Anyone who has carried something heavy onto a road will know the difference, and may find the plainness of it a relief.

And there is the age of the writer. A little further on in the same letter he says "I am almost eighty". A man of that age may well have stopped waiting for the right moment to start, and the shortness of his advice suggests it.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Very little, and that is the point. One remembrance in the first hour. Another when you sit down for water or coffee. Do not count them, do not grade them. Let them be late, and plain, and a little clumsy. A remembrance is not a performance. If it takes one breath, it is finished.

## For the walk

- What is the first place today where I could stop for the length of one breath?
- When you first sit down, before you reach for the phone or the food, stay still for one breath and say, quietly or silently, "Here I am." Say it to God if that is your word. If it is not, say it to the morning, or to yourself, or to whoever is nearest. Then go on. The shorter it is, the better it keeps. If you forget until noon, do it at noon.

## At the end of the day

Boots off. Whatever kind of day it was, the evening is when your feet make their report, and they should be heard first, before any other part of you has an opinion. Sit for a minute with them. Drink some water, eat something, see to whatever needs seeing to. The rest of this can wait ten minutes. It will not be offended.

The first evening of a road can feel like a verdict on the whole plan: too tired, too far, too much. It is not a verdict. A first day is mostly your body finding out what you have asked of it, and it will have a good deal to say.

Now look back over the day with the letter in mind. How many little remembrances were there? If the honest answer is none, that is a fair first count and nothing to be ashamed of. You set out with a list, a pack and a body to look after, and a day on foot is very full of the next thing. If the answer is one or two, a pause on a bench, a moment when you noticed the light and were glad to be here, then keep those. They are the whole of what the letter asks.

Think also about what you carried that you did not need. Some of it was in the pack, and you may find out tonight by lifting it. Some of it was in your head: a worry about the days to come, a plan for the whole road, a comparison with how other people seem to be doing. Lawrence's list is short because he had put down most of the other one. You do not have to put yours down tonight. You only have to see what is on it.

And remember why you came. People set out for all sorts of reasons: a promise, a grief, a question, a rest, a curiosity that would not go away. Reasons get buried under logistics by the second hour, under water and weather and where to sleep. The evening is a fair time to dig yours out and see that it is still there. It does not need to be a grand one. "I wanted to see if I could" is a complete reason, and so is "I did not know what else to do."

If there is someone at home on your mind tonight, you could offer them a remembrance too, as small as the letter's: one kind sentence, said inwardly, with no message sent. It costs nothing, and it keeps them company.

If the God of the letter is a question for you, tonight is not the time to answer it. Be tired in peace. One plain sentence will do: *I was here today.*

The questions for tonight:

- When, even for a moment, did I notice that I was glad to be on this road?
- What did I carry today, in the pack or in my head, that I did not need?
- Where could one small pause fit into tomorrow morning?

## A short prayer

*For the road behind me, thank you. For the road ahead, be near. I asked for no great matter today and I ask for none tonight. Keep my feet. Keep my small resolution. Let me sleep, and begin again in the morning.*
