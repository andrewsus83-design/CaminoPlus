# Day 8. Take up and read

*What are you putting off until tomorrow?*

## The passage

> For I said with myself, "Be it done now, be it done now." And as I spake, I all but enacted it: I all but did it, and did it not: yet sunk not back to my former state, but kept my stand hard by, and took breath. … I cast myself down I know not how, under a certain fig-tree, giving full vent to my tears; and the floods of mine eyes gushed out an acceptable sacrifice to Thee. … So was I speaking and weeping in the most bitter contrition of my heart, when, lo! I heard from a neighbouring house a voice, as of boy or girl, I know not, chanting, and oft repeating, "Take up and read; Take up and read." Instantly, my countenance altered, I began to think most intently whether children were wont in any kind of play to sing such words: nor could I remember ever to have heard the like. So checking the torrent of my tears, I arose; interpreting it to be no other than a command from God to open the book, and read the first chapter I should find. … No further would I read; nor needed I: for instantly at the end of this sentence, by a light as it were of serenity infused into my heart, all the darkness of doubt vanished away.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 8 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

At the foot of a long climb there is a moment when you say, quietly, right, now. You look up at the slope. You adjust a strap. You take a drink you do not need. Then you say it again, and a minute has gone, and your feet have not moved. Nobody is watching, and nothing is wrong with your legs. It is the smallest version of a very old difficulty.

The eighth book of the *Confessions* is the book of that difficulty, at the largest size. By now Augustine has been searching for years. He has read, listened, doubted and half agreed, and in this book he sits in the little garden of his lodging with his friend Alypius, and something has to be decided. In the pages before the passage he tries to say what is happening in him, and the sentence he finds is one of the best in the book: *The mind commands the mind, its own self, to will, and yet it doth not.* A hand will move when it is told. A will does not always move when it is told, because part of it is willing and part is not. He calls it *partly to will, partly to nill*, and says it is a sort of sickness of the mind. Anyone who has ever meant to begin something and not begun it knows what he means.

The passage opens in the middle of the struggle. *Be it done now, be it done now*, he says to himself, and then: *I all but did it, and did it not.* He gets close to the edge and stops, but he does not go back either. In his words, he *kept my stand hard by, and took breath*. That is almost funny, and entirely true. Most of what we call putting something off is exactly this: standing close to the edge, breathing.

He goes away from Alypius to be alone, and throws himself down under a fig tree, and weeps. Then comes the oddest moment in the book. From a house nearby he hears a voice, *as of boy or girl, I know not*, singing over and over: *Take up and read; Take up and read.* He tries to think whether children chant such words in any game, and cannot remember that they do. So he takes it as a command to open the book that he has left lying by Alypius, which holds the letters of the apostle Paul, and to read the first passage his eyes fall on.

The passage then skips the lines that he reads, and it is right that you should know what they are. They are a few sentences of Paul, which Augustine copies out. They begin *Not in rioting and drunkenness*, they list behaviour to leave behind, and they end by telling the reader to *put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ*. He does not read on. *No further would I read; nor needed I.* At the end of the sentence, he says, a light of calm came into his heart and *all the darkness of doubt vanished away*.

Two things in this are hard. The first is the struggle itself. In the pages before the passage, what Augustine fights is mostly desire and the habits of a life he wants to leave, understood in the terms of a fourth-century Christian, and it is not a language that many readers share. We have kept to the part everyone can recognise: the divided will, the delay and the turning. If the rest sits badly with you, you are not alone and need not argue with him about it.

The second is the voice and the book. It is easy to hear this as a conjuring trick: a child's song, a page opened at random, a life changed. Nobody should settle a life by opening a book at a chance place, and Augustine does not. The scene comes at the end of years. He is also not sure where the voice came from. All he says is that he could not remember a game with such words. What changes him is not the verse as such. It is that he had been ready for a long time, and something finally gave him a reason to stop standing at the edge.

On an ordinary day, this comes down to something very small. The task is to find what you are all but doing, name it, and then look for its smallest piece. Not the whole of the matter. A message to be written, a number to look up, a hard word to be said to a person at home. And look, too, at who is sitting beside you. Alypius waited, in silence, close by, while his friend struggled. That counts for more than it seems.

## For the walk

- What am I all but doing, and what is the smallest first piece of it?
- Take that smallest piece, and do it before today's walk is over: a message, a call, a decision to ask. If it is too big for the day, cut it in half. Then walk on and see how the rest of the day feels with the thing begun.

## At the end of the day

Do not decide anything tonight. That is the first piece of advice, and perhaps the most useful. A body that has walked a long way, and a mind that has taken in a long day, are bad judges of large things. Augustine's garden scene happens in the middle of a day, with a friend close by and a night's sleep still ahead. Anything of yours that needs deciding deserves the same: daylight, company and a night's sleep still to come.

So, only a look back. Did you take the small step? If you did, notice how it was. It may have been over very quickly, and felt out of proportion to the weeks of worry. That is a common way of such things. The step is often smaller than the wait.

If you did not, be gentle. *All but* is a respectable place to stand. Augustine stood there for a long time and wrote a book about it. Look at what held you back, as honestly as you can, and without any scolding. Was it fear? Tiredness? A reasonable doubt? Or just the long habit of the old way? Naming it is a step of its own.

Think also about the tomorrow of the passage. He tells us that he asked himself *How long, how long*, and then *tomorrow, and tomorrow*, and the question is also yours. There is a good tomorrow and a bad one. The good one is a real day, with a place in it for a small thing already chosen. The bad one is a name we give to never.

Then think of the voices that arrived unasked. A child calling across a yard, a bell, a stranger's passing remark that landed somewhere odd. None of them need be treated as a command. Augustine himself hesitates over the voice he heard. But a day with open ears gathers more than a day with closed ones, and it is a modest kind of attention to go back over what reached you without being asked.

And who sat beside you? On a road there is nearly always someone, a fellow walker, a person on the phone, a face on the next bunk. They may not know that they were doing anything. Tonight you might thank one of them, in a few words, for the silence they kept while you struggled.

If you took the small step and the day felt lighter after it, take note. Lightness is a kind of information. It does not prove that the step was right, but it tells you something about the weight of the delay, which was probably heavier than the deed.

A word about weeping. Augustine goes away to be alone for it, and Alypius, astonished, lets him go. If today brought tears, from a struggle or from nothing you can name, they belong in the day as much as the kilometres do.

- What did I all but do today?
- What would I do now if I were not waiting to feel ready?
- Who sat beside me, in silence or in kindness, while something in me struggled?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I stand where I stood this morning, close to the edge, breathing. I ask for no sudden light. Give me one small step, and the courage to take it, and a friend beside me while I do. Forgive me the days I only stood. Let me sleep, and wake to a real tomorrow.*
