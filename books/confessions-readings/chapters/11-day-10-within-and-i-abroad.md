# Day 10. Within, and I abroad

*Where did you walk today, and where were you?*

## The passage

> And I come to the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images, brought into it from things of all sorts perceived by the senses. … And men go abroad to admire the heights of mountains, the mighty billows of the sea, the broad tides of rivers, the compass of the ocean, and the circuits of the stars, and pass themselves by; nor wonder that when I spake of all these things, I did not see them with mine eyes, yet could not have spoken of them, unless I then actually saw the mountains, billows, rivers, stars which I had seen, and that ocean which I believe to be, inwardly in my memory, and that, with the same vast spaces between, as if I saw them abroad. … Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new! too late I loved Thee! And behold, Thou wert within, and I abroad, and there I searched for Thee; deformed I, plunging amid those fair forms which Thou hadst made. Thou wert with me, but I was not with Thee. Things held me far from Thee, which, unless they were in Thee, were not at all. Thou calledst, and shoutedst, and burstest my deafness. Thou flashedst, shonest, and scatteredst my blindness. Thou breathedst odours, and I drew in breath and panted for Thee. I tasted, and hunger and thirst. Thou touchedst me, and I burned for Thy peace.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 10 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

Lie down at the end of a mountain day and close your eyes, and the hill is still there. The bend where the path doubled back. The colour of the rock at the pass. The cold of a stream on your wrists. None of it is in front of you, and all of it can be seen, with its distances intact. Where, exactly, is it?

The tenth book of the *Confessions* begins with that question. The first nine books were the story of a life, from the cradle to the death of his mother. Now the story stops, and Augustine says plainly what he will do instead. He will tell his reader, he writes, *not what I have been, but what I now am and what I yet am*. The rest of the book looks inward, and he starts with memory.

This is a hard stretch for a first-time reader. Books ten to thirteen are philosophy and scripture more than story, and a reader who loved the pear tree and the garden may feel the ground change underfoot. Memory is the easiest of the four to enter, and the passage gives only a taste of it.

He calls it a country. *I come to the fields and spacious palaces of my memory, where are the treasures of innumerable images*, he says, *brought into it from things of all sorts perceived by the senses.* He walks into it as you might walk into a house: fields, palaces, rooms behind rooms. In the pages that follow he wonders at it for a long time. In *darkness and silence* he can call up colours at will; he can recall a song with his mouth shut. Nobody put the images in a drawer. They are simply there, with the vast spaces between them, as if he saw them abroad.

Then comes a sentence that a mountain walker is likely to feel. *And men go abroad to admire the heights of mountains, the mighty billows of the sea, the broad tides of rivers, the compass of the ocean, and the circuits of the stars, and pass themselves by.* It stings, a little, as the first day's sentence about feet did. The sentence does not say that you should not admire a mountain. It says that people travel far to wonder at a mountain and never turn to wonder at the thing that holds it. Augustine finds the mountain he remembers, inside, with its distances intact. You are the place where the mountains are kept.

Next, the line for which the whole book is perhaps best known. *Too late loved I Thee, O Thou Beauty of ancient days, yet ever new!* It is a cry, and also a thanksgiving. He is late, and he has been found. *Thou wert within, and I abroad, and there I searched for Thee; deformed I, plunging amid those fair forms which Thou hadst made.* The thing he was looking for far off was nearer than the search. Then the rhythm quickens, and all five senses arrive in turn: *Thou calledst, and shoutedst*, for the ears, *Thou flashedst, shonest*, for the eyes, *Thou breathedst odours*, for the nose, *I tasted*, and *Thou touchedst me*. It is a reminder that he did not meet what he sought by thinking alone. You have used all five of yours today.

Three things need saying. The first is that it is a sharp thing to be told, in a book that goes with a road, that the road may be the wrong place to look. Take it as half a sentence. The other half is that the road may be the best place to begin looking in, since a long walk empties the head of its usual noise. The second is that *deformed* is harsh. Augustine's picture of himself plunging among the fair things of the world and not seeing their source can feel like a verdict on loving the world at all. Read the next lines as a correction: the beauty was a real beauty, only not the last one. The third is that it is addressed to God, and a reader who does not share that may hear *Thou wert within* as a puzzle. One way in is this: whatever you are looking for out there is perhaps nearer than the looking.

So what might it change in a day? A modest thing. To look at something long enough that it goes in. And, from time to time, to turn from the view to the one who is viewing.

## For the walk

- What am I passing by, in myself, while I look at the mountains?
- Choose one thing on the road, a rock face, a gate, a bend of a stream, and look at it for a full minute, as if you will be asked tonight to describe it. Then walk on and see how much of it you can find this evening, with your eyes closed. You do not need to remember it perfectly. The point is to look at one thing properly.

## At the end of the day

Here is the evening's work, and it is lighter than it sounds. Lie down, if you can, and close your eyes. Then walk the day again, without moving.

Start with the first hour. A door, a step, the cold air. Let the images arrive in their order, and do not force them. Then the middle: a long slope, a bend, a village, a meal. Notice which pictures come up bright and which have already gone grey. The ones that come back brightly are the ones you were really looking at. The grey ones are the ones you passed by.

Augustine is amazed that the mind can do this, and tells us that in darkness and silence he can summon colour at will. You may find the same. If a place has stayed with you in such detail that you could walk it again, give it a moment's thanks. If very little has stayed, that is not a failure, and it is useful to know. Perhaps you were looking at your own feet, or at your own thoughts, and the mountains were only behind them.

Think of the five senses. Which of them had the best day? A road like this feeds them all. Cold water on the tongue, wood smoke or cut grass in the air, the sound of bells or a stream, the pull of the slope in your legs, the colour of the hills as the light moves. If one was missing today, because you were too tired or too hurried or too far inside your own head, you might give it a little more room tomorrow.

Then turn the same attention round, as the passage suggests, from the view to the one who views. Ask in plain words how you are. Not what you did, but how it is in there. Tired, certainly. Hungry, perhaps. Glad, or low, or somewhere between. Give each of those a single word and leave it at that. It is the nearest thing to an inward walk that a worn-out evening allows, and it takes less than a minute.

*Too late* is not a sentence for tonight. You are not too late for anything this evening, except supper. Augustine wrote that cry as a man who had been found, and a cry of that kind looks back with relief and does not sulk. If you find something within yourself tonight that you wish you had noticed years ago, treat it as welcome.

Tiredness has a plain use here. A body that has finished its work leaves a good deal of room for memory, and sleep does a good deal of the filing of what you have gathered, whether or not you approve.

- What did I take in today that I would like to keep?
- Where was I abroad today, in my head or my phone, while something waited within?
- Which of my five senses had the best day, and which needs more room tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I close my eyes and walk the day again. Thank you for what I saw, and for what I only half saw. Let me not pass myself by. Whatever I am looking for, let it be nearer than my looking. Keep what I have gathered, and let me sleep.*
