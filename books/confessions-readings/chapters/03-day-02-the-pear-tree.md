# Day 2. The pear tree

*What do you do in company that you would never do alone?*

## The passage

> A pear tree there was near our vineyard, laden with fruit, tempting neither for colour nor taste. To shake and rob this, some lewd young fellows of us went, late one night (having according to our pestilent custom prolonged our sports in the streets till then), and took huge loads, not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs, having only tasted them. And this, but to do what we liked only, because it was misliked. … Behold my God, before Thee, the vivid remembrance of my soul; alone, I had never committed that theft wherein what I stole pleased me not, but that I stole; nor had it alone liked me to do it, nor had I done it. O friendship too unfriendly! thou incomprehensible inveigler of the soul, thou greediness to do mischief out of mirth and wantonness, thou thirst of others' loss, without lust of my own gain or revenge: but when it is said, "Let's go, let's do it," we are ashamed not to be shameless.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 2 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

It is two in the afternoon, and the sun sits on the back of your neck. The group ahead has just agreed to push on to the next village. Your legs had already agreed to stop at this one. You go on. Nobody asked you to, and nobody would have minded if you had stayed. Something in the air of a group did the asking.

That is a very mild form of what Augustine writes about in the second book of the *Confessions*, and his example is a theft of pears.

He is in his sixteenth year. There is a pear tree near the family vineyard, and the fruit is *tempting neither for colour nor taste*. Late one night, after hours of roaming the streets, a gang of lads shakes the tree and carries off great loads of pears. They do not eat them. They throw them to the pigs, having only tasted a few. The whole point, he says, was to do what they liked *because it was misliked*.

The detail that stays is the pigs. A crowd of young men, in the dark, shakes a tree for fruit they do not want and flings it to animals that will eat it without a word of thanks. It would be funny if he did not mean it so seriously.

What troubles him, decades later, is that there was no reason. People do wrong for money, for power, for revenge, out of fear, and he goes through the list in the pages around this one. None of it applied. He had better pears at home. He was not hungry. The fruit gave him no pleasure, and the only sweetness in it was the wrongdoing. *It was foul, and I loved it*, he says, which is about as blunt as a sentence can be. So he asks himself the question that makes the passage worth reading: why did I do it? And he finds an answer he did not expect. It was the company. *alone, I had never committed that theft*, he writes, and then he turns on the very thing he valued most: *O friendship too unfriendly!*

The line the whole day hangs on comes last: *but when it is said, "Let's go, let's do it," we are ashamed not to be shameless.* It is not wickedness that moves the lads, as he sees it. It is embarrassment. Nobody wants to be the one who says no.

There are two honest doubts to set beside this. The first is proportion. To most readers now, a night of stolen fruit by teenage boys is small stuff, and the weight Augustine gives it can look out of scale. Much of the second book is built on it. He seems to choose a small wrong on purpose, one with no excuse of hunger or gain, so that he can look at something in himself without anything else in the way. You do not have to share his verdict to find his observation sound.

The second doubt is about who is speaking. The boy is seen by a bishop in his forties, who has had a long time to be hard on him. He is both the only witness and the prosecutor. Take the story as one man's portrait of himself, told in his own severe light, and hold the severity a little loosely.

The pull of a crowd does not only run towards harm. The instinct that makes a gang of lads shake a tree is the instinct that makes a table of strangers pass the bread along, or a whole group slow down for the one who is limping. Augustine is not telling you to distrust company. He is asking where it is carrying you, and whether you chose the direction.

A road with many walkers on it has a great many small crowds, forming and dissolving all day: the early start everyone seems to be making, the daily distance, the rumour of a good place to eat. Each one makes a decision within earshot of you. What does the passage ask of an ordinary day? Mostly attention to the small *let's go* moments, before they have finished happening. In each there is a half second in which you could ask whether you want this. You may find that you do. Being carried along is not automatically wrong. It is only unexamined until you have looked.

## For the walk

- Whose pace am I walking at today, and whose pace did I choose?
- At the next decision the group makes, about where to stop, when to eat or how far to go, say to yourself what you want before you hear what anyone else wants. Then notice whether you say it aloud. If you are walking alone, the crowd may be in your head, in the walkers you passed yesterday and the pace you think you ought to keep. Notice that crowd too.

## At the end of the day

Pack straps leave marks that are faint and a little sore, and so do group decisions. The evening is when you find them. Look back over the day for the moments when a plan was made around you.

Some of those were good. A walker you had never met shortened a stretch so that you could keep up. Somebody made room at a crowded table. Nothing about being in company was a burden for an hour, and you may be a little surprised how warm that feels.

Some of them were hard. You went on when you needed to stop, or said yes to a drink you did not want, or laughed at a remark that sat badly. If you can find one, give it a name. You do not need to be hard on yourself about it. The boy in the passage has a grown man to be hard on him for everyone, and he does not need your help.

Then turn the question over, and look for the quieter half of Augustine's line. We are ashamed not to be shameless. Was there a moment today when you were a little ashamed to be decent? You might have swallowed a thank you because the mood was dry. You might have let a joke go past that you would have liked to answer. You might have wanted to ask the quiet walker to join the table, and not done it, because nobody else had. Those are the small pear trees. Nothing is stolen, and still something is lost.

Ask the other question too. Where did you pull someone else along today? You may have been the one who said that the next village was not far. You may have been the one who set a pace without asking whether it suited the others. A crowd is made of people, and today you were one of them.

One more look, and a kinder one. Think of the person whose company made the day lighter: the one who made you laugh at the wrong moment, or handed you a sweet at the right one. You need not know the name. A day with such a person in it is a good day, whatever else it held, and the passage is not an argument against them. It is only a reminder to notice what your company is doing to you, the way you notice what a new pair of boots is doing to your feet.

Honest tiredness is useful here. A tired body is easy to lead, and the afternoon's agreements were probably made with the least of you present. If that is true, tomorrow's first decision, made with a rested mind, is your chance to take one back.

- When did I say yes today because everyone else was saying it?
- Where was I a little ashamed to be kind, and what did I do about it?
- Whom did I carry along with me today, without asking?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I thank you for the company I kept. Where it carried me well, let me remember it. Where I went along without choosing, forgive me, and help me choose tomorrow. Let me be someone who can say yes and no. Keep my feet honest and my mind awake.*
