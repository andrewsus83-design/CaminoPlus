# Day 13. Our rest is our place

*What carries you, and where does it set you down?*

## The passage

> In Thy Gift we rest; there we enjoy Thee. Our rest is our place. Love lifts us up thither, and Thy good Spirit lifts up our lowliness from the gates of death. In Thy good pleasure is our peace. The body by its own weight strives towards its own place. Weight makes not downward only, but to his own place. Fire tends upward, a stone downward. They are urged by their own weight, they seek their own places. … When out of their order, they are restless; restored to order, they are at rest. My weight, is my love; thereby am I borne, whithersoever I am borne. We are inflamed, by Thy Gift we are kindled; and are carried upwards; we glow inwardly, and go forwards. … O Lord God, give peace unto us: (for Thou hast given us all things;) the peace of rest, the peace of the Sabbath, which hath no evening. … Let it be asked of Thee, sought in Thee, knocked for at Thee; so, so shall it be received, so shall it be found, so shall it be opened. Amen.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 13 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

At the end of a walking day, you take off the pack. Do it slowly, once, and notice what happens. For a moment your shoulders do not know what to do with themselves. Your whole body leans a little towards the nearest bench, like a stone towards the ground. A weight that you had stopped feeling has gone, and what was under it, tiredness, comes up to say hello. Then you sit, and the body settles as though it had been looking for that exact spot all day.

The last book of the *Confessions* has a picture for this. Augustine is thinking about why anything goes anywhere, and he answers with weight. *The body by its own weight strives towards its own place.* Fire tends upward, a stone downward. Each thing is urged by its weight to seek its place. *When out of their order, they are restless; restored to order, they are at rest.*

That is a word you have met before. On the first day of this book, he said *our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee*, and here, on the last day, the same thought comes round again. Restlessness is a thing out of its place. Rest is arrival. *Our rest is our place*, he says, which is a short and rather beautiful sentence. Where you rest is where you belong.

Notice what the sentence does not say. It does not make rest the opposite of the road. It makes rest the place the road arrives at, the thing you were being carried towards all along. On that picture a pilgrimage is not a flight from rest. It is the long way round to it.

Then the line that matters most: *My weight, is my love; thereby am I borne, whithersoever I am borne.* On Day 7 the word to keep was *weight*, because it pulled Augustine down. Here it has turned. It may be that the weight that drags and the weight that carries are one thing seen from two ends. What you love is what carries you. It gets you up in the morning and takes you over the hill, and it is also the thing that will carry you to the place where you put the pack down. He says that *we glow inwardly, and go forwards*, which is as good a description of a walker as any in the book.

The passage then jumps to the end of the whole work. Book thirteen, the one we are in, reads the days of creation in Genesis as a long picture of the Church and the soul, and it is hard going. We have left all that. We join it as it finishes, with a prayer: *give peace unto us*, *the peace of rest, the peace of the Sabbath, which hath no evening.* Of the seven days of Genesis, the first six end with evening and morning. The seventh does not. Augustine seizes on that, and reads it as a day that does not close. A day that does not have to end because the work is done.

And the last sentence of the book is not a conclusion. It is a request. *Let it be asked of Thee, sought in Thee, knocked for at Thee; so, so shall it be received, so shall it be found, so shall it be opened. Amen.* Thirteen books of questions, and the final word is to go on asking.

Three honest difficulties. The first is the physics. Fire going up and stones going down because of their natural places is ancient science, and it is no longer how we explain falling. Keep the picture and let the science go. Water finds its level, and so, he is saying, does a heart. The second is that *My weight, is my love* does not tell you whether the thing you love is good for you. It says only that love carries you somewhere. The question of what to love is left to you, and it is the real question. The third is the Sabbath. Augustine means the rest of eternal life, after all our works. For a tired person in a bunk, that may feel a long way off. The passage gives a taste before then, in the lines about the gift in which we rest and the peace that goes with it. It does not promise that you will feel it tonight.

So how might this go into a day? Loosely. By noticing what you love enough to carry, and by letting yourself rest when you arrive. A walker who cannot rest has not finished walking. And by ending, as the book does, with a question left open.

## For the walk

- What carries me? What do I love, that gets me over the next hill?
- At your next stop, take the pack off slowly and sit. Before you reach for a drink, a phone or a map, let your body find its rest for one full minute. Notice where it settles. That settling is Augustine's picture, in a small way, at work in you.

## At the end of the day

This is the last evening of the book, and it may or may not be the evening of the end of your road. Either way, it is a good one for resting.

The body knows how. A day on foot has done its weighing, and it will tell you the answer in the usual way: heavy legs, loose shoulders, a slow wish for horizontal. Tonight there is nothing to improve. Augustine's Sabbath is a day without an evening, but yours has one, and it is a good one. Let it be the kind of evening that does not ask you to finish anything.

Look back, quite gently, at what carried you. A person, a hope, a stubbornness, a hunger for the next village. It does not have to be grand. For some of us it is a plain wish to see the next view, or to finish what we started. If you were carried today by something you love, name it. If you were carried only by habit, or by the group, or by fear of stopping, name that too, without fuss. It is useful to know which weight is doing the work.

Then think of the nearest thing to rest you found today. It might have been a bench, or a long drink, or a few minutes when nobody wanted anything of you. It might have been the moment you took the pack off, as in the morning's reading. If you cannot think of one, make it now: sit up, put your feet flat, and let the weight of your body go into the bed.

If you want one more look, take it at the whole of the book and not only the day. Thirteen readings, thirteen evenings. Is there one that stays with you, a sentence, a scene, a question? You need not know why. Augustine is not a comfortable companion, but he is an honest one, and the day that stays is usually the one that was honest with you.

The book you have been reading for thirteen days began with a heart that could not rest and ends with a request to be given rest. It does not claim that the request has been granted. It ends in the act of asking, and tonight that is enough. You can go to sleep in the middle of a question and trust the morning to hold it.

Tiredness has come all the way with you, from the first day. It is not an enemy of the pilgrimage. It is, more often than not, the shape that the day takes when you have spent yourself on something. Honour it. If you are too tired to ask for anything, the sentence for tonight is only: thank you, and good night.

- What carried me today, and what did I love enough to keep walking for?
- Where did I find the nearest thing to rest?
- What am I asking for, as I go to sleep?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I ask for rest. I ask in the middle of the day's dust, with my boots by the bed. Let what I love carry me, and let it be worth carrying. Where I have not found my place, let me stop pretending I have. Thank you for the road. Let me sleep like one who is home.*
