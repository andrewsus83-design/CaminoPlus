# Day 4. A great riddle to myself

*Where do you take a sorrow that goes everywhere you go?*

## The passage

> At this grief my heart was utterly darkened; and whatever I beheld was death. My native country was a torment to me, and my father's house a strange unhappiness; and whatever I had shared with him, wanting him, became a distracting torture. Mine eyes sought him every where, but he was not granted them; and I hated all places, for that they had not him; nor could they now tell me, "he is coming," as when he was alive and absent. I became a great riddle to myself, and I asked my soul, why she was so sad, and why she disquieted me sorely: but she knew not what to answer me. And if I said, Trust in God, she very rightly obeyed me not; because that most dear friend, whom she had lost, was, being man, both truer and better than that phantasm she was bid to trust in. Only tears were sweet to me, for they succeeded my friend, in the dearest of my affections. … For I bore about a shattered and bleeding soul, impatient of being borne by me, yet where to repose it, I found not. … For whither should my heart flee from my heart? Whither should I flee from myself? Whither not follow myself? And yet I fled out of my country; for so should mine eyes less look for him, where they were not wont to see him.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 4 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

People walk the Camino for many reasons, and grief is one of them. It travels in the pack with the water and the spare socks, and it does not get lighter at the top of a hill. Perhaps you are carrying one. Perhaps the person on the next bench is, and you will never know. Either way, today's reading is for them.

In the fourth book of the *Confessions*, Augustine is a young teacher in his home town, and he has a friend. They grew up together, as school-fellows and play-fellows, and for a year or so the friendship has been the sweetest thing in his life. Then the friend falls ill with a fever and dies. Augustine was away when it happened.

What follows is some of the plainest writing about grief in any old book. *At this grief my heart was utterly darkened; and whatever I beheld was death.* His own town becomes a torment. His father's house is a strange unhappiness. He hates every place *for that they had not him*, and the places cannot even say what they used to say, that his friend is coming soon. Notice how small the details are. Grief in this passage is mostly a matter of eyes that keep looking at doors.

Then the line that gives the day its title: *I became a great riddle to myself.* He asks his soul why it is so sad, and it has no answer. He tells it to trust in God, and the soul, as he puts it, *very rightly obeyed me not.* This is worth a pause. A consolation offered too early, even to oneself, is not an answer, and Augustine is honest enough to say that his soul was right to refuse it. The friend, a real person, was truer and better than the vague, shapeless God he then had to trust in, which Augustine later calls a phantom. The only thing he found sweet was crying.

If you carry no such sorrow, the passage is still worth keeping, because it tells you how not to be a companion. Nobody who is grieving needs to be told to trust in anything, not at first. A walker beside you who is quiet and keeps pace, and says very little, may be doing the kindest thing on the road.

In the second stretch he looks for rest, and the verb he uses will mean something to anyone who has carried a pack: *I bore about a shattered and bleeding soul, impatient of being borne by me.* He is carrying himself, and he is impatient of the weight. He tried the usual remedies. The passage skips the list, but it is there in the text: groves, games and music, banquets, books. Nothing took the load.

So, in the last stretch, he does what a good many people have done. *And yet I fled out of my country; for so should mine eyes less look for him, where they were not wont to see him.* He left for Carthage. Before that, though, comes the question that is the best thing in the passage: *For whither should my heart flee from my heart?* You cannot walk away from the one who is walking. In the pages that follow, time and other friends patch him up, little by little, and the sorrow turns out not to have ended. It only changed its place.

Two limits are worth stating. The passage offers no consolation, and you will not find one in it. Augustine finds an answer of his own a few pages later, and it belongs to his faith. It need not be handed to anyone today. And a walk will not mend every sorrow. If what you carry is heavier than a road can lighten, tell somebody: a friend, a doctor, a counsellor, a helpline in your own country. In Spain, 112 is the emergency number for anything that cannot wait.

What is an ordinary day to do with all this? Very little. It gives leave to be a riddle to yourself. It gives leave to cry, and equally leave not to, if the tears do not come. And it suggests a small change in the way you walk with the weight. Not to flee, which he tried, and not to wrestle, but to let it walk beside you, and to put it down, now and then, on a bench.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying that I cannot put down yet? Could it walk beside me today, rather than in front?
- At a place where the path turns, say the name of someone you miss, silently or aloud. Do not try to feel anything in particular. Then walk on. If no name comes, say the name of someone you are glad of. At your next rest, take the pack off slowly and notice the moment when its weight leaves your shoulders. Let that be the whole practice.

## At the end of the day

Some evenings the body is quiet and the heart is not. If today was one of those, sit for a while with the lights low and let the day arrive.

You may have cried, in a field or a bathroom or a corner of the dormitory, with a hand over your mouth. That is allowed, and on a long road you are not the first to have done it. Or the tears may not have come, and you walked all day as if through glass. That is allowed too. Nothing in the passage says you must feel on schedule, and Augustine himself spent a long time not knowing what he felt.

Think of the places in the day that had a gap in them, like the doors in the passage. A seat across from you that nobody filled. A joke you wanted to tell someone. A view you would have liked to show to one person in particular. These are the small doors. You do not have to explain them to anyone. You only have to notice that you looked.

Think also of what was good, because grief is not the whole of any day. A mouthful of something hot. A kindness that arrived from nowhere. A moment in which you forgot, and then remembered, and found you could bear the remembering. If a good moment feels like a betrayal of the one you miss, you may have it anyway. In the very next paragraph after the first stretch, Augustine says that *time hath assuaged my wound*, a sentence written from the far side of the sorrow, and you are allowed to arrive at it in your own time.

Grief is hard work, and it is the sort of work that does not show on a map. If you are more worn out tonight than the distance can explain, this is why. Be gentle with yourself about the pace. A friend, a call, a cup of tea, a very early night: all of these are good company for a sorrow.

If you were beside someone else's sorrow today, perhaps without being told, think about what you did. Did you fill the silence, or share it? Often the whole help is in staying at the same pace.

If you did tell someone today how things really are, however briefly, notice what it was like. If you did not, ask whether there is someone you could tell tomorrow. And if a church door is open on tomorrow's road, a candle is a gentle thing to light for the one you miss, whatever you believe. It is a small and quiet way of saying a name.

- Who did I miss today, and what did I do with the missing?
- Where did I try to outwalk something, and what happened when I stopped?
- Who could I tell tonight, or tomorrow, how the day really was?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I bring the one I miss. I do not know what to say, so I will simply sit. Let the sorrow be allowed to be here. Let the day's small kindnesses be enough for tonight. Hold what I cannot carry, and let me sleep.*
