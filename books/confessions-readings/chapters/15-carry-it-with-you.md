# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the thirteen days, or you have stopped early, or you are reading them again. All three are fine. This book has no certificate to give you. It was made to keep a little company on a hard road, and a road has no use for a book once it is over, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep. Not everything. Three small things will do.

- **A word.** *Restless* opened the book and *rest* closed it, with *weight* in between. Take whichever word did the most work for you, and say it on a day when you need it.
- **A window.** The ten quiet minutes of Day 9: no talk, no music, no phone, only the day going quiet. All it needs is a wall or a doorway to stand at.
- **A question.** Some of the evening questions will have gone on turning in your mind. Keep the one that did, and do not hurry to answer it. The *Confessions* itself ends by asking.

If you keep all three for a month, you will have kept a good deal from a long book.

## How to go on

Come back whenever you like. The days are short, and a day that said little the first time may say more the fifth. You can also go to the day that fits the week you are having.

- If someone you love is out of reach, Day 3.
- If you are carrying a loss, Day 4.
- If there is something you keep putting off, Day 8.
- If an argument has gone cold, Day 12.
- If you cannot rest, Day 13.

You might also read Augustine himself. The work is in the public domain, in the same translation, at Project Gutenberg, ebook number 3296. It runs to about 110,000 words, something like eight hours of quiet reading. The first nine books tell the story and are the easiest. The last four are the hardest. Some of it is strange, and some of it will not suit you. That is allowed. A book of this kind is not a test, and a passage that asks for more than you can give, or less than you hoped, can be left where it is.

## A last word

Augustine began by saying that the heart is restless, and he finished by asking. He did not claim to have arrived. The last book is full of questions about creation and time and the meaning of a few lines of scripture, and its last sentence hands the matter to the reader: ask, seek, knock. It is an odd way to close a long book, and a good one. Nothing in it pretends that the restlessness was cured by thirteen days, or by a road.

You may or may not have reached Santiago. Either way, the road goes on from where you are. The thing you carried to the start came with you to the end, and it will come home with you, a little better known than before. That is not nothing. A heart that has been listened to is easier to live with.

Walk again when you can, even if it is only round the block with the phone left behind. And when the restlessness comes back, as it will, remember that Augustine thought it was the compass.

*Buen Camino.*
