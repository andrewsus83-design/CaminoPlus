# Day 6. The man who read in silence

*What do you do when the person you need has no time for you?*

## The passage

> But when he was reading, his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest. Ofttimes when we had come (for no man was forbidden to enter, nor was it his wont that any who came should be announced to him), we saw him thus reading to himself, and never otherwise; and having long sat silent (for who durst intrude on one so intent?) we were fain to depart, conjecturing that in the small interval which he obtained, free from the din of others' business, for the recruiting of his mind, he was loth to be taken off; and perchance he dreaded lest if the author he read should deliver any thing obscurely, some attentive or perplexed hearer should desire him to expound it, or to discuss some of the harder questions; so that his time being thus spent, he could not turn over so many volumes as he desired; although the preserving of his voice (which a very little speaking would weaken) might be the truer reason for his reading to himself. But with what intent soever he did it, certainly in such a man it was good. … I however certainly had no opportunity of enquiring what I wished of that so holy oracle of Thine, his breast, unless the thing might be answered briefly. But those tides in me, to be poured out to him, required his full leisure, and never found it.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 6 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

Somewhere on every road there is a doorway with a queue in it. Behind the door is someone who could answer the one question you have been carrying for days, and between you and them is a crowd of people with questions of their own. You get as far as the threshold, see how things are, and sit down to wait. After a while you find you have been waiting a long time and your question has not got any smaller.

That is Augustine in Milan, and the person behind the door is Ambrose, the city's bishop. He has just arrived in the city to teach. He wants to talk to Ambrose about the things that trouble him, and he cannot. The bishop is, as Augustine puts it, shut off by *multitudes of busy people, whose weaknesses he served*. Whenever Ambrose is not with them, he is eating what the body needs, or he is reading.

Augustine writes the scene carefully, as someone who watched it more than once. Nobody was forbidden to enter, and visitors were not even announced. You simply walked in and found him with a book. Augustine describes it exactly: *his eye glided over the pages, and his heart searched out the sense, but his voice and tongue were at rest.* He was reading to himself, with no sound. Augustine and the others would sit for a long time, *for who durst intrude on one so intent?*, and then slip away.

It clearly struck Augustine as worth explaining, because he offers three guesses. Perhaps Ambrose wanted a quiet stretch to rest his mind away from the din of other people's business. Perhaps he did not want a puzzled listener to ask him to explain a hard line aloud. Perhaps he simply wished to save his voice, which a little speaking would tire. Then comes the best sentence in the passage: *But with what intent soever he did it, certainly in such a man it was good.* He does not know which reason is true. He decides to think well of the man whichever it is.

The second stretch is plain frustration. He had *no opportunity of enquiring* what he wished from *that so holy oracle of Thine, his breast*, unless the question could be answered briefly. But the tides in him *required his full leisure, and never found it*. The image is good: a seeker with a sea of questions in him, and a teacher who has only a few minutes at a time.

Two things deserve a second look. The first is the generosity itself. Augustine reads Ambrose kindly because he has already decided to admire him. Would he have been as kind to a man he did not like, who sat silent behind a book and kept him waiting? It is easier to be generous to people we have already put on a shelf marked good. That is not a reason to give the generosity up. It is a reason to try it on someone harder.

The second doubt is about the ending. The passage does not tell you that waiting is rewarded. Augustine does not get his private conversation here. He gets something else, which he mentions in the next lines: he hears Ambrose preach to the people every Sunday, and over time he grows more sure that the objections he had met could be answered. The help arrives sideways, in public, to a crowd he happens to be part of. What you cannot get alone, you sometimes get as one of many. That is a comfort, but a small and unpredictable one.

On a plain day it asks for a little patience at the door of someone's time. And a habit of generous reading, where it costs nothing: the walker who strides ahead without a word, the host who has no time to chat, the stranger who answers shortly. They may be tired, or shy, or saving their voice. You do not need to know which. You can decide, as Augustine did, to think well of them whichever it is.

A small extra, for fun. Read the passage once more with your own lips and tongue at rest, the way he saw it done. You may hear it differently.

## For the walk

- Whose silence am I judging this morning? What are three kind reasons for it?
- For the first stretch of the day, walk without starting a conversation. If you have a question you want answered by someone, let it wait unasked. Notice what the question does while it waits. Does it shrink, or sharpen, or turn into a different question? If a quiet moment comes later, you might also read something short with your lips at rest, and see what the eyes alone can take in.

## At the end of the day

A room full of tired people has its own way of going quiet. Lamps are clicked off, one at a time. Someone turns a page. Someone eases a zip along a millimetre at a time. Somewhere in the room there is probably a person who would love a conversation and is not going to have one. Perhaps it is you.

Look back at the day for the doors. Where did you have to wait? A queue for the kitchen, a long hour before a place opened, a walker you hoped to catch up with, a person you hoped to reach by phone and could not. Waiting is one of the plain facts of the road, and it is rarely mentioned in the pictures. What was it like, today? Did you fill it, or just sit through it?

Think about the people who were busy on your behalf. Whoever checked a great many walkers in, found beds, answered the same question forty times, kept the kitchen going. Augustine's Ambrose was surrounded by *multitudes of busy people, whose weaknesses he served*. It is easy to feel that such a person is unavailable to you. It is also true that they are available to everybody, and that you are one of a crowd that they are serving. If someone was short with you today, you could ask whether that was the tide going out.

Now turn to the silences. Whose did you misread today? A short answer, a face turned away, a quiet that seemed cold. Think of the guesses that come to mind first, and try giving each one a kinder twin. He was saving his voice. She was tired. He was shy. It will not always be true. It is a good habit even so, and it is gentler to sleep on.

If there was a stretch today in which you were quiet yourself, whether by choice or from fatigue, ask what it held. You may find, after a few days on a long road, that silence is less of an absence than it looked.

One last small kindness is worth the trouble. If there is somebody who has been quiet all day, near you, give them the smallest greeting before the lights go: a nod, a good night, a pass of the water bottle. You do not have to pull a conversation out of them. Augustine and his friends sat in Ambrose's room and left without a word, and he remembers it with respect, not resentment. A shared silence can be a way of keeping company.

Tiredness has a gift tonight. When you are very tired, you stop trying to make conversation, and a good deal of the effort drops away. Allow it. You are not being rude. You are being, for a while, a person who reads without a sound.

- Whose silence did I read badly today, and what might be a kinder reading?
- What question did I carry that needed more time than anyone had?
- Where did I find even a minute of real quiet, and what did I do with it?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I thank you for the people who made room for me, and for the ones who had none. Teach me to wait at a door without bitterness. Let me read the quiet of others generously. Let my own silence be rest and not retreat. Let my questions keep until they are ready to be answered.*
