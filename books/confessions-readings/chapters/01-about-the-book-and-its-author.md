# About the book and its author

## The man

Augustine of Hippo lived, by the usual reckoning, from about 354 to 430. The file we use gives no dates for his life, so those are the dates that reference books give, and we mark them *about*. What the *Confessions* itself tells us is where he moved. His father, it says, was *a poor freeman of Thagaste*, a small town in North Africa. Augustine studied and then taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Thagaste and at Carthage, and later at Rome and Milan. In Milan he was baptised. The title page of the file calls him Saint Augustine, as the Church does, and Bishop of Hippo, the post he held when he wrote.

## The book

The *Confessions* has thirteen books. The first nine tell his life, from infancy to the death of his mother at Ostia, the harbour town of Rome. The tenth turns from the past to the present and looks at memory. The eleventh is about time, and the last two are about the first chapter of Genesis. He wrote it about the year 400. The file dates it AD 401, and reference books generally give about 397 to 400, so we say about 400.

*Confession* here means two things at once, owning a wrong and giving praise, and Augustine means both. The book is a prayer, and it is spoken to God, not to us. In the second book he asks, *To whom tell I this? not to Thee, my God; but before Thee to mine own kind*, and in the fourth he says that he would *still confess to Thee mine own shame in Thy praise*. We overhear, as it were, and are invited to say the words along with him.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

Augustine is nearly always on the move in this book. The places it names, Thagaste, Madaura, Carthage, Rome, Milan and Ostia, are a chain of departures and arrivals, and a few of those journeys are at night, or by sea, or against someone's wishes. Yet its best-known sentence says that the heart is restless *until it repose in Thee*, and that sentence is not about a place. The book keeps both things in view: a restless traveller, and the thing he is travelling towards. That makes it an honest companion for a long walk, where the body goes one way and the mind goes several. It is also honest about being unfinished. It ends with a request, not with a conclusion.

## What is hard, and what we left out

Augustine writes about the body, desire and sin as a Christian bishop looking back on his youth. He is much harder on his younger self than most readers would be, and he writes with real heat against the Manichees, a religious movement of his day which he had followed for about nine years. In the first book he says that in God's sight not even a day-old infant is pure from sin. These are his terms and his century, and we do not argue with him in the reader's ear. We chose passages that a walker can follow. Where one of them touches these matters, the reflection says so calmly (see Days 2, 3 and 8), and it does not make him say more or less than he says.

The last four books are more philosophy than story. They are about memory, time and the book of Genesis, and much of the last book is a long figurative reading of the days of creation. The last four days take what a first-time reader can follow, and say honestly where it is hard. We have taken thirteen passages, one from each book, about 2,500 words in all. The whole of the work, in this translation, is about 110,000 words.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is the translation of E. B. Pusey, who is named on the title page of the file as Edward Bouverie Pusey. Pusey was an Oxford churchman and scholar, and he died in 1882. Augustine and he are both long out of copyright, so the translation is in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 3296, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

The file does not print a date for the translation. Pusey's version was first published in 1838, which is the year we give, and we cannot say which later printing the file follows. The file prints the thirteen headings BOOK I to BOOK XIII, but it does not number the chapters inside them, so every passage here is cited by Book alone. The words themselves will find it in any other edition.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. We kept the old spellings and the *Thou*, *Thee* and *Thy*, and the file's double hyphen where it stands for a dash. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. We quote only Augustine's text, never the headings, the header or the licence of the file. The choice and order of the passages, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original.
