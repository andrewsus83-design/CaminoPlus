# Day 9. A window at Ostia

*What would you have to hush, in order to hear?*

## The passage

> The day now approaching whereon she was to depart this life (which day Thou well knewest, we knew not), it came to pass, Thyself, as I believe, by Thy secret ways so ordering it, that she and I stood alone, leaning in a certain window, which looked into the garden of the house where we now lay, at Ostia; where removed from the din of men, we were recruiting from the fatigues of a long journey, for the voyage. … And while we were discoursing and panting after her, we slightly touched on her with the whole effort of our heart; and we sighed, and there we leave bound the first fruits of the Spirit; and returned to vocal expressions of our mouth, where the word spoken has beginning and end. … "Lay," she saith, "this body any where; let not the care for that any way disquiet you: this only I request, that you would remember me at the Lord's altar, wherever you be." … "Nothing is far to God; nor was it to be feared lest at the end of the world, He should not recognise whence He were to raise me up."
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 9 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

There is a certain kind of evening, near the end of a long day, when you find a window. The room behind you is full of bags and boots and the noise of people sorting themselves out. In front of you there is a garden, or a yard, or a strip of darkening hill. Someone you care for is beside you. You say very little, and for a few minutes the day is exactly the right size.

That is where the ninth book of the *Confessions* brings us. Augustine and his mother are in a house at Ostia, the harbour town of Rome, on their way back to Africa. They have come a long way and are waiting for a ship. The passage begins with the sentence that sets the scene: they stood alone, *leaning in a certain window, which looked into the garden of the house*, *removed from the din of men*, *recruiting from the fatigues of a long journey*. That last phrase could be written on any pilgrim's wall. Before all of it, in a bracket, stands a quiet sadness: the day of her death was near, *which day Thou well knewest, we knew not*.

What they talk about is the largest subject there is. They ask what the eternal life of the saints might be like. Augustine then tries to say what they imagined. Suppose everything were hushed. *If to any the tumult of the flesh were hushed, hushed the images of earth, and waters, and air*, he writes, *hushed also the pole of heaven, yea the very soul be hushed to herself*. The sentence runs on and on. Suppose every sign and every voice went quiet, even the voice of creation, and then suppose the one who made it all spoke by himself, with no word of flesh or angel or thunder in between. He ends by asking, *were not this, Enter into thy Master's joy?*

It is a very long sentence, and it is built on a single word, said again and again. It is easy to lose the thread, and the thread is this. Everything we hear is a message about something else. Augustine imagines what it would be if all of it fell quiet and the thing itself was there.

After the book's long story of her tears and his evasions, it is a relief to see the two of them simply talking, side by side, about what they both want. He is in his thirty-third year, and she is in her fifty-sixth. For a few minutes they are not mother and wayward son. They are two people at a window.

Then the passage turns, honestly, to how little they got. They *slightly touched* it, he says, *with the whole effort of our heart*. They sighed. And they *returned to vocal expressions of our mouth, where the word spoken has beginning and end*. That is the line to remember. The moment was short. They did not live in it. They came back to speech, which starts and stops, and went on being people in a house at Ostia. This scene is often described as an ecstasy. Augustine's own account is more modest, and more useful.

Within about five days his mother fell ill with a fever. On the ninth day of her sickness she died, in the fifty-sixth year of her age and the thirty-third of his, as he tells us. The passage quotes the two things she said that matter to a walker. She asked that they lay her body anywhere, and *let not the care for that any way disquiet you*. She wanted only to be remembered, at the altar. And when friends asked whether she was not afraid to die so far from home: *Nothing is far to God.*

Three things are hard here. The hush sentence is hard going, and we have given you only its first lines. Read it aloud, slowly, for its rhythm, and do not worry if you lose the argument. The request to be remembered *at the Lord's altar* belongs to the Catholic practice of praying for the dead, and some readers will hold it differently. And the story does not explain itself. The conversation at the window is followed within days by her death, and Augustine draws no moral from the order of events. He only tells it.

So what does the day need? First, a window in it. Not an hour. Ten quiet minutes, with the din a little way off. Second, one person given your whole attention, as he gave his mother his. Third, an acceptance that it will not last, and that coming back to ordinary speech is not a failure. It is how the day goes on.

## For the walk

- What would I have to hush, to hear what this day is saying?
- For ten minutes, walk without talk, music or phone. If you are with others, ask them for the quiet out loud: "Shall we have ten minutes?" You may find that they are glad to be asked. When the ten minutes are over, notice the moment you go back to speaking.

## At the end of the day

Find a window, if you can, or a door that opens on something that is not a screen. A courtyard, a hill, a street with the light going. Stand there for as long as it takes to hear the day quieten.

You have done a great deal today without being asked to describe it. The kilometres, the food, the faces, the weather, the small decisions: all of it has been carried by a body that will want to sleep soon. For a few minutes, let it be the window and not the room. The passage does not ask you to think about eternity. It asks only that you stand where the noise is a little way off, and let the hush be as large as it chooses.

Think of the conversation of the day that mattered most. Not the loudest one. Was there one, with someone, in which you were not hurrying to be somewhere else? A few quiet sentences at a table, a long walk side by side that arrived somewhere. If there was, remember who said what, and be glad of it. If there was not, no one has failed. It may be waiting tomorrow, with the next stranger who sits down beside you.

A small practice, if the evening allows it: turn off one thing more than you usually would. A phone, a speaker, a second conversation. Make the room one sound quieter than it was, and sit in the difference for a minute. If you are not alone in the room, you can still make a window of a kind. The people around you are also finishing their day, and a quiet good night across the room is a small hush of its own.

And think of the people who are far away tonight. Augustine's mother was a long way from her own country, and her last thoughts were about distance. *Nothing is far to God* is her comfort, and a believer will hear it as a promise. If you do not believe, it can still be heard as a wish for anyone who is far from home tonight: that they are not as far away as they feel.

You may feel nothing special, and that is allowed. Augustine's conversation has been called a vision. Yours may be an ordinary evening. Both are good.

An honest word about tiredness. Quiet may come more easily when you are worn out, and it can be hard to tell the hush of rest from the hush of exhaustion. Either is welcome tonight. Neither needs a name. Do not try to make the evening into a vision.

If there is someone you want to be remembered by, or someone you want to remember tonight, say the name once, quietly, before you sleep. It is a small thing, and it is not nothing.

- Whom did I talk with today, unhurried, about something that mattered?
- What did I manage to hush, even for a minute?
- Whom would I like to remember tonight, or be remembered by?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I stand at the window of the day. Let the noise go a little way off. Keep those who are far from me, and let them know they are not forgotten. Thank you for the talk that mattered, and the quiet after it. Let me rest, and let the morning find me gentle.*
