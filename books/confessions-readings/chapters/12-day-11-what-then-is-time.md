# Day 11. What then is time?

*How long is an hour on a road?*

## The passage

> For what is time? Who can readily and briefly explain this? Who can even in thought comprehend it, so as to utter a word about it? But what in discourse do we mention more familiarly and knowingly, than time? And, we understand, when we speak of it; we understand also, when we hear it spoken of by another. … What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not: yet I say boldly that I know, that if nothing passed away, time past were not; and if nothing were coming, a time to come were not; and if nothing were, time present were not. Those two times then, past and to come, how are they, seeing the past now is not, and that to come is not yet? But the present, should it always be present, and never pass into time past, verily it should not be time, but eternity. … Yea, that one hour passeth away in flying particles. Whatsoever of it hath flown away, is past; whatsoever remaineth, is to come.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 11 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

There is an hour in most long days that will not pass. It is usually early in the afternoon, on a stretch that is straight and flat and exactly the colour of the one before it. You look at your watch. Eleven minutes have gone by. You walk on, and look again, and it is nine. Then, later, there is another hour, at a table or on a bench, with someone you like, and you look up and the light has changed and it is evening.

It is the same hour, in the same number of minutes. And yet it is not. The eleventh book of the *Confessions* is a long, patient attempt to say what is going on.

Augustine does not begin with a theory. He begins with a small, honest embarrassment: *What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not.* It is a very good sentence. We all use the word every minute of the day, and all of us understand each other. The moment someone asks us to say what it is, it slips out of our hands. You know a good many things in that way: what home is, what a good day is, what you mean when you say you are tired.

Then he tries. The past is gone, so it is not. The future has not come, so it is not yet. So only the present is left. But *the present, should it always be present, and never pass into time past, verily it should not be time, but eternity.* The present only counts as time because it is on its way out. That is a curious thing to say about the one moment you are sure of.

The third stretch of the passage drives it home. Take a century, he says a little further on, and only one year of it can be present. Take that year, and only one month. Take the month, and one day. Take the day, and one hour. At last he arrives at the hour you are in, and it will not stay still either: *that one hour passeth away in flying particles.* *Whatsoever of it hath flown away, is past; whatsoever remaineth, is to come.* If you try to hold the present, there is nothing to hold. A few lines later he says it outright, in a short sentence worth keeping: *The present hath no space.*

Where does that leave a walker, with eleven minutes on the watch and the afternoon dragging? Not with a plain answer, and Augustine is honest about that. Later in the book he proposes, tentatively, that time is held in the mind. There is a *present of things past*, which is memory; a *present of things present*, which is sight; and a *present of things future*, which is expectation. On that picture, one might say that the long afternoon is long because your expectation is stretched towards the end of it, and the pleasant evening is short because you were wholly in the sight of it. That is a walker's gloss, not his, and it is not a proof. He keeps saying as much himself as he goes: *I ask, Father, I affirm not*. He is thinking aloud, and asking for help.

It is worth noticing that the book in your hand is itself an example. Nine books of memory, and then a prayer that leans forward into what is not yet known: Augustine's own mind, stretched across time as he writes. He does not say so here. The passage is only the place where he stops, in the middle of a prayer, to ask how such a thing is possible at all.

Two difficulties, then. This is a place where a reader may lose patience. It is a puzzle about words, and a reader may well wonder what it has to do with a Camino. Fair. You can hold it lightly. The second is that it will not shorten a single afternoon. A philosopher's argument does not lengthen the daylight.

What it can do is smaller, and it is not Augustine's claim but a use that a walker might make of it. If the present is never all there at once, and only the moment you are in is real, then an unbearable hour is not an hour at all. It is only this step, and this breath, followed by another. The hill is long. The step is short. You may find that this is a comfort at four in the afternoon, and you may find that it is not. It costs nothing to try.

## For the walk

- How long is this hour? Not by the watch, but as it feels.
- For the next hour, do not look at a watch or a phone. Guess when it is over, then check. Notice what the guess says about how you spent it. Do not mind if you cheat and look. The point is the guessing. If the hour was long, ask what you were stretching towards. If it was short, ask what held you.

## At the end of the day

Some days are measured in kilometres and some in hours, and tonight you can look at the day by neither. Think of it as a day made of stretches that were different from one another in length, whatever the clock said.

Which hours vanished? There was probably one. A good conversation, a pleasant meal, an hour on a bench in the sun. You looked up and it was gone. On his picture, you were wholly in the sight of them. Those hours are worth naming, because you may want to find your way back to them tomorrow.

Which dragged? Perhaps the long flat stretch, perhaps an afternoon of wanting to arrive. Be fair to it. A dragging hour is often an hour in which part of you was somewhere else: in the next village, the next meal, the end of the day. There is nothing wrong with the wish to arrive. But a good deal of the road passes through the hours you were waiting to get through.

What were you waiting for? Not a grand thing, perhaps. A rest, a shower, a message. If the waiting took more of the day than the thing itself, that is an ordinary human result, and worth a smile. Most of life is spent between one thing and another.

A road gives days clear edges: wake, walk, stop, eat, sleep. It is an unusual gift to have hours with so little else to do but be the hours they are, and tonight you can see the shape of the day as you rarely can at home. Give it three words, one for the fast hour, one for the slow hour and one for an hour in between. It is a very small diary, and it will be enough to bring the day back in a year.

Then try the oddest part of Augustine's argument, just once, as an experiment, before you sleep. Take a single breath, and notice that this breath is the whole of what is happening. Not the day behind, which is memory, and not the day to come, which is expectation. Only the breath you are in. It will be over before you finish noticing, which is about as close as a breath can come to his argument.

Tonight's tiredness is a very physical kind of time. Your body has measured the day in its own way, in muscles and soles and shoulders, and its measure is usually truer than the watch's. Let it be the clock tonight. When it says that it is time to sleep, it is probably right.

- Which hour of today do I want to keep, and which am I glad to let pass?
- What was I waiting for today, and did the waiting take more of the day than the thing itself?
- What am I actually doing in this breath, and is that enough?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I thank you for the hours that flew and the hours that dragged. Forgive me the time I wished away. Let me be where I am, as far as I am able. Keep what has passed, and hold what has not yet come. Let my body keep its own good time, and let me sleep.*
