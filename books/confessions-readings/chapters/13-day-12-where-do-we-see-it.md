# Day 12. Where do we see it?

*How do we read together what none of us can finish reading?*

## The passage

> My heart, O Lord, touched with the words of Thy Holy Scripture, is much busied, amid this poverty of my life. And therefore most times, is the poverty of human understanding copious in words, because enquiring hath more to say than discovering, and demanding is longer than obtaining, and our hand that knocks, hath more work to do, than our hand that receives. … The lowliness of my tongue confesseth unto Thy Highness, that Thou madest heaven and earth; this heaven which I see, and this earth that I tread upon, whence is this earth that I bear about me; Thou madest it. … For this brotherly and peaceful word do I return unto Him: "If we both see that to be true that Thou sayest, and both see that to be true that I say, where, I pray Thee, do we see it? Neither I in thee, nor thou in me; but both in the unchangeable Truth itself, which is above our souls." … So when one says, "Moses meant as I do"; and another, "Nay, but as I do," I suppose that I speak more reverently, "Why not rather as both, if both be true?"
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 12 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

Two people at a table are looking at the same map and disagree. The road offers a choice at one point, a higher way or a lower one, and each of them is quite sure which is right. One has read something. The other has heard something from a friend. They are polite about it for a while. Then one of them says that the other is simply wrong, and the evening goes a little cold.

For that choice, a book of readings is not the place to look. Weather and conditions change, and the answer belongs to current information and local advice. But the table is a good place to read the twelfth book of the *Confessions*, because it is about what to do when people read the same words and see different things.

Here is the plain situation. Book twelve is probably the hardest of the thirteen. Augustine takes the first line of Genesis, the story of creation in the Bible, and tries to say what *heaven and earth* means in it, and what the *earth invisible and without form* was, and what became of what he calls the *heaven of heavens*. It is the kind of thing a first-time reader nods at for a page and then loses. Augustine knows it. He even asks, at one point, *what reader would hold out to take in the whole?* We have not tried to carry you through it. We have taken the three places where it steps back and says something a walker can hold.

The first is the opening. *My heart, O Lord, touched with the words of Thy Holy Scripture, is much busied*, he says, and then: *enquiring hath more to say than discovering, and demanding is longer than obtaining, and our hand that knocks, hath more work to do, than our hand that receives.* It is a very plain description of seeking. The asking takes longer than the finding, and the hand that knocks is busier than the hand that is handed something. Anyone who has asked for directions and been given a long answer will know it.

The second is a short sentence about the ground. The creation he is trying to understand is not far away. He says: *this heaven which I see, and this earth that I tread upon, whence is this earth that I bear about me; Thou madest it.* The earth he is walking on is the earth he is asking about. For a walker that is a nice small turn: the thing under your boots is the subject of the book.

The third is the heart of the passage. People differ about what Moses meant. One says one thing and another says another, and each is sure. Augustine imagines saying to one of them: *If we both see that to be true that Thou sayest, and both see that to be true that I say, where, I pray Thee, do we see it? Neither I in thee, nor thou in me; but both in the unchangeable Truth itself, which is above our souls.* The point is delicate. If we both see a thing is true, we are not seeing it in each other. We are both looking at the same thing, and it belongs to neither of us. Then, in the last lines: *Why not rather as both, if both be true?*

A few lines before this passage, in words we have left out, he says it more broadly: *Thy truth is neither mine, nor his, nor another's.* Truth, in his picture, is public, like the road. Elsewhere in the book he compares a short sacred text to a spring: a small source that feeds more streams than any one of the streams could.

Two cautions. First, *both* is not a blanket. Augustine says plainly that there are readings which are false, and on those he is not gentle: *away with all those who imagine themselves to know as a truth, what is false.* The generosity applies when two readings are both true. It does not say that every opinion is as good as every other. Second, the whole conversation is about the Bible, and it takes the authority of that book for granted. A reader who does not share it can still hear the manner: how to disagree without making it a fight over who owns the light.

So how would this look at the next table? Slow down before you say that someone is wrong. Ask what they can see from where they sit. And ask yourself the question that is the day's title.

## For the walk

- What am I sure of that someone near me sees differently?
- In your next conversation where you differ, ask the other person to say what they see, and say it back to them before you answer. If you are alone, do it in your head with a person you recently argued with, and see what is left of the argument. If there is no disagreement today, choose a small difference of taste with someone and ask about it with real curiosity.

## At the end of the day

Arguments on the road are usually small, and very few of them are about anything. Which way, how early, whose turn. They are worth looking at all the same, because they are made of the same stuff as the large ones.

Look back for a disagreement you had today, or one you only had in your head. It might have been over a route, or a bunk, or a remark in the kitchen. Where did it stand at the end? Resolved, shelved, still warm? Whichever it was, ask Augustine's question. Where, exactly, were the two of you looking? Sometimes the honest answer is that you were looking at different things, and had not noticed.

Now find the true thing in the other person's view. It is nearly always there, and nearly always smaller than they think and larger than you thought. It might be as little as the fact that they were tired. It might be a good point about the weather. If you can find it tonight, you can be gentler with the person tomorrow, which is more than a victory would have given you.

The other half of the passage is easier to miss. Augustine is willing, in this very book, to confess that he does not know what Moses meant. Was there a place today where you could have said as much, and did not? A route, a fact, a rumour you repeated with more confidence than you had? It costs something to admit it. It also saves a good deal. A day in which you owned up once, and meant it, to not knowing is a day on a stable footing.

Try one more thing before you sleep. Name something that you and the person you disagreed with both see clearly: a hill, a hot afternoon, an empty stomach. Begin from the light you share. And if there is a step, a wall or a patch of ground within reach, lay the flat of your hand on it for a breath. It is the earth that Augustine was asking about, and it has carried you all day.

You do not need to settle any of this tonight. The earth you tread on will be there in the morning, and so, probably, will the table.

Tiredness makes people quick to take offence. If you are feeling sharp at the edges tonight, it may not be anyone's fault, and it may be best to leave all the arguments of the day exactly where they are until you have slept.

- Where did I insist on being right today?
- What was true in a view I did not share?
- Where could I have said that I did not know, and what would it have cost me?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I lay down my arguments, the won ones and the lost ones. Thank you for the people who see differently from me. Let me look for the light we share, and not for the corner where I am right. Teach me to say that I do not know. Let me rest, and let the truth be larger than my opinion.*
