# Day 3. The son of these tears

*What can you do for someone you cannot reach?*

## The passage

> Thou gavest her then another answer, by a Priest of Thine, a certain Bishop brought up in Thy Church, and well studied in Thy books. … For he answered, that I was yet unteachable, being puffed up with the novelty of that heresy, and had already perplexed divers unskilful persons with captious questions, as she had told him: "but let him alone a while" (saith he), "only pray God for him, he will of himself by reading find what that error is, and how great its impiety." … Which when he had said, and she would not be satisfied, but urged him more, with entreaties and many tears, that he would see me and discourse with me; he, a little displeased at her importunity, saith, "Go thy ways and God bless thee, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish." Which answer she took (as she often mentioned in her conversations with me) as if it had sounded from heaven.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 3 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

At the end of a day's walking there is often a phone, and on the phone there is often someone who is worried. Perhaps a message is waiting from a person at home who wants to know that you are safe. Perhaps you are the one composing a message you are not sure you should send, to someone whose choices you cannot stop thinking about. In both cases the distance is the same. There is a person you love, and there is a screen, and there is not much more that you can do.

The third book of the *Confessions* has a mother in that position. She is Augustine's mother, whom the book names only once, at the very end of the ninth book, as Monnica. The usual spelling is Monica. He is a young man in Carthage, and he has joined a religious movement of his day called the Manichees. He stays with it for about nine years. The details of what the Manichees taught do not matter for today. What matters is that his mother thinks he is lost, and cannot make him listen.

She had already been given a dream, just before this in the book. She stood weeping on a wooden rule, and a shining young man asked her why. When she said that she was mourning her son, he told her to look, and there he was, standing beside her on the same rule. Augustine, when she told him, tried to explain it away: it meant that she should not despair of becoming what he was. She answered at once that the dream had said no such thing. It had said that where she was, he would be also. She did not give way on that, and she was right to notice what the sentence said.

So she goes to a bishop. Augustine does not name him. He is *well studied in Thy books*, and she begs him to talk to her son, to argue with him and set him right. The bishop says no. Augustine, writing long afterwards, adds *wisely, as I afterwards perceived*. The young man, the bishop tells her, is not yet teachable. He is excited by his new ideas and has been catching out less skilled people with clever questions. Arguing with him now would only make him dig in.

Then comes the advice: *let him alone a while.* Pray for him, the bishop says. Reading will show him the error in time. The bishop knows what he is talking about. He says that he too was handed over to the Manichees as a boy by his own mother, that he read and copied their books, and that he saw for himself, with nobody arguing him out of it, that he must leave. She will not be put off, and keeps asking *with entreaties and many tears*. At last he answers, a little displeased at her importunity, as Augustine candidly says. *Go thy ways and God bless thee, for it is not possible that the son of these tears should perish.* The most hopeful sentence in the story was said by a tired man who wanted the interview to end. She took it, Augustine says, *as if it had sounded from heaven.*

There are two places where this is hard. The first is the advice itself. *Let him alone* can sound like giving up. It is not quite that. The bishop is not telling her to stop caring. He is telling her to stop arguing, and to keep praying. What he asks of her is patience, and trust that the young man has a road of his own to walk. Even a reader who does not pray may know that some people cannot be talked out of a position they have just taken up, and that the talking only hardens it.

The second is the last sentence. Augustine tells the story long after it ended well. He knows the result, and so the bishop's words come to him as a prophecy. Not every story ends that way, and a mother's tears are love, not a guarantee. If you are carrying someone whose road has not turned, the line may sting. Take it as the comfort it was to her on that day, and not as a promise anyone can be held to.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? If you are the worried one, perhaps one small release: to do what you can, which may only be a kind message that asks for nothing, and then to let the person be, for a while. If you are the one being worried about, the story offers something too. Somewhere behind you, someone has been weeping for you without making a sound you can hear. You do not have to repay it today. You could let them know that you are well.

## For the walk

- Who am I trying to change at the moment, and what would it be to leave that person alone for an hour?
- Choose one person, near or far, whom you have wanted to correct or fix. For the rest of the morning, do not rehearse the argument. Each time it begins, wish them well in one short sentence, and walk on.
- If nobody comes to mind, think of yourself. What are you pushing against in your own body or mind this morning that might do better left alone for an hour?

## At the end of the day

Evenings are when the messages arrive. If one is waiting, read it slowly. If you can answer in one kind line, do. A short "I am well, thank you for thinking of me" has been a great comfort to a great many people, and it costs a minute.

Look back at the day through the bishop's advice. Notice first who, today, said something to you that you took in precisely because they did not press it: a fellow walker who told you one thing about their own mistakes and then let it lie. That is how the bishop dealt with her. He told his own story once, and left it. Was there someone you wanted to set right? It might have been a companion who gave a wrong direction with great confidence, or someone whose view of the world you could not let pass. Perhaps you said what you thought and it did not help. Perhaps you held it in, and found that holding it in was easier than you expected. Either way, notice how it felt to want to correct someone and not do it. That is the practice the bishop was asking for.

Think too of the opposite person, the one who has been letting you alone. A friend who did not push when you were short with them. A partner who did not ask the question you would have snapped at. They are often easy to miss, because what they give is an absence. Tonight you could be glad of one such absence.

If today brought news that worries you, be honest that the evening is the worst time to think about it. Tired minds make small problems large. Nothing you decide at this hour will be your best decision. Write down the worry if that helps, in a line, and leave it on the page. The bishop's advice is not to forget the person. It is to put the matter down for the night and trust the morning to hold it.

Keep the length of the road in proportion, too. Augustine's nine years were a long time, and a road of about two weeks is short beside them. It may stir something. It will not finish anything. That is no failure of the road, and no failure of yours.

And a word about the tiredness that comes after emotional days. You may find that you are more tired than the distance explains. Worry is heavy and carries no weight on a scale. A long hot wash, a cup of something, an early night: these count as help. Nobody on earth is served by a walker who sits up rehearsing a conversation.

- Whom did I try to change today, and what happened in me when I held back?
- Who may be thinking of me tonight, and have I let them know that I am well?
- What would it be to wait with someone instead of working on them?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I think of the people I cannot reach. Keep them, and let them know they are loved. Where I have been pushing, teach me to wait. Where I have been waited for, give me the grace to answer. Let me sleep, and trust the morning to carry what I cannot.*
