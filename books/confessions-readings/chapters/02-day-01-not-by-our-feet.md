# Day 1. Not by our feet

*Where does a journey begin, if it is not in the feet that people leave and return?*

## The passage

> Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee. … Passing hence from infancy, I came to boyhood, or rather it came to me, displacing infancy. Nor did that depart,--(for whither went it?)--and yet it was no more. … For it is not by our feet, or change of place, that men leave Thee, or return unto Thee. Or did that Thy younger son look out for horses or chariots, or ships, fly with visible wings, or journey by the motion of his limbs, that he might in a far country waste in riotous living all Thou gavest at his departure? a loving Father, when Thou gavest, and more loving unto him, when he returned empty.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 1 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

On the first morning of a long walk, nearly everything can be counted. The kilometres to the first village. The litres of water. The weight of the pack, which you will feel in your shoulders within the hour and know to the gram by the end of the week. A walk is a countable thing, and there is comfort in that.

Augustine begins his book with something that cannot be counted. A few lines into the first page he says why anyone praises God at all, and the reason is one sentence long: *Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.* He is speaking to God, which is why the whole book says *Thou*. The sentence says that people are made for God, and that nothing smaller settles the heart. The book keeps coming back to it, and so does this one. The last day returns to the same word.

You do not have to pray to hear the other half of it. There is a restlessness in a good many of us that a better view, a bigger lunch or a finished day does not quite quiet. You may know it from the way a hand goes to the phone in the first still moment, or from the second coffee nobody wanted. Augustine does not treat it as a fault in the equipment. He treats it as a compass. That is a surprising way to open a confession. You might expect a list of failings. Instead the first thing he admits is a longing, and the first thing he says about himself is that he is not finished.

The second stretch of the passage turns, without warning, to his own early life. A few paragraphs earlier he has said that he remembers nothing of his infancy. He knows it only from what he was told and from watching other babies, and he is careful to say so. Now he writes: *Passing hence from infancy, I came to boyhood, or rather it came to me, displacing infancy.* Then comes a small, odd question in brackets: *for whither went it?* The infant is gone, and not gone. Each age of a life is pushed aside by the next and stays somewhere underneath. A long walk can stir them up. The tired child who wants to sit down at the next wall. The teenager who will not be seen to struggle. The grown-up with a plan. You may meet all three before lunch.

Third comes the sentence that a book for walkers has to stop for: *For it is not by our feet, or change of place, that men leave Thee, or return unto Thee.* His picture is the younger son in a story from the Gospel of Luke, which Augustine expects you to know. A young man takes his share of his father's money, goes off to a far country and spends it all, then comes home to a father who is glad to see him. Did the son need horses, chariots, ships or wings to get away, Augustine asks? He did not. The distance lay in what the son loved, not in how far he travelled, and the way home was the same.

It stings to read that on the first day of a walk of about 320 km. If distance is not in the feet, why lace the boots? The sentence does not say that walking is pointless. It says that walking cannot be all of it, and cannot do your returning for you. Feet are very good at moving a body to a place. They cannot carry the rest of you unless the rest of you is willing. Here is a fair doubt to set beside it. A body that is tired, away from its usual noise and out of reach of its usual chores, may be better placed to notice what is happening inside it than the same body at a desk. Augustine does not say so. The road might.

Where is the passage hard? Perhaps in its very first word of address. A reader who does not believe in a God to whom things can be said may feel shut out on the first page. The invitation is modest. Listen as you would to someone praying quietly in the next bunk. You need not join in. You can still hear what it sounds like to want something that much.

What might this change on an ordinary day? Something small. To bring the restlessness along instead of hiding it. To stop calling it a bad mood, and ask what it is reaching for. Over the coming days it will turn out to be reaching for a good deal: a crowd's approval, a lost friend, a clever teacher, a quiet window. For today it is enough to notice that it is there, and that you set out with it.

## For the walk

- What is restless in me this morning, and what am I reaching for to quiet it? Give the answer one honest word, even if the word is "coffee".
- For the first ten minutes, notice each time your hand goes to a pocket for the phone, a snack or the map. Do not stop it, and do not scold it. Take one more step before you decide, and see whether the wish is still there when you arrive at the decision.

## At the end of the day

The boots are off, and your feet are making their case. Before the evening fills up with other things, give the day one look from the restless end.

Some of it was hard in the ordinary way. The pack argued with your shoulders. A stretch dragged. At some point the plan for the day and the body that had to carry it disagreed, and the body won. Name the hardest hour, plainly, and leave it there. It does not need mending tonight.

Some of it was good. It might have been a small thing: the first cold drink, a bell somewhere behind you, the plain pleasure of having gone a long way without a screen. Say that too.

Then think about the restlessness. Perhaps it eased as the day went on, as it often does when the body has something simple to do. Perhaps it only changed shape, from a wish to be somewhere else into a wish for a bed, a meal, a wash. That is a smaller restlessness than the one in the passage, but it is where an honest evening starts. There may also have been a moment when you saw what you were reaching for, and reached anyway. That is allowed. The aim of a first evening is only to have seen it once.

Your feet did their work today, and they deserve a mention. Augustine's sentence leaves a question that the evening is good for. Did the rest of you arrive where the feet did? Some days the body is in the village while the mind is still back at the last argument, or a step ahead at tomorrow's hill. That is not a failure. It is only worth noticing which part of you is in the room tonight.

And a small kindness for the child who walked with you. Whatever age turned up today, the tired one or the proud one, it did what it could. It is probably the one who most needs the bed.

If the restlessness is loud tonight, there is an old and plain remedy: do the next small thing. Rinse the socks. Fill the bottle. Write two lines to someone at home. Nothing in Augustine forbids a clean pair of socks. His book is stern in places, but its first pages are grateful for milk and warmth, and he says that he was received into life by *the comforts of woman's milk*. Nothing there is ashamed of needing things, and neither need you be.

A word for the tired. If the questions below slide off, let them. A restless heart and a tired body are not the same thing, and sleep is a perfectly good answer to the second. The questions will keep until morning, and so will the heart.

- What did I reach for today when I was restless?
- When, even for a minute, did I feel entirely where I was?
- Which of my ages walked today: the child, the teenager or the grown-up?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I am tired, and something in me is still not at rest. I will not pretend otherwise. Let the walking do what it can. Let sleep do the rest. Whatever it is I am looking for, let it be near. Let the morning find me willing to go on.*
