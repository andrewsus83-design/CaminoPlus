# How to use this book

This is a book of thirteen days, made to go with the Camino Primitivo. It takes the *Confessions* of Augustine of Hippo one book at a time, thirteen books in thirteen days, in the order they stand. You read one day at a time, whether you are on the road or at home.

Every day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reading of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A stretch of the *Confessions*, in the exact words of E. B. Pusey's old English translation, *Thou* and *Thee* and all. Read it twice, once aloud if you can. The English is archaic, and that slows the eye, which is half its use.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Augustine's. It begins on the road, with a bench, a hill or a long afternoon, and lets the passage open it. It says what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might change in an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour, and one small practice that needs only attention.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back over the day you have lived, with two or three questions to lie down with.
- **A short prayer.** Original and brief. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish.

## Why this book for this road

The CaminoPlus catalogue lists the Camino Primitivo as running from Oviedo to Santiago de Compostela, about 320 km, and rates it 4 on a scale of 1 to 5. It climbs through the mountains of Asturias before it crosses into Galicia, and it is a road of about two weeks. This book does not give stages. Thirteen days is the length of the reading, not a plan for the walk.

The pairing is an inward climb. The *Confessions* begins low down, with a baby and a stolen pear, and rises through grief, doubt and a garden to memory, time and rest. Augustine uses the picture himself. In the tenth book he speaks of *rising by degrees*, and in the last he writes *We ascend Thy ways that be in our heart, and sing a song of degrees*, a phrase that echoes the psalms called the songs of ascents, which are traditionally linked with pilgrims going up to Jerusalem. Both the road and the book are long, with hard stretches, and both are better taken in short pieces.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

The *Confessions* is a Christian book, and it is a prayer. Augustine writes to God from the first line to the last, as a believer and as a bishop in the late Roman world, and that is why the whole thing says *Thou*. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk the Camino for many reasons, faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work, and all of them are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Augustine believed to recognise a restless heart. Where he is hard to follow, the reflection says so.

## The shape of the thirteen days

The days follow the books in order, and have a shape of their own.

- **Days 1 to 4: a young man and his restlessness.** A child, a stolen pear, a mother's tears and a lost friend.
- **Days 5 to 8: the search and the turn.** A teacher who disappointed, a bishop who read in silence, a glimpse that would not stay, and a garden.
- **Days 9 to 13: the climb inward.** A window at Ostia, memory, time, many ways of reading, and rest.

Days 10 to 12 are the hardest. They are more philosophy than story, and the reflections say plainly where a passage is difficult. If a day will not open, read the passage aloud, say the prayer, and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, or in a café while the soup arrives, then put the phone away and take the question with you. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off. At home, the morning goes with tea and the evening with bed. If your road is longer than thirteen days, read a day twice. If it is shorter, read a morning on one day and its evening on the next.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a shared kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well.
