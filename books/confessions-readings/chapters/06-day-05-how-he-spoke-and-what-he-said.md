# Day 5. How he spoke, and what he said

*When someone speaks well, what are you listening to?*

## The passage

> But what availed the utmost neatness of the cup-bearer to my thirst for a more precious draught? Mine ears were already cloyed with the like, nor did they seem to me therefore better, because better said; nor therefore true, because eloquent; nor the soul therefore wise, because the face was comely, and the language graceful. … For he was not altogether ignorant of his own ignorance, nor would he rashly be entangled in a dispute, whence he could neither retreat nor extricate himself fairly. Even for this I liked him the better. For fairer is the modesty of a candid mind, than the knowledge of those things which I desired; and such I found him, in all the more difficult and subtile questions. … That man of God received me as a father, and showed me an Episcopal kindness on my coming. Thenceforth I began to love him, at first indeed not as a teacher of the truth (which I utterly despaired of in Thy Church), but as a person kind towards myself. … And while I opened my heart to admit "how eloquently he spake," there also entered "how truly he spake"; but this by degrees.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 5 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

Anyone who has been truly thirsty knows that the beauty of the cup is not the point. A glass can be cut with great care and polished until it sings, and if it is empty you are still thirsty. Augustine uses exactly this picture in the fifth book of the *Confessions*.

For nearly nine years he had belonged to the Manichees, the movement he joined in his nineteenth year, and for most of that time he had been carrying questions the local teachers could not answer. They all said the same thing: wait for Faustus. Faustus was a bishop of their movement, a famous speaker, and when he came to Carthage the questions would be answered. Augustine longed for that visit, he says, rather too intensely, and when Faustus finally arrived he turned out to be pleasant, fluent and graceful, and to say *the self-same things* as everyone else.

That is where the cup-bearer comes in: *what availed the utmost neatness of the cup-bearer to my thirst for a more precious draught?* The words did not seem to him better because they were better said. They did not seem true because they were eloquent. The soul was not wise because the face was comely. It is a hard lesson to hold on to in a world of good presenters, and anyone who has been swept along by a confident voice, in a kitchen or on a screen, will know how easily the cup can be mistaken for the water.

Here is the surprise of the passage. Having lost his hopes in Faustus, Augustine does not turn on him. He gives him credit. When Augustine put his hard questions, Faustus did not bluster. He knew his limits. Faustus, Augustine says, *was not altogether ignorant of his own ignorance*, and would not walk into an argument he could not get out of. *Even for this I liked him the better.* A teacher who says that he does not know is rarer than one who says a great deal, and Augustine saw it. The passage ends the story of a letdown with a small piece of respect.

Then the road moves to Milan. Augustine has come to teach, and there he meets the bishop, Ambrose. Ambrose receives him *as a father*. Augustine does not begin by loving the teaching. He begins by loving a person who was kind to him, *not as a teacher of the truth*, he says, since he had given up on finding truth in Ambrose's church, *but as a person kind towards myself*. He goes to hear the sermons only to judge the style, to see whether the famous eloquence lived up to its name. Of the content he was *a careless and scornful looker-on*. And then comes the best sentence in the passage: *And while I opened my heart to admit "how eloquently he spake," there also entered "how truly he spake"; but this by degrees.*

Notice what Augustine remembers about that first meeting. Not a word of what Ambrose said. He remembers how he was received: *an Episcopal kindness on my coming*. A bishop with a great many calls on his time made room for a stranger who had been sent to teach in his city, and treated him as if he mattered. The teaching came later. The welcome came first, and it may be the reason the teaching was heard at all.

It is a very gentle account of changing one's mind. No argument defeats him. He went for the style, and the meaning came in beside it, a little at a time, through a door he had left open for another purpose.

There is a fair doubt to raise. Taken alone, that sentence could describe how people drift into anything, good or bad. Being moved by a speaker proves nothing about what the speaker says, and Augustine knows it. The lines just after the first stretch, which we have left out, make the point at length. Eloquence does not prove something true, and plainness does not prove it false. So the sentence is not a recommendation to follow whoever moves you. It is an observation about how slowly and sideways the truth, if it comes, tends to come.

Put to work on an ordinary day, this means listening in two ways. Once for the manner: the accent, the humour, the confidence, the warmth. Once for the matter: what, in plain words, was actually said. And to notice whom you trust because of kindness before you have heard a word of their argument. On a road with a great many conversations in it, at tables and in doorways, there is plenty of practice to be had.

## For the walk

- Who has spoken to me well lately, and what did they actually say?
- In your next conversation, listen once for how it is said and once for what is said. At the end, give yourself the "what" in one plain sentence. If the sentence will not come, that is worth knowing too. If you are walking alone, try the same with the next thing you read: a sign, a message, a headline. What did it say, once the tone has been taken away?

## At the end of the day

Conversations leave a residue, like the salt on a shirt. By the evening you may not remember a single thing anyone said, only that you liked them, or did not. That is not a failure of memory. It is the first half of Augustine's lesson, the part that happens before the meaning arrives.

Go back through the day's talk, as far as you can reach. Who spoke well? Not necessarily the loudest. Perhaps a man at a table who told a story that held the room. Perhaps a woman who said very little, and said it exactly. Try to remember what they said, not only how they said it. Some of it will have gone. Some of it will have stayed, and you may be surprised which.

Then ask the harder question. Was there anything you accepted today because of the way it was said? A confident view on the weather, the route, the right way to pack a bag, a person's character, the state of the world. Confidence is often borrowed, and most of us lend it on trust. There is no need to go back and unpick all of it tonight. It is enough to notice that you took some of it on credit.

Think also of how you were received today, because that is Ambrose's half of the lesson. A greeting at a door. A hand pointing out the right bunk. A word of welcome from someone who owed you nothing. It is easy to forget these within an hour, and it is a pity, because they are often the true beginning of whatever the road is teaching you. Augustine never forgot his, and wrote it down sixteen centuries ago.

And think of Faustus, because he is the friendlier figure in the passage. He did something rare and slightly brave. When he did not know, he said so, and the man he disappointed liked him better for it. Did you have a chance today to say that you did not know? Did you take it? If you did not, that was probably a small relief at the time. But it is worth remembering how the sentence is received by someone who is listening for the truth.

One small practice for tonight, if you have the energy. Take the last conversation you had, and listen to it again in memory, twice: once for the way it was said, once for what was said. You will not hear the second the first time.

Tiredness, honestly: a tired mind is easy to persuade, and may nod at anything offered with a smile. If tonight you read a message, a headline or a rumour and nodded along, give it a night before you decide what you think.

- Whose words did I take in today without weighing them?
- Who was kind to me before I knew what they had to give?
- When did I say that I did not know, or wish that I had?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I am grateful for the voices of the day. Let me hear what is true under what is only pleasant. Keep me grateful to the kind people who are not impressive. Teach me to say that I do not know when I do not. Let slow truth come in, a little at a time, and let me not run from it.*
