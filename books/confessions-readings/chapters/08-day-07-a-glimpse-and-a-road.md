# Day 7. A glimpse and a road

*What do you do after the view?*

## The passage

> And being thence admonished to return to myself, I entered even into my inward self, Thou being my Guide: and able I was, for Thou wert become my Helper. And I entered and beheld with the eye of my soul (such as it was), above the same eye of my soul, above my mind, the Light Unchangeable. … And I wondered that I now loved Thee, and no phantasm for Thee. And yet did I not press on to enjoy my God; but was borne up to Thee by Thy beauty, and soon borne down from Thee by mine own weight, sinking with sorrow into these inferior things. … But I could not fix my gaze thereon; and my infirmity being struck back, I was thrown again on my wonted habits, carrying along with me only a loving memory thereof, and a longing for what I had, as it were, perceived the odour of, but was not yet able to feed on.
>
> — Augustine of Hippo, *The Confessions*, Book 7 (tr. E. B. Pusey, 1838)

## Reflection

If the weather is kind, a mountain road will sooner or later give you a view. The cloud lifts, or the trees thin, and for a few minutes a whole country lies open below you: ridges folded behind ridges, a river somewhere, the line of a road you cannot yet walk. You stop. You may stand there for a good while. Then you shoulder the pack and go down into the next valley, and within the hour you are in a lane between hedges, and the view is a thing that happened.

The seventh book of the *Confessions* is about a view of that kind, and what became of it.

Augustine is about thirty and still searching. He has left the Manichees and is not yet sure what to believe. The book tells us that he has read certain books of the Platonists, philosophers in the tradition of Plato, in a Latin translation. They turn him away from the outside of things. *And being thence admonished to return to myself, I entered even into my inward self*, he says. And there, with what he calls *the eye of my soul*, above his own mind, he sees *the Light Unchangeable*.

Notice, too, how he got there. Nothing in the passage is a climb. It begins with a turn, *to return to myself*, and what follows is received rather than achieved. On a mountain road the same is true in a plain way. Two walkers can go up the same slope, one into clear air and one into cloud, and what each sees is not theirs to arrange.

This is the first day on which the passage may stretch you. He does not tell us what he saw. He tells us what it was not: it was not ordinary light, and not a stronger kind of ordinary light. If you cannot picture it, nothing has gone wrong with you. He could not, either, and says so by the way he writes. The part of the passage anyone can follow comes next.

It happens at once. He *was borne up to Thee by Thy beauty, and soon borne down from Thee by mine own weight*. He is lifted, and then he is let down, sinking with sorrow, as he puts it, into the ordinary things of life. The word to keep is *weight*. It returns on the last day of this book, with a different meaning.

And then the best image in the passage. *I could not fix my gaze thereon*, he says. He was thrown back on his old habits, and what he kept was *a loving memory thereof, and a longing for what I had, as it were, perceived the odour of, but was not yet able to feed on.* He smelled the meal and could not yet eat it. Nearly everyone who has walked past a kitchen on a hungry evening knows the exact feeling. A smell like that is not a feast, and it is a great deal more than nothing. It tells you where the food is, and keeps you walking.

A few pages after our passage, Augustine puts the whole thing in one sentence about a mountain: *from the mountain's shaggy top to see the land of peace, and to find no way thither.* He is thinking of the Platonist books, which showed him the goal clearly and did not show him the road. For him the road lay elsewhere, in the Christian faith and in what he calls humility, and he goes on to say so. This is a Christian book, and we say so plainly. But the sentence works on any hill. A view is not a route. To see where you are going is one thing. To find the path down, round and up again is another.

Two doubts, then. First, the vision is hard to share. Many religious traditions describe moments of this kind, and a reader who has never had one may feel shut out, or suspicious. Both are fair. Augustine does not ask you to have one. What he describes next is more widely shared: a high moment, and the return of the ordinary. Second, he is hard on the ordinary. To him, the pull back into old habits is a sorrow. You may think differently, and a good meal and a warm bed are not a failure of vision. Hold that part loosely.

What does a plain day need from all this? To expect the descent, and not read it as a defeat. And to look after what the view leaves you with. Not the view itself, which cannot be kept, but the loving memory and the longing: a good thing remembered, and a hunger for more of it. They are enough to walk on.

## For the walk

- What did I glimpse once that I could not hold? Is the longing still doing its work?
- At the next open view, stop and look for a full minute, until it has gone a little ordinary. You will feel the moment. Then walk on and see that you can leave it behind, and that something comes with you. If today has no view in it, try the same with the next good thing instead: a shaded bench, a cold tap, a long stretch of sun on a wall.

## At the end of the day

Most days have a high point and a low one, and in the evening it is worth finding both. Without hurry, go through the day as you might scroll back through a road: the places where you were lifted, and the places where you were let down.

What lifted you? It might have been a view, as in the passage. It might have been a small kindness, a good song in your head, the second wind that arrives after a long climb. Be specific. A good thing named is easier to keep than a good thing left vague.

What pulled you down? It might have been a stretch of road, an ache, a thought that came uninvited at four o'clock. Augustine says he was borne down by his own weight. On a long walk the phrase is almost literal. You are heavier at the end of the day than at the beginning, and your thoughts are heavier with you. Part of what pulled you down may simply have been the hour.

Then ask what you kept. If the best part of today has already faded, as it nearly always does, is there still something left of it, like the smell of bread in a coat? A remembered light on a hillside. The sound of a river you passed. A hunger to see something like it again. That is the loving memory and the longing, and it is more than you might think.

If you shared a view today, there is a small gift in remembering that you saw it together. In this passage Augustine's glimpse is entirely his own, and it is a lonelier thing for it. Yours need not be.

Be honest, too, about the days when there was no view. Some days are wet and low, and the distance is the only thing on offer. They count. The passage does not say that only the high days are the real ones. It says that high moments pass, and the road goes on.

If a view did give you a clear sight of something, a kinder way of being, a conversation you have put off, a rest you need, here is the practical use of Augustine's mountain. A view is not a route, so choose the smallest step towards it that you can take tomorrow before noon, and fix it in your mind tonight. A step is a modest thing. It is also what is left when the view has gone, and it is the thing that can actually be walked.

If you cannot think what lifted you today, think instead of what carried you: a pair of socks, a warm drink, a bed with a blanket. A great deal of life is carried by small things.

If today included a long descent, your knees may have something to say. Take the ache seriously and tomorrow's downhill slowly, and ask a doctor or a pharmacist about any pain that does not ease. A road is long, and the body is the only one you have.

- What lifted me today, and what pulled me down?
- What have I kept from the best hour of today?
- What am I hungry for tonight?

## A short prayer

*Tonight I thank you for whatever lifted me, and for whatever carried me when I was low. Let me keep the memory of the good hour, and the longing that comes with it. Do not let me mistake a view for the road. Show me the next step, and let it be a small one. Let me sleep.*
