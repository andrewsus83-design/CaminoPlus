# About the book and its author

## The man

Henry David Thoreau lived from 1817 to 1862, and spent most of his life in and around Concord, Massachusetts. He is best known for *Walden*, published in 1854, an account of living for a time in a small house he built beside a pond near Concord, and for an essay on conscience and the state now called *Civil Disobedience*. He earned his living in several ways, among them land surveying, and you can watch him at it in this essay. He died in 1862, aged 44.

We will not say more about his life than we can stand behind. His books are easy to find, and they speak for him better than a summary does.

## The essay

*Walking* began as lectures and was first published in 1862, the year he died. (The edition we used does not state a year, so the date is general knowledge, and we give it as that.) It is about 12,000 words long, roughly an hour of reading. It is a string of thoughts more than an argument: one idea leads to the next as one bend leads to the next, with side paths, jokes, and quotations from Latin, from Chaucer and from travellers’ books. It tells you at once what it is for. It begins: “I wish to speak a word for Nature, for absolute Freedom and Wildness”. It has no chapters, so the readings cite it as *Walking* and nothing more.

The essay says that walking is not exercise, taken like medicine at fixed hours, but “the enterprise and adventure of the day”. It says that walking takes leisure, freedom and independence, and that no money buys them. It says that a real walk is a kind of leaving. It says that the author, left to himself, turns west. Then it wanders through wildness, knowledge, names, literature and the future of a nation, before it ends, quietly, at a sunset.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

It is a walking essay for walkers, and it is about direction. A road to the Atlantic coast goes the way Thoreau’s feet go, and a road that ends at the sea ends where his essay ends, at the edge of the light. It uses the language of pilgrimage throughout, and it is more interested in how to walk than in where. It is also short, funny and a little stubborn, and it can be read a page at a time.

It is not a gentle book, and it is not a humble one. Thoreau is sure of himself, he enjoys an overstatement, and he is not always fair. These readings do not try to make him humbler. They try to walk beside him.

## Two cautions

**The word “saunter”.** One of the essay’s best-known passages tells how “saunter” came from people on their way “à la Sainte Terre”, to the Holy Land. It is Thoreau’s derivation, and it is generally not accepted by etymologists. He gives another, from *sans terre*, “without land”, and that one is not accepted either. Day 4 uses the passage and says so in the reflection. We kept the story because the idea inside it does not depend on it.

**The assumptions of its time.** The essay was written in the United States in the middle of the nineteenth century, and it carries the assumptions of its place. Its “West” is the American one, and it treats the westward movement of settlers, and the displacement of Native peoples by the farmer, as natural. It speaks of “savages” and “wild men” as figures of admiration, and of named peoples in ways we would not write today. The freedom it praises, to walk where you like and to have the leisure for it, was not open to everyone then, and it notices this only in passing. We have not tried to answer all this for him, and we have not hidden it. We chose passages that do not rest on it, and where a day comes close, it says so briefly and moves on.

## The edition and the licence

This is an English original, so there is no translator. The text is the Project Gutenberg edition, ebook number 1022, which names no modern editor. Thoreau’s essay is a nineteenth-century text, and it is in the public domain. Every passage in this book was checked against that edition, word for word.

We changed nothing in the passages. We kept his spelling, his capital letters and his long dashes, and the American spellings of a New Englander. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the five passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original. Most of what we quote comes from the first half of the essay, and the last day comes from its closing paragraphs. The long middle of the essay is not here. It is still in the original for anyone who wants it.
