# Day 5. The sun on our backs

*What do you do when the west runs out?*

## The passage

> I was walking in a meadow, the source of a small brook, when the sun at last, just before setting, after a cold grey day, reached a clear stratum in the horizon, and the softest, brightest morning sunlight fell on the dry grass and on the stems of the trees in the opposite horizon and on the leaves of the shrub-oaks on the hillside, while our shadows stretched long over the meadow eastward, as if we were the only motes in its beams. It was such a light as we could not have imagined a moment before, and the air also was so warm and serene that nothing was wanting to make a paradise of that meadow. When we reflected that this was not a solitary phenomenon, never to happen again, but that it would happen forever and ever, an infinite number of evenings, and cheer and reassure the latest child that walked there, it was more glorious still. … The west side of every wood and rising ground gleamed like the boundary of Elysium, and the sun on our backs seemed like a gentle herdsman driving us home at evening. … So we saunter toward the Holy Land, till one day the sun shall shine more brightly than ever he has done, shall perchance shine into our minds and hearts, and light up our whole lives with a great awakening light, as warm and serene and golden as on a bank-side in Autumn.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walking* (English original, 1862)

## Reflection

Light just before sunset does something no other light does. It comes in low and from the side, and it turns the ordinary into something nobody put there: a wall, a thistle, a wet road. Thoreau’s passage is an attempt to say what that is.

The scene is a meadow by the source of a small brook, in November, after a cold grey day. Just before setting, the sun finds a clear gap in the horizon, and the softest, brightest light comes across the dry grass. It is “such a light as we could not have imagined” a moment before, and the air is so warm and serene that nothing is wanting to make a paradise of the meadow. Then comes the thought that matters most. It was “more glorious still” when they reflected that this was no one-off. It would happen “forever and ever, an infinite number of evenings”, and would “cheer and reassure the latest child that walked there”.

There is comfort in that. A sunset is a small private event, and it is also something the world keeps on doing, whether or not anyone is there. You do not have to hold on to it. This evening it is yours, and tomorrow evening it will be someone else’s.

Now the doubt. Thoreau’s sunset was a gift: a grey day that cleared at the last moment. The Atlantic does not promise one. You may get cloud, or mist, or rain, and the sun will go down behind it all the same. The essay does not promise a sunset either, and the road was never for sunsets alone. If the sky is shut tonight, the day has not been wasted. And no one can be asked to feel awe on schedule. If you watch with other people and feel nothing much, you have not failed the sunset, and it has not failed you.

This is the day the west runs out. For four days Thoreau has given us a direction. At Finisterre or Muxía the direction meets the sea, and there is a horizon with no road in it. His own west does not end like that. Every sunset sends him on towards “a West as distant and as fair”, and he never has to stop. Yours does. So what then?

The essay answers with its last turn, and it is a quiet one. In the passage the sun is on their backs, “like a gentle herdsman driving us home at evening”. They are walking home. They are going east, away from the sunset, and yet the last sentence begins: “So we saunter toward the Holy Land”. The walk does not stop being a pilgrimage when the walker turns round. At the shore you will turn round too, and if you do it in the evening, the sun, if there is any, will be on your back. It is not defeat. It is the part of a walk that most stories leave out, the going home, and the essay says it still counts.

His last sentence is a hope, not a promise. The sun shall “perchance” shine into our minds and hearts, and light up our whole lives, “as warm and serene and golden as on a bank-side in Autumn”. We cannot promise a great awakening, and neither could he. But the light on a bank-side in autumn is on offer on any good evening, and it costs a walk.

One plain practical thing. If you go to a cape to watch the sun go down, plan the way back before the light goes, because you will return in the dark. Carry a light, keep well back from the edge of the rocks, and keep an eye on the waves, which can reach higher than they look. 112 is the European emergency number.

What does it ask of an ordinary day, the last one? To look once more at what is in front of you, properly, before the light changes. And to remember that turning round is also a way of walking.

## For the walk

- What would I like to look at once more, properly, before the light changes? Choose one thing, and give it the whole of a long breath.
- Sometime today, stop and turn all the way round, once. Look at the road behind you, the same road seen from the other side, and let it be lit by whatever light there is. Then turn back and walk on. A road looks different from the other side.

## At the end of the day

Whether you are at the end of the road tonight or only at the end of the book, this is an evening for endings. Take them in order, gently.

You set out with a list of things to put down. You looked at roads and found two kinds. You turned west, or leaned that way, and followed something that pulled. You were a guest, again and again. Now the land has run out, or the pages have. There is nothing to prove tonight and nowhere left to get to.

You may feel a great deal, or hardly anything. Both are ordinary. You may weep at the sea, or eat a sandwich and look at it. Neither is a failure. If you keep a journal or send a message home, one line is plenty: where you are, what the light was doing, one thing you saw. If the water is near, listen to it for a minute. If it is far away, listen to whatever is.

Look back at what was hard. The long afternoon, the blister, the day you wanted to stop, the one you did not want to end. Say thank you to your feet. They did it, whatever else was going on.

And what was good? Not only the big things, if there were any. The ordinary kindness, the soup, the stretch where the walking became easy. Hold one of them for a moment before it fades into the general blur of a finished road, which it will. Remember the list on the first morning, with its small debts and the one worry you set down for the day. See whether it is still on the doorstep. You do not have to pick it up.

Think of the people who made the road possible: whoever marked it, whoever fed you, made the bed or opened the door, and the people who live beside it and let you pass. You will probably not meet most of them again. Say thank you in whatever way is left to you. And if the sea was grey, or the sunset did not come, notice any disappointment and let it be there. It does not cancel the road.

Then the practical question that follows every end: what are you taking home? Not objects. A direction you like to lean, a pace, a way of being a guest, one small thing you put down and have not picked up again. It is enough to know that there is a home to take it to. You may find that tomorrow feels flat, with no arrows to follow. If so, plan it gently: a long breakfast, a message to someone, a short walk in a direction you have not tried. The next chapter says more about going on.

Three questions, and nothing to prove:

- What do I want to carry home from this road, and what am I glad to leave behind here?
- When on this walk did I feel most awake?
- Who do I want to tell about it, and what is the first thing I will say?

## A short prayer

*May the light be kind tonight, wherever it falls. Thank you, road, for the long way round. Let what I leave here stay here, and let what I carry be light. May the way home be easy, and may I walk it awake.*
