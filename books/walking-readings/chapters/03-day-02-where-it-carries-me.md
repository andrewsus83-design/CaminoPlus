# Day 2. Where it carries me

*What would it be to take a road for its own sake?*

## The passage

> Some do not walk at all; others walk in the highways; a few walk across lots. Roads are made for horses and men of business. I do not travel in them much, comparatively, because I am not in a hurry to get to any tavern or grocery or livery-stable or depot to which they lead. I am a good horse to travel, but not from choice a roadster. … However, there are a few old roads that may be trodden with profit, as if they led somewhere now that they are nearly discontinued. There is the Old Marlborough Road, which does not go to Marlborough now, methinks, unless that is Marlborough where it carries me. … Life consists with wildness. The most alive is the wildest. Not yet subdued to man, its presence refreshes him.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walking* (English original, 1862)

## Reflection

Every walker learns two kinds of road. One is built to get you there. It is straight and surfaced, it has a line down the middle, and it is not interested in the fact that you are on it. The other is a track between walls or hedges that exists because people kept walking along it, and it goes on, patiently, because they did. A morning on each is a different kind of weather. On the first you hear engines. On the second you may hear your own boots, then birds, then, now and again, nothing at all.

Thoreau has views on the first kind. “Roads are made for horses and men of business.” He is in no hurry to reach any tavern, grocery, livery-stable or depot, and so he keeps off them and walks across lots, which means across fields, which means wherever his feet can find a way. He is, he says, “a good horse to travel”, though not, from choice, a roadster. A good horse to travel can go a long way without fuss. A roadster is a horse schooled to the highway. He claims the first and declines the second.

Then he does something more interesting than turning his back on roads. He praises one particular sort: an old road so little used that it has lost its purpose. His own Old Marlborough Road, he says, does not go to Marlborough now, “unless that is Marlborough where it carries me.” It is a lovely sentence, because it turns a destination into a result. A road with no business left in it is a road for walking, and where it ends is wherever it takes you.

A pilgrim road is an old road in that sense. Almost nobody needs it for speed. There are quicker ways to the coast, and most of them have a line down the middle. People walk this one for the walking, and for the sea at the end.

The last lines of the passage come from a different part of the essay, and they say what he is really after: “The most alive is the wildest.” It is easy to hear that wrongly on a road that thousands of feet have worn smooth. Thoreau does love a wilderness, but he finds wildness close to home as well: in a bog beside the village, and in a neighbour’s cow that breaks out of her pasture early one spring and swims the river, which he cheers. Wildness, for him, is whatever has not been wholly arranged for us: weather, hunger, a bird in a wall, a cow in a lane, and the animal part of the walker that wants water and a chair. It is what we have not yet been trained out of, and its presence, he says, refreshes.

A word about lots. Thoreau crosses fields because, in his time and place, he could. Later in the essay he says that the walker enjoys “comparative freedom” there, and he fears the day when fences go up. You are walking among people’s homes and livelihoods. Stay on the way. The narrowness of a pilgrim road is a courtesy to the people whose fields lie beside it, and keeping to it is the freedom you give them.

Where is it hard? In two places.

First, he is a little sniffy about roads and the people who need them, and most of us need both. Somebody has put a mark at the corner where you were about to be lost. That is no insult to wildness. It is a courtesy, and you can accept it and still keep your eyes open.

Second, “wild” is a risky word on a walk. It is no reason to walk where you cannot be seen, to ignore the weather, or to stay on the wrong side of a busy road. Where this way meets traffic, the sensible thing is to walk where you can see what is coming. Thoreau’s wildness lives in the field beside the road, not in the middle of it. The same goes for weather, which can change within the hour. Carry water and a layer for rain, and let the wild be a pleasure you have prepared for.

And a small honest doubt. Some days the wildest thing you want is a chair and a hot meal. That is allowed. It is wild in its way. Nobody arranged your hunger.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Give one stretch of the road no purpose at all. Not “the way to the next village”, just this lane, this wall, this wind. Let it be the place you are, and not the means to somewhere else, and see what the road has been saying while you were busy getting along it.

## For the walk

- Which part of today’s walk could I do for its own sake, with no part of me waiting to arrive? If none, which part could come closest?
- Find something living that nobody planned: a bird, a weed in a wall, a cow at a gate, a cloud going a different way from you. Watch it for a full minute, sixty slow breaths if that helps, then walk on and notice how the road feels and whether you are walking any differently.

## At the end of the day

By now your feet know the road better than your head does. Tonight, look back over the day as a set of roads. Some stretches were the first kind, built to get you there: you walked them with your head down and a number in your mind, kilometres or minutes to the next place. Some were the second kind, a way that carried you. Which was which? It is not always the surface that decides. A paved lane can feel old, and a path through trees can feel like a corridor.

Then think about the wild, and begin with your own body, which is the wildest thing you carry. Tonight it is asking for water, salt, a hot shower, a chair with arms, a little silence. Give it as much of the list as you can. It asks without apology, and it is usually right.

Did anything else today refuse to be arranged? A change of wind, a shower, a smell, a sound you could not place, the mood of the afternoon. The body counts: its hunger, its limp, its surprising wish to sing or to lie down. None of it was on the plan. All of it was yours.

Somewhere today you were probably hurried by something: a bed to secure, a café before it closed, weather coming in. That is the road of horses and men of business, and you are allowed to use it. The only question is whether it took the whole of the day or only the part it needed.

Think, too, of the hard part, and give it a fair hearing. It may have been the length, or a road with no verge, or the plain monotony of putting one foot after the other. Say what it was. It does not have to be turned into a lesson. If it was a stretch where you felt unsafe, tell your host and ask about another way round tomorrow.

If you are reading at home, the same questions fit an ordinary day. Which of today’s roads were for business, and which for walking? Was there a lane, a park path or a bit of pavement that carried you? There nearly always is one.

If you can, step outside for a minute before bed. Look at what the evening is doing from the doorway: the light going, the smell of the ground, the first lit windows. Nobody arranged that either.

Tired walkers think in straight lines: food, bed, sleep. Before you lie down, spend a minute on a bend you liked. It is the part of the day that will not come back, and it takes no time to keep. Tomorrow’s road will have its own, and you will not know which until you are on it.

To take to sleep:

- Where today did the road simply carry me, and where was I only trying to get somewhere?
- What was the least arranged thing I met today, and how did it feel to meet it?
- What would I give a little less hurry to tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*May the road carry me where it will. May I be in no great hurry to arrive. Let something wild and small cross my path tomorrow, and let me stop for it. Let my feet be sure and my eyes be open. Let sleep come easily.*
