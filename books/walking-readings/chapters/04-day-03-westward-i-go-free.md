# Day 3. Westward I go free

*When nothing decides for you, which way do you turn?*

## The passage

> When I go out of the house for a walk, uncertain as yet whither I will bend my steps, and submit myself to my instinct to decide for me, I find, strange and whimsical as it may seem, that I finally and inevitably settle southwest, toward some particular wood or meadow or deserted pasture or hill in that direction. My needle is slow to settle—varies a few degrees, and does not always point due southwest, it is true, and it has good authority for this variation, but it always settles between west and south-southwest. The future lies that way to me, and the earth seems more unexhausted and richer on that side. … Eastward I go only by force; but westward I go free. Thither no business leads me. … Every sunset which I witness inspires me with the desire to go to a West as distant and as fair as that into which the sun goes down. He appears to migrate westward daily, and tempt us to follow him.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walking* (English original, 1862)

## Reflection

On a waymarked road you hardly ever choose a direction. Someone has chosen it for you and put a mark at the corner. It is restful, and it can make you forget that direction is a choice at all.

Thoreau has to decide every time. He steps out of his front door with no plan and lets his instinct decide, and he finds, strange and whimsical as it may seem, that he always ends up heading south-west or west. He turns round and round, sometimes for a quarter of an hour, irresolute, and then the needle settles. It is a slow needle, and it varies a few degrees. But it never points east. “Eastward I go only by force; but westward I go free.”

Then comes a short sentence that holds a whole idea: “Thither no business leads me.” Free, here, may not mean without limits. It may mean without errands. Elsewhere he says that we go east to study history and the works of art, and west into the future. East is where the obligations are, and west is where he has nothing to do. That is one way to read it, and it is ours, not his, but it fits the way a walk feels around the third afternoon. The road has stopped being a list of things to get done, and has begun to be a direction.

There is a second thing in the passage, and it is easy to miss: “The future lies that way to me.” For Thoreau, direction is not only geography. West is where he expects something to come from. You may have set out from a change, a loss, a decision, a job that ended, and feel that the road leads away from one thing and towards something you cannot see. You do not have to know what. That the earth “seems more unexhausted and richer on that side” is a very honest description of a hope: not a plan, only a feeling that there is more over there.

The last lines of the passage reach for something older than Thoreau. Every sunset, he says, makes him want to go to a West as distant and as fair as the one the sun goes down into, and the sun seems to tempt us to follow. He seems to take it for an old feeling: in the same paragraph he adds that the ancients appear to have set their islands of paradise out there, in the west.

Your road has that direction in it. Both Finisterre and Muxía lie west of Santiago, on the Atlantic coast, and the name Finisterre comes from the Latin for the end of the earth, *finis terrae*. The CaminoPlus catalogue gives the road to Finisterre as ~90 km, a figure worth checking before you walk.

And here is the difference between you and Thoreau. His west never ends. Whenever he walks, there is more of it, further on. Yours does. There is a coast, and beyond it only water. A direction is something you can follow for ever. A destination is something you arrive at. Over the next days you may find that you are walking both: leaning west out of long habit, and going to a particular place, on a particular day, where you will have to stop.

Two cautions.

The first concerns the West itself. Thoreau’s is the American one. Elsewhere in the essay it grows into the march of a nation across a continent, and the settlers’ advance, and the displacement of the people already living there, are treated as natural, even as good. The land to the west was not empty, and to walk west was never an innocent thing for everybody. We have chosen a passage that stays with one man and his compass, and we do not ask you to carry the rest.

The second is smaller, and kinder. A needle that “varies a few degrees” is an honest one. Your road will not run due west either. Roads rarely do. This one will bend round hills and villages, as most roads do, and for part of an afternoon it may point somewhere else altogether. That is not a failure of direction. It is what a path does when it respects the ground.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? To notice which way you lean. Not only on the map, but in the day: towards the next meal, towards the next person, towards the end. And, once, to follow something just because nothing is asking you to.

## For the walk

- Which way am I leaning today, on the map and in my mind, and is anything leading me there that I have not noticed?
- If the sun is out, watch your shadow. In the morning, walking west, it falls ahead of you. In the afternoon it falls behind. Notice the moment it changes sides. If it is cloudy, notice the wind and the light instead, and ask which part of the sky is brightest. If you are reading at home, find the west by the evening sun.

## At the end of the day

Somewhere around the middle of a walk, the road stops being new and starts to be a habit. Tonight, ask which way it led you, and not only on the map.

Think about what pulled you. The next village, the next meal, the sight of a bed, a walker just ahead whom you wanted to catch, or to avoid. These are small magnetisms, and they are fine. But there may also have been a quieter one, which you notice only when you stop: a wish to see how it ends. You may be able to name it by now.

Think about the hard part of today. It might have been the length, or the heat, or the rain, or a bend where the road turned away from where you wanted to go. Say it plainly, and then notice that you came through it.

And the good part: an easy stretch where the road and your legs agreed, perhaps with the light low in front of you, if the sky allowed it. A day has one of those more often than we give it credit for.

Thoreau draws the outline of his walks as a curve that opens to the west, with his house in the place where the sun would be. Home is the warm point you keep moving away from, and the pull of it does not stop. It may have been strongest this afternoon, or when you looked at your phone. That is allowed too.

As the end of a road comes closer, two wishes arrive together: to get there, and for it not to end. Both are fair. Hurrying to arrive and dawdling to avoid arriving are both ways of not being on the road. Whichever you feel, notice it without arguing, and tomorrow walk at the pace of the day and not of the end.

There is a question underneath all this, and you do not have to answer it tonight. Is it a place you are walking to, a direction you are walking in, or something you are walking away from? Grief, a change, a need to be out of a room: these are all good reasons to walk, and they point differently from a destination. Thoreau gives reasons for his west, but he also calls it an instinct, and an instinct does not always know its own reasons.

Before you sleep, find the west. If there is a window or a door, look at the part of the sky where the light went, even if cloud hides it. Tomorrow’s road lies in that direction, and it costs nothing to say good night to it.

Tired people steer by the nearest light. That is allowed. Tonight the only direction needed is the way to the bed, and the rest of the compass will keep until the morning.

Three questions for tonight:

- What has been pulling me west, and what else has been pulling me that I have not named?
- Where today did I follow something just because nothing was asking me to?
- Am I walking toward a place, in a direction, or away from something?

## A short prayer

*May I know which way I am leaning. May the light be kind at the end of the day. Let me follow the next small thing that calls me, and let it be good. If I am lost for a while, let me be lost gently. May my feet keep their direction while I sleep.*
