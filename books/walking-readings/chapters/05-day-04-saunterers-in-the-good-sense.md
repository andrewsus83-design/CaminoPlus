# Day 4. Saunterers in the good sense

*What are you walking toward, even if you cannot yet name it?*

## The passage

> I have met with but one or two persons in the course of my life who understood the art of Walking, that is, of taking walks—who had a genius, so to speak, for sauntering, which word is beautifully derived “from idle people who roved about the country, in the Middle Ages, and asked charity, under pretense of going à la Sainte Terre,” to the Holy Land, till the children exclaimed, “There goes a Sainte-Terrer,” a Saunterer, a Holy-Lander. They who never go to the Holy Land in their walks, as they pretend, are indeed mere idlers and vagabonds; but they who do go there are saunterers in the good sense, such as I mean. … Some, however, would derive the word from sans terre without land or a home, which, therefore, in the good sense, will mean, having no particular home, but equally at home everywhere. For this is the secret of successful sauntering. He who sits still in a house all the time may be the greatest vagrant of all; but the saunterer, in the good sense, is no more vagrant than the meandering river, which is all the while sedulously seeking the shortest course to the sea.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walking* (English original, 1862)

## Reflection

Thoreau tells a story about a word. It is a good story, and it is almost certainly not true.

The word is “saunter”. In his account it comes from idle people who roved the country in the Middle Ages and begged, saying they were on their way “à la Sainte Terre”, to the Holy Land, until the children shouted “There goes a Sainte-Terrer.” A saunterer, then, is a Holy-Lander. Those who never reach the Holy Land in their walks, he says, are mere idlers and vagabonds. Those who do are saunterers “in the good sense”.

Say it plainly: this is Thoreau’s etymology, and it is doubted. Etymologists do not generally accept it, and they see no link between “saunter” and the Holy Land. The origin of the word is usually called uncertain. He offers a second derivation, from the French *sans terre*, “without land”, and says he prefers the first, “the most probable derivation”. Neither is accepted today. So read the story for what it is: a metaphor, told by a man who likes a good tale. The idea inside it does not need the history to be true.

What is the idea? That the difference between a wanderer and a saunterer is not speed, or distance, or even religion. It is whether the walk is going somewhere in the walker’s own mind. Thoreau leaves the Holy Land undefined, which is the generous thing to do. For one reader it may be a church, for another the sea, a person, a rest, a quieter head. Whatever you put there, a walk that has one is a different walk from a walk that has none.

Notice, too, what he says before the story: that in his whole life he has met only one or two people who understood the art of walking, “who had a genius, so to speak, for sauntering”. He means a way of paying attention, not stamina. A little later he adds, in Latin, that a walker is born and not made. We doubt it. You may find that a few days on a road teach you more of the art than you expected.

The second derivation gives the better lines. A saunterer would be someone “having no particular home”, and so “equally at home everywhere”. That will sound familiar to anyone with a pack on their back. You own what you carry. The bed changes every night. You are a guest in all of it, and some evenings the guest is comfortable and some evenings not.

Then the river. The saunterer, he says, is no more a vagrant than a meandering river that is, all the while, seeking “the shortest course to the sea”. It is a consoling picture for a winding path. A river’s bends are not a failure to go straight. They are the shortest way the ground allows. Your road bends round hills and through villages for the same sort of reason, and it is going to the sea.

Three things in the passage are hard.

First, he is unkind to the idle, roving poor of his own story, and the line does him no credit. Pilgrim roads have long relied on the hospitality of people who gave a bed or a meal to strangers.

Second, “equally at home everywhere” is a fine ideal and a difficult one. There will be an evening when you are at home nowhere: a strange bed, sore feet, a room full of other people’s sleep. It will not help to be told that you should feel otherwise. The ideal is a direction and not a test.

Third, “Holy Land” is a religious phrase in a book that is not religious. We have kept it because it is his, and because he stretches it so far that it fits any reader. If it does not fit you, put your own word in its place. A reader who is Christian, or of another faith, or of none, may hear it differently on different days, and that is all right.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? To let the road be crooked and not mind. To keep the point of the walk somewhere in view without staring at it. And to be a good guest: at home enough to rest, and light enough to leave in the morning.

## For the walk

- What am I walking toward today, even if I cannot name it? If nothing comes, let that be the answer for now. Some walks only show their Holy Land when you arrive.
- For one hour, do not check the distance or the time. Let the bends be the way, as they are for a river, and notice how the walking changes when you stop measuring it. If you cannot resist, check once at the very end, and see how far you came without counting.

## At the end of the day

Tonight the question of home is a practical one. Where are you sleeping, and how much does it feel like yours? Some places take a person in at once: a clean sheet, a kind word, a chair that fits. Others need a night to warm up. Think about how it was to be a guest today, and about whoever played host, even if they were only a stranger who held a door.

Take a moment, too, over the pack on the floor. What did it hold that you used today, and what is only weight? There may be something you have carried for days and not touched. You do not have to decide tonight. But the saunterer with no particular home carries what the road asks for and not what the worry asked for.

Then think about the bends. What went the long way round today: a path round a field, a wait you did not plan, a wrong turn, a stretch that felt twice as long as the sign said? From here, which of them looks like part of the shortest way the ground allowed? You may find that some of them were the best of the day. You may find that none of them were, and that is honest too.

There may have been a moment when you felt at home nowhere. A strange town in the late afternoon, an unanswered message, a meal eaten alone at a table meant for two. It is part of the walk, and it passes. You do not have to turn it into wisdom. Put it down next to whatever was warm.

If a host was kind to you today, thank them once more in your mind. A roof and a bed are the oldest gift of any road, and the story of the idle beggars leaves out the people who put the pilgrim up. However far the sea is tonight, your river is still going to it.

A small exercise for the bed: name three things in the room that make it bearable. The light switch you can find in the dark, the window, the sound of a tap. Being at home everywhere is built of small things like these, one room at a time. Some evenings the Holy Land is a hot meal. If that is what lies ahead, then it is.

A tired saunterer is just a person who wants a shower. That is a perfectly good thing to be, and the Holy Land, whatever you have put there, will not mind waiting until you have had one. After that, eat something warm if you can, and let the day finish itself without any help from you.

Questions to sleep on:

- Where did I feel at home today, and what made it so?
- What was I walking toward today, even if I could not have said it aloud?
- Which bend, delay or wrong turn now looks like part of the shortest way?

## A short prayer

*May I be a good guest and a good host. May the road bend as it needs to, and may I not mind. Let me be at home in this bed tonight and light enough to leave it. Let the long way round be the right way for me. Let sleep find me quickly.*
