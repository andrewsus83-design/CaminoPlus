# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the road, or the end of the five days, or both. This book gives no certificate. It was meant as company for a short walk west, and a road has no use for a book once the land has run out, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep.

Not everything. Nobody carries a whole book home. Choose three small things, one of each kind.

- **A direction.** Thoreau’s needle settled in the west. Yours may settle somewhere else. Notice which way you lean when nothing decides for you, and give it a little room when you are home. One evening this week, walk toward wherever the day ends for you, with no errand at all.
- **A line.** One sentence from the five days that stayed in your head. It might be “westward I go free”, or the gentler “unless that is Marlborough where it carries me”, or “an infinite number of evenings”. Write it on a card and put it where you will see it.
- **A practice.** One of the small things from the end of each morning: ten minutes of walking as if you were not coming back, a minute with something nobody planned, a shadow noticed, an hour without measuring, a turn all the way round. Pick the one that cost you least and helped most. Do it again tomorrow.

If you can keep all three for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from a week away.

## How to go on

Go back whenever you like. The days are short, and they were written to be read more than once. A day that did nothing for you at the start may say a good deal on a grey Tuesday in March. Read them in another order. Read only the evenings for a week. Read the first passage aloud on a morning when you cannot get out of the door.

You might also read Thoreau himself. *Walking* is about 12,000 words, it is in the public domain, and you will find it at Project Gutenberg, ebook number 1022. Read the parts we left out with the same calm we have tried to keep. You will meet his jokes about knowledge and names, his fondness for swamps and, in places, assumptions that we named at the start and have not repeated. Some of it you will love, and some of it may make you wince. Both are fair responses to an essay that is more than a century and a half old.

He said he could not keep his health and spirits unless he spent “four hours a day at least” walking. Few of us have that. An hour is a good start. A walk to the end of your own road and back counts, and so does a walk to the end of the next one.

And be honest about what did not work. Some days will have rubbed. A passage may have asked for more than you had, or sounded too sure, or too far from your life. That is allowed. A book of this kind is not a test. If a day did not speak to you, nothing has gone wrong. Leave it, and take what did.

## A last word

Thoreau died in 1862, at 44, and the essay reached readers after him. It ends with a hope and not a promise: that one day the light will shine “into our minds and hearts”. He could not say when, and we cannot either. What a road can do is smaller and surer. It gives you a long afternoon, a direction and a reason to look up.

If you are at the sea, look at it once more before you go. If you are at home, find the west on the next clear evening, and go and stand where you can see the sky. Then walk again, next year if you can, or tomorrow, around the block, with the phone left behind.

*Buen Camino.*
