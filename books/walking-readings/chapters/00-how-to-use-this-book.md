# How to use this book

This is a small book for a short road. It has five days, one to read on each day of a walk, and it is made to go with the walk from Santiago de Compostela to Finisterre and Muxía: the road that leaves the cathedral city and goes west to the Atlantic coast. The CaminoPlus catalogue gives the route as ~90 km, measured to Finisterre. The figure is approximate, so check the current route before you go. If your walk takes more than five days, rest for a day or read one day twice. If it takes fewer, read two on a long evening. The book will not mind. It works at home too, with five mornings and five evenings and no boots at all.

Every day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from *Walking*, an essay by the American writer Henry David Thoreau. The words are exactly his, in the English he wrote. Read them twice, the second time aloud if you can. He wrote for the ear, and the essay began as lectures.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Thoreau’s. It starts from something on the road, a verge, a wall, a bend, and lets the passage open it. It says plainly what he says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt, and sometimes to disagree.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back over the day you have just lived, with the morning’s passage in mind. It asks two or three gentle questions and is honest about tiredness.
- **A short prayer.** It is a prayer for those who pray and a wish for those who do not, so say it either way, and change any word that does not fit.

## Not a Christian book

*Walking* is not a devotional book, and this is not a Christian reading. Thoreau was one of the New England writers called Transcendentalists, and here he writes about wildness and freedom. He uses the language of pilgrimage freely, as a picture. We have let him say what he says and made him say nothing more. People walk to the sea for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here.

## The way west

The essay walks west, and so does the road. Thoreau says that when he goes out for a walk with no plan, he always ends up heading west, or a little south of west, and the essay finishes at a sunset. Finisterre and Muxía both lie west of Santiago, and the name Finisterre comes from the Latin for the end of the earth. The five days follow that line.

- **Day 1, leaving the house.** What it means to be ready to go.
- **Day 2, wildness and the open road.** Roads for business, roads for walking, and what is wild.
- **Day 3, the west.** Which way you turn when nothing decides for you.
- **Day 4, sauntering as a kind of pilgrimage.** A famous story about a word, and the idea inside it.
- **Day 5, the sunset and the shore.** What the edge of the land asks of you, and what comes after.

The book cannot know which day of your walk is the last, so read the day that fits the day you are having. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## Two things to know

Thoreau tells a well-known story about where the word “saunter” comes from. It is his story, and it is doubted. Day 4 says so plainly. The essay also carries the assumptions of its time, about the West, about Native peoples and about who is free to walk. We have chosen passages that do not rest on them, and where a day comes close, it says so, briefly. The next chapter has the rest.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk in an *albergue* (the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep), at a café table while the soup arrives. Then take the question with you for an hour. Read the evening part last, when the boots are off. At home, do the same with your tea and before bed.

*Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road. Roughly, it means: may your Way go well.

Read slowly. The essay was written to be spoken, and it sounds better aloud.
