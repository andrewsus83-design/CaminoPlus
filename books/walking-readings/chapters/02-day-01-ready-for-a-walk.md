# Day 1. Ready for a walk

*What would you have to put down before you could simply go?*

## The passage

> It is true, we are but faint-hearted crusaders, even the walkers, nowadays, who undertake no persevering, never-ending enterprises. Our expeditions are but tours, and come round again at evening to the old hearth-side from which we set out. Half the walk is but retracing our steps. We should go forth on the shortest walk, perchance, in the spirit of undying adventure, never to return,—prepared to send back our embalmed hearts only as relics to our desolate kingdoms. If you are ready to leave father and mother, and brother and sister, and wife and child and friends, and never see them again,—if you have paid your debts, and made your will, and settled all your affairs, and are a free man; then you are ready for a walk.
>
> — Henry David Thoreau, *Walking* (English original, 1862)

## Reflection

The night before a walk, most of us make a list. It is mostly things to take: socks, plasters, a charger, a hat. Thoreau’s list runs the other way. It is a list of things to put down. Debts paid. A will made. Every affair settled. Father and mother, brother and sister, wife and child and friends, all left behind. Only then, he says, are you ready for a walk.

He is swinging a very large hammer, and he knows it. Near the start of the essay he warns that he means to make “an extreme statement”, and this is one. Hear it as half joke, half dare. A will, for an afternoon’s stroll! But the joke has an edge, and the edge is the point. Most of our walks are not leavings at all. They are tours that “come round again at evening” to the hearth they set out from, and half of every one is the same ground in reverse. We go out with a hand still on the door. He includes himself in that “we”, and fairly: most of the walks in his essay are afternoons near home.

Notice the flourish in the middle, the hearts embalmed and sent back as relics to desolate kingdoms. That is Thoreau enjoying himself. The whole essay goes like this: a big claim, a grin, and under the grin something he means. The word to listen for is the last one on the list, *free*. He opens by saying that he wants to speak for “absolute Freedom and Wildness”, and you can read everything after as an attempt to say what that would feel like on foot.

What he asks for is a spirit, not a distance. Even the shortest walk, he says, can be taken “in the spirit of undying adventure”. The difference is in what you are holding when you step out. Someone who has settled things, with nothing pulling at their sleeve, walks differently. They look up. They arrive in the field they are standing in, not the office they left.

The point of the list is not to be rid of your life. It is to put it somewhere safe, so that your attention can come with you. The debts and the will are a way of saying that nothing is left tangled behind you. You cannot carry a tangle and a pack at once, and by the second day the tangle may have shrunk to its true size.

Your road is a good place to try it, because it is not a circle. It leaves Santiago de Compostela and goes on towards the Atlantic coast, and each evening’s bed is a day further from your own door than the last. There is no hearth-side to come round to at six o’clock.

Now the hard parts, and there are three.

The first is the list. Hardly anyone is free in that way, and most of us should not want to be. There are people who would be frightened if you did not come back, and they are right to expect you. Nobody is asking you to walk out of your life. The word “never” belongs to the figure of speech. The mood is the part you can borrow.

The second is his word “crusaders”. It carries the memory of real wars, and he is using it only for a picture: someone who goes forth with nothing held back. Take the picture and leave the word.

The third is a plain doubt. If you wait until you are ready in his sense, you will not go. Nobody has paid every debt. The will is not made, the affairs are not settled, and there is a message you meant to answer a week ago. A walk has to begin anyway, in the middle of an unfinished life, and that may be the more honest kind of readiness.

So what does it ask of an ordinary morning? Something much smaller than a will. Pay one small debt: answer the one message that is sitting on you, or tell someone where you will sleep tonight. Choose one worry and put it down for the day, with a time to pick it up again if you still want it. Then go out as if this day were the whole of the walk. It is a decent way to spend a short walk, and a decent way to begin a long one. The list is not a hurdle. If you are reading this with your boots on, you have already passed the only test it sets.

## For the walk

- What am I still holding that I could put down for the length of this walk? Give it one word, and leave the word on the doorstep.
- For the first ten minutes, walk as if you were not coming back this way. Look at each thing once, properly, the way you look at something you will not see again. Then let the trick go, and notice whether the rest of the day is any different. If you are walking with someone, tell them what you are doing and ask them to try it too.

## At the end of the day

The first evening has its own small sounds: boots set down by a door, a tap running, a phone searching for the one free socket. Wherever you are tonight, it is some way from the door you shut this morning. If you are reading at home, the same is true of the person who sat down at the desk. Let the day be over.

Look back at it as a leaving. What did you actually put down this morning? It may be nothing you could name. It may be one small thing: a message answered, a worry set on the table, an hour of “what if”. Say it to yourself in a sentence. Then ask the other question: what came with you after all? A plan for how the walk ought to feel. A comparison with somebody else’s pace. The office, which has a way of walking quietly behind. These are not failures. They are only heavier than they looked in the morning, and now you know where they sit on you.

One affair does need settling tonight, and it is not on Thoreau’s list: your feet. Look at them. Wash them, air them, and deal with a sore patch before it becomes a blister. If anything worries you, ask a pharmacist or a doctor. It is the most practical way to be ready for tomorrow.

Some of the day was good. Think of one stretch when you were simply where you were: a gate, a wall in the sun, a smell from a kitchen, a few minutes when you forgot to look at the time. It is probably short. It counts, and it is worth a second look now, while the day is still warm.

And some of it was hard, perhaps the first hour, when the pack argued with your shoulders, or the long middle, when you wondered why you had come. Name it kindly and set it down beside the good part. Neither needs a verdict tonight.

You may sleep badly. A strange bed, other people’s breathing, a day still humming in your legs: poor sleep on the first night is ordinary, and it does not mean the walk is going wrong. Lying still counts as rest even when it is not sleep.

A word about tiredness. A tired person is a poor judge of a whole walk. Tonight’s mood is not the book’s verdict on the road, or on you, and nothing has to be settled before sleep. The list of unfinished affairs will scroll once or twice behind your eyes. Let it scroll. By morning it will be shorter, or at least lighter, and the road will still be going west.

Questions for tonight:

- What did I put down today, even for an hour, that I expected to keep holding?
- Where did I walk as though I might not pass this way again?
- Who is expecting to hear that I am well, and what would I like to tell them?

## A short prayer

*Let me be where my feet are. Let what I left at home be safe without me for a night. May what I carry be only what I need. May the bed be warm and the evening kind. Tomorrow, let me begin again, lightly.*
