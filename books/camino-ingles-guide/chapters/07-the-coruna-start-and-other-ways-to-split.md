# The A Coruña start, and other ways to split the days

## The hundred-kilometre rule, in plain words

The Compostela is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons and meet its rules. On foot or on horseback, the Office asks for the last hundred kilometres on a single recognised route, in one continuous stretch, with the last stage ending at the cathedral. By bicycle it asks for the last two hundred. Your stamped credencial is the proof.

The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.

The English Way is only just long enough to meet the rule. From Ferrol it is ~113 km, so you can start there and qualify. Add up the stage figures in this book and you will see how little margin there is: from Neda, the next stage town, you would have ~98 km left, which falls just short. That is why many pilgrims start in Ferrol. Wikipedia says that Ferrol is the only start on this route in Spain that is far enough from Santiago, and the official English Way site calls Ferrol a perfectly valid starting point for the Compostela.

If you start in Ferrol, walk every stage on foot and keep your credencial stamped, you meet the distance and the stamp rules, and the Office will talk to you about the rest when you arrive. A word of caution on the figures: published lengths differ by a kilometre or two, and one page of the official site gives ~119 km for the whole route. If you are planning a start close to the limit, ask the Office.

## Starting in A Coruña

The Camino Inglés has a second start in A Coruña, the city of the Tower of Hercules. This is a shorter variant: the official English Way site gives it as ~74 km, the Xunta as ~73 km. The official site calls it the Camiño do Faro, the Lighthouse Way, after the Roman lighthouse that still stands in the city. A Coruña is the traditional landing port of the sea pilgrims, and the official history names it as the great meeting point of those who came by ship.

It can be walked in three or four days. The official stages are A Coruña to Sergude (~20 km), Sergude to Bruma (~13 km), and then the two stages shared with the Ferrol branch. The two branches meet at the chapel of As Travesas, just over ~2 km before Hospital de Bruma. The official site calls the second stage physically very demanding because of its long climb, and very rewarding for the landscape. You can also walk from A Coruña to Bruma in one day, which the site puts at ~33 km.

Fewer pilgrims choose it. In 2024, as Wikipedia reports from the Pilgrim's Office statistics, about 96 per cent of those who finished the English Way started in Ferrol and about 2 per cent in A Coruña.

### Why A Coruña alone does not give the Compostela

At ~74 km, the A Coruña route is short of a hundred. The Office's rules do contain a provision for people who have already walked a recognised route outside Spain: they need no less than seventy kilometres in Spain. The A Coruña route is a little more than that. The Office also says the Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special cases, and that it should be contacted in advance.

The official English Way site describes a way to make up the distance with an earlier walk, and Wikipedia reports a rule from December 2016 which it states as a certified twenty-five kilometres on a pilgrimage route linked to St James in the walker's own country. The sources describe the rule differently, and rules change. For that reason this book does not tell you it works. If you want the Compostela and plan to start in A Coruña, ask the Pilgrim's Office, before you go, what it accepts in your case. The official site also says that segments of different Caminos cannot be mixed, so ask about yours.

In England, the association names Finchale Priory in County Durham as the official English starting point, and a route there was recognised in 2019. Whether a walk on that route counts towards the Compostela is for the Pilgrim's Office to say.

## Other ways to split the six days

The six stages in this book are the usual stage towns. They are not the only plan.

- **Five days.** The official planning page says five or six stages from Ferrol. The Xunta's own stages are Ferrol to Neda, Neda to Miño (~22 km), Miño to Bruma (~35 km), Bruma to Sigüeiro and Sigüeiro to Santiago. A ~35 km day is a long one for most people, and this book does not suggest it.
- **Seven, eight or nine days.** The official leaflet divides the long days into shorter sections: Pontedeume to Miño (~10 km) and Miño to Betanzos (~10 km); Betanzos to Presedo (~12 km) and Presedo to Bruma (~11.5 km); and Bruma to Poulo (~10 km) and Poulo to Sigüeiro (~15 km). Add one split and you have seven days; add all three and you have nine.
- **A slow plan.** If you have time, take any of these and add a rest day at Betanzos. It is a town worth an afternoon.

## Where you will sleep

Which towns have a public hostel, and which are open this month, changes from season to season, so this book does not list them. Use the CaminoPlus app and the official English Way site for where to sleep tonight, and ask locally for tomorrow. How the hostels work is in the chapter on sleeping, eating and resting. The official site also lists baggage-transport services if you would rather walk with a lighter pack; ask which cover your days.
