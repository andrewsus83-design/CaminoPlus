# Body, weather and safety

This chapter is plain on purpose. Nothing here is a reason to be afraid of the English Way, which the official site rates low to medium and which stays under ~500 m. It is a list of the things worth taking seriously.

## Feet and legs

Your feet decide how the walk goes. Stop at the first hot spot and dress it, before it becomes a blister. Change your socks at lunch if they are damp. Rest for a few minutes each hour, take your pack off, and eat and drink before you are hungry or thirsty. If you feel a sharp or growing pain in a knee, a shin or the top of a foot, stop. Rest it for a day, or take a shorter stage. A day lost to rest is better than a week lost to an injury.

## Rain and cold

Rain is the thing to prepare for, as the official site says. Wet clothes cool you quickly, even in mild weather. Carry a waterproof jacket and a pack cover, and keep one dry layer in a bag that cannot get wet. Paths, steps and old stones are slippery in the wet, so shorten your stride and use your poles, if you have them.

In Santiago the average daily high in January is ~12 °C and the low ~5 °C, and the daylight lasts only ~9 hours at the shortest. In cold, wet weather, if you start to shiver, stop in a warm place before you try to go on. Do not start a long day in the dark.

## Heat

July averages in Santiago are ~25 °C by day, and a hot spell can be far warmer than the average. On a hot day, start early, wear a hat, put on sun cream, and carry more water than you think you need. Rest in the shade through the hottest hours. A headache, dizziness or nausea in the heat are signs to stop, get into the shade, and drink. If they do not pass, ask for help.

## Roads, paths and dogs

Most of the way is on tracks and paths. The official site says the Camino Inglés is clearly signposted along most of its route, with yellow arrows on milestones and at key points, and it suggests a map for the hidden details. Two stretches are close to traffic. On the fourth day, just before Hospital de Bruma, the path runs beside the main road, and the official site advises caution. On the sixth day the path runs parallel to the N-550 on small tracks with little traffic, and you should stay on the tracks.

Wherever you must walk on a road, face the oncoming traffic, keep well to the edge, and wear something bright. Never walk on a road in the dark.

Dogs on farms may bark as you pass. Walk on calmly and do not stare or run. If you are afraid of them, ask in the village before you set out.

## Staying safe on your own

The official site notes that the route passes through many populated areas, and also reminds you to keep your belongings in check. Tell someone where you plan to sleep each night, and message them when you arrive. Keep your phone charged, carry your map offline, and know the name of the last village you passed.

## When to stop

Stop, and get help, for chest pain, a fever, severe dizziness, a fall with a blow to the head, a swollen joint, or anything that frightens you. A doctor is the right person for a worry, and a plan can always change. The Way will still be there.

## Emergencies

**112** is the European emergency number and it works in Spain. Say where you are as exactly as you can: the village, the stage and the last landmark you passed.

## Check before you go

In dry summers, ask locally about fires and any closed paths. After heavy rain, ask about flooded sections and slippery descents. Closures and diversions change, so look at the official site and the current information in the app before each day.
