# The ría days: Ferrol to Betanzos

## The character of these days

The first three days belong to the water. You walk out of a naval city with an inlet of the Atlantic on your right, a *ría*, as Galicians call these long sea inlets, and you follow the estuaries of the Eume, the Lambre and the Mandeo through small towns that grew up where the tide ends. The days are short at first (~15 km, ~13 km) and then longer (~20 km). Every night is in a town with an old centre, and the second and third days each bring you to an old bridge.

The distances in this book come from the official English Way site. The Xunta de Galicia and the federation of friends of the Camino give slightly different figures, by up to about a kilometre, and the official site's own pages disagree with each other in one place. Treat every figure as a good guide, not as a measurement.

### Day 1: Ferrol to Neda (~15 km)

The official site rates this day low, and estimates three to four hours. It is the gentlest start on offer, and an unusual one: you leave a naval city by its harbour. Ferrol has built ships for most of its history, the Royal Dockyards were built between 1726 and 1783, and its sheltered harbour is often called the best natural port in Europe. The official route starts at the port of Curuxeiras, in the old quarter, and crosses the city by the Calle Real, with the naval-military area on your right. Take a moment for the co-cathedral of San Xiao and the convent of San Francisco before the town falls away.

Then the sea takes over. It stays on your right for almost the whole day. Ferrol and Neda are only ~7 km apart as a gull flies, but the path has to go right round the head of the inlet, which is why a short line becomes a ~15 km walk. Along the way you pass the Romanesque monastery of San Martiño de Xuvia, once a double monastery, with three Romanesque apses and a medieval sarcophagus in its atrium, in front of an eighteenth-century tower. Shortly after leaving Narón you pass the Royal Flour Factory, built in 1775 and now a visitor centre. The official gastronomy page lists the bread of Neda, and a visit to the town's wheat mills.

In Neda you will find the churches of Santa María and the Gothic San Nicolás. The official site says you can stop here or go on ~16 km to Pontedeume. This book stops, because a short first day is not a weakness. Get your first *sello* before you leave Ferrol and a second here. Then wash your socks, look at your feet, and eat something local.

### Day 2: Neda to Pontedeume (~13 km)

The official site rates this day low and calls it a quiet stage with only one climb, at the exit of Neda. It is the shortest day of the walk, and the one where the Camino is closest to a stroll.

Leaving Neda, you pass the churches again, cross the River Belelle by a footbridge, and join the old arcaded Calle Real. In the middle of it stand the Clock Tower and the Town Hall, beside the remains of the old pilgrims' hospital, dedicated to the Holy Spirit. It is the first of the hospitals that this road remembers, and it is worth stopping for. At San Nicolás the official site points out one of the oldest crosses in Galicia.

The climb out of the town is the only real one of the day, and from the top you can see the Ferrol ría, which shows you how far you have already come. After a washing place and a fountain you pass Fene, a town with services for pilgrims. Beyond the washing place of Mundín, a dirt track climbs towards the town hall of Cabanas. As you drop into Cabanas you see the mouth of the River Eume. A few kilometres upstream it forms the Fragas do Eume Natural Park. Ahead, on the mountain, you can see the castle of Nogueirosa, which Wikipedia credits to Fernán Pérez de Andrade, the fourteenth-century knight known as *o Bóo*, "the good one".

The bridge over the Eume used to be far longer. The official site says it has only 15 arches today and once had 70. On the far side, Pontedeume is a town founded by a charter of Alfonso X the Wise in 1270, with arcades, wooden balconies and glass galleries. The historic quarter holds the Tower of Andrade, part of the palace of the Andrade family, the church of Santiago, the Town Hall and the Augustinian convent.

The official site's two pages disagree about this stage: one says ~13 km from Neda to Pontedeume, another says ~16 km. Expect ~13. Do not push on. Tomorrow starts with a steep kilometre, and the best use of this afternoon is to rest.

### Day 3: Pontedeume to Betanzos (~20 km)

This is the first medium day, with an estimate of about six hours. The official site gives the one warning that matters: the exit from Pontedeume is a steep slope of one kilometre. After that the climbing eases into a gentle descent through farmland, down to sea level at the Baxoi bridge, which the official site says pilgrims crossed in the Middle Ages. From here the tide is close again.

The path passes Miño and enters the mouth of the Lambre, part of the Natura 2000 network of protected areas, where you follow the river for a short stretch and cross a Gothic bridge. Then you climb to a viewpoint above the mouth of the Mandeo, the estuary of Betanzos. The descent goes through vineyards to the Romanesque church of San Martiño de Tiobre, which the official site calls an obligatory stop. The wine of these vineyards was once famous, and the site says it was sought after by the British Navy: a small reminder of how many ties this coast had with Britain.

One more climb brings you to Os Remedios, a Renaissance church, which the official site calls something rare in Galicia. Then you cross the old bridge over the Mandeo and enter Betanzos by the gate of A Ponte Vella.

Betanzos has one of the best preserved old quarters in Galicia, and it is worth an evening. The church of San Francisco was built in 1387 by order of Fernán Pérez de Andrade, and his tomb, carved with hunting scenes, is inside. The church of Santiago, from the fifteenth century, was built by the tailors' guild, and has a portal with an equestrian statue of the saint. The town's omelette, the *tortilla de Betanzos*, is a local speciality.

The Xunta's own stages end at Miño, and the official leaflet splits this day there too, into two walks of ~10 km each. If your feet are tired after the climbs, that is a fair plan. Whichever you choose, it is the longest day so far, so carry lunch.
