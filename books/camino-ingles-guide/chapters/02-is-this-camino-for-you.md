# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

The official site rates four of the six days as low difficulty and two, the third and the fourth, as medium. It calls the fourth the most demanding, and the hardest for most pilgrims, while adding that this does not mean it is very difficult. The Xunta rates the whole route medium. In plain terms: nothing here needs mountaineering, and the highest ground stays under ~500 m. What it asks for is steady legs, good shoes and a willingness to walk in the rain.

The days are not long by Camino standards. The official planning page gives an average of 20 to 25 km a day and suggests five or six stages from Ferrol. This book uses six. The first two days are short, ~15 km and ~13 km, which is kind: you meet your feet before they meet the hills. The two longest days are the fourth and fifth, each ~24 km. The fourth is hilly, and the fifth is long but mostly downhill.

## Who it suits

- **First-time pilgrims** who want to try a real Camino in about a week, without committing to a month.
- **Anyone with limited time**, such as a long weekend plus a few days, who still wants to arrive in Santiago on foot.
- **Walkers who want the Compostela** and meet the Pilgrim's Office rule of the last hundred kilometres. Ferrol is the one start on this route that does so on its own (see the chapter on the A Coruña start).
- **Returners** who have walked the Francés or the Portugués and would like a quieter, shorter Way with a good deal of history.
- **People who like water and old towns.** The first three days run through estuary towns with medieval centres.
- **Those walking for a reason that needs quiet**: grief, a decision, a rest from work. Six days is a good length for it.

## Who might choose another

- If you want a **long journey**, with weeks of walking and the rhythm of a long-distance life, choose a longer Camino. The Camino Francés is ~773 km, the Portugués Central ~240 km, the Norte ~820 km.
- If you want the **sea beside you all the way**, the English Way is only half that: the estuaries are in the first half, and the second half is inland. The Portuguese Coastal Way, ~260 km, stays near the coast.
- If you want **a big crowd** of fellow pilgrims every evening, this is not the busiest road. About one pilgrim in eighteen who arrived in Santiago in 2024 had walked it, by the Pilgrim's Office figure as reported by Wikipedia.
- If you hope to earn the Compostela **from A Coruña alone**, it does not work: that start is ~73 km, short of the hundred. A later chapter explains the exceptions.
- If you are a **cyclist** and want the Compostela, the rule is the last two hundred kilometres, and this route gives you ~113 km. You would need a longer approach by another route.

## Compared with its neighbours, in general terms

The English Way is about the same length as the last stretch of the Camino Francés from Sarria, which is ~115 km. It is a smaller company than the busiest roads, and its first half, along the estuaries, has a character of its own. On a quieter road there are fewer pilgrims to share news of a bed or a closed cafe, so plan each evening as carefully as each day. It is much shorter than the Portuguese and Northern Ways, and Ferrol and A Coruña both have train and bus stations, with Santiago's airport not far away.

## Walking it alone

If you walk alone, know that the official site describes the route as passing through many populated areas, and still reminds you to keep your belongings in check. A few days on a quiet road is a good way to find out whether you like your own company. Tell someone your plan, and check in each evening. The chapter on body, weather and safety says more.

## A short test

If you can walk ~20 km on a weekend, with a pack, in the shoes you plan to wear, and still want to walk the next day, you are ready. If you cannot yet, you have a month or two to train, and the chapter called "Before you go" tells you how.
