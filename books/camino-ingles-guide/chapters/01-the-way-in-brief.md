# The Way in brief

## Where it starts and ends

The Camino Inglés starts in Ferrol, a naval city on a long, sheltered inlet of the Atlantic, and ends at the cathedral in Santiago de Compostela. Every step is in Galicia, in the province of A Coruña. The official site of the association of councils along the route gives the length from Ferrol as ~113 km, and the Xunta de Galicia, the regional government, gives ~112 km. Other pages say ~110 km and ~119 km, and you will often see a rounded ~120 km. All of them describe the same road. This book uses ~113 km, which is also what the six stage figures add up to.

The usual six days, with the towns this book follows:

- Day 1: Ferrol to Neda, ~15 km
- Day 2: Neda to Pontedeume, ~13 km
- Day 3: Pontedeume to Betanzos, ~20 km
- Day 4: Betanzos to Hospital de Bruma, ~24 km
- Day 5: Hospital de Bruma to Sigüeiro, ~24 km
- Day 6: Sigüeiro to Santiago de Compostela, ~16 km

Plans differ: the Xunta's stages and the official leaflet divide the route in other ways, and a later chapter shows how. These are the usual stage towns.

## What it feels like

The first half belongs to the water. You leave Ferrol with the sea on your right for almost the whole of the first day, then follow the estuaries of the Eume and the Mandeo through small towns with arcaded streets, medieval bridges and Romanesque churches. The official site says the route starts at sea level and climbs inland without passing ~500 m, so this is a gentle Camino by the standards of the long ones.

Gentle does not mean flat. Several days begin with a steep climb, and the fourth day, over the hills between Betanzos and Hospital de Bruma, is the one the official site calls the most demanding. After that the path turns inland for good: woodland, hamlets, rural churches, a long gradual descent, a medieval bridge over the Tambre and, on the last morning, an oak forest called the Enchanted Forest before the city.

## Why it exists

The Way of St James is a pilgrimage to the tomb of the apostle in Santiago. The Pilgrim's Office dates its beginning to the discovery of the tomb under King Alfonso II, who reigned from 792 to 842, and calls the twelfth and thirteenth centuries its golden age.

Most pilgrims walked or rode. Those from Britain, Ireland and northern Europe often took a ship instead, and landed on this coast. The official history says that for more than eight hundred years pilgrims came from northern Europe, mainly from Britain and Ireland, and that the sea route was especially popular from the thirteenth to the sixteenth centuries. A voyage took at least two to four weeks, wind and currents permitting. Ships sailed from at least thirty ports in the United Kingdom, with Bristol and Plymouth the busiest. A Coruña is named as the traditional landing port, and Ferrol, with its deep, sheltered harbour, is the start of the route today.

Some of the history is specific. The Xunta's route page records that in 1147 a troop of English, German and Flemish crusaders visited the tomb of St James. Wikipedia adds that during the Hundred Years' War, which ran from 1337 to 1453, English pilgrims and traders travelled to Galician ports with the permission of the English Crown. Among the travellers the official history page names is Margery Kempe, an English mystic who, according to Wikipedia, set off for Santiago in 1417. On the road itself, you will pass the remains of two medieval pilgrims' hospitals: one beside the clock tower in Neda, and the chapel of San Lourenzo at Hospital de Bruma, a village that still carries the memory of its hospital in its name. Wikipedia lists eight places where pilgrim hospitals had significant English support: Neda, Miño, Pontedeume, Betanzos, Bruma, Sigüeiro, San Paio and Paderne. In a real sense, the Camino Inglés is a string of old hospital towns.

## Silence, and a revival

The Renaissance and the Reformation reduced the numbers of pilgrims on every route. For this one, the official site says it fell into oblivion in the eighteenth century and revived with great vitality in the 1990s. It is now a real Camino again. In 2024 about 26,800 pilgrims finished the English Way, the fourth most walked route to Santiago, according to the Pilgrim's Office statistics as reported by Wikipedia. That is quiet company compared with the busiest roads, but you will not be alone.

The name is English because of who came, not because the path is English. If you are walking from Britain, the association names Finchale Priory in County Durham as the official starting point of the Camino Inglés in England. From there you will need a ship, a plane or a great deal of patience to reach Ferrol.
