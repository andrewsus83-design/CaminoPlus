# Arriving

## The cathedral

The Camino Inglés ends where every Way ends: at the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, built over the reputed tomb of St James. Wikipedia dates the start of construction to 1075 and the consecration to 1211. It is a Romanesque building with Gothic and Baroque additions. The Pórtico da Gloria, the carved Romanesque doorway inside, was completed by Master Mateo in 1188, and the great Baroque façade on the Praza do Obradoiro, the square in front of it, was finished in 1740. The old town around it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985.

The cathedral keeps a Holy Door, opened only in a Jacobean Holy Year, and the next Holy Year is 2027. Also kept there is the botafumeiro, the great censer, which Wikipedia says is swung on certain important religious days, and at all the Pilgrims' Masses in Holy Years.

Take a moment in the square before you do anything else. Sit on the stones if you wish. Many people find that the arrival is quieter or noisier than they expected.

## The Pilgrim's Office and the Compostela

The *Compostela* is the certificate of your pilgrimage, written in Latin, and the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago issues it. The Office says that it is for those who have come to the tomb for religious or spiritual reasons, on foot, by bicycle or on horseback, and who have travelled at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred by bicycle. The Latin text speaks of those who come *devotionis affectu, voti vel pietatis*, which the Office's own English translation renders as coming "with an attitude of devotion or because of a vow or promise". The Office issues it without charge.

What to bring is simple: your credencial, stamped along the way, with the date, the place and the seal in each box. You also take a ticket with a QR code, which lets you follow your place in the queue on your phone. The Office warns that in times of great influx it cannot guarantee that you will receive the Compostela on the same day. In August, or in a Holy Year, arrive with that in mind. The Office's own website gives its address and its current hours, which are not in this book because they change.

If you walked for other reasons, or are not sure that your reasons count, the Office also offers a separate *certificate of distance*. It records where you began, how many kilometres you walked, the dates and the route, whatever your starting point. It carries a fee, and the Office gives the amount.

The Office meets a great many people in a day. Be patient, be warm, and if you have a doubt about your credencial, ask before you queue.

## The pilgrims' Mass

The cathedral holds a Pilgrims' Mass. Check the cathedral's own website for the time, which changes. You do not need to be a believer to sit at the back and listen. It is a cathedral full of people who have walked.

## The next day

Try not to leave on the morning after you arrive. A day in Santiago lets the walk settle. Wash your clothes, sleep late, and look at the old town. You may like to see the Hostal dos Reis Católicos, which the Catholic Monarchs founded in the sixteenth century as the Royal Hospital for pilgrims, and which has been a hotel since 1954, according to the Pilgrim's Office.

If you still have legs and time, some pilgrims walk on to Finisterre and Muxía on the coast, ~90 km farther, a route you will find in the CaminoPlus app's list. If you do not, buy a postcard and write to someone you walked with in your head.
