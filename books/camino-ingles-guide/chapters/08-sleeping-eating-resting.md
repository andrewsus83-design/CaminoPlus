# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office lists access to "hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way" as the first practical use of the credencial, and notes that hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

On this Camino the official site says public hostels generally work on a first come, first served basis and generally for one night. Private hostels, hotels and guesthouses can be booked ahead. Dogs are allowed on the route, but public hostels generally do not let them sleep inside, so ask before you arrive with one. Details change from year to year, and a call or a message ahead saves a tired evening.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and do not rustle bags at night or before dawn. Pack your rucksack in the hall.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed. Shoes belong where the house puts them.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. Somebody will snore, and if it is you, apologise at breakfast.
- Keep your valuables close, as the official site advises for belongings generally.

**Bedbugs** are a risk in any shared bed anywhere. Check the seams of the mattress, keep your pack and clothes off the bed, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is not a reason to avoid hostels, only a reason to look before you lie down.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating. A good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

Many places along the Way offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what is on it and what it costs before you sit. A set meal is a good way to eat well on a long day, and it is an easy way to try what the region cooks.

The official site's gastronomy page is a short guide to what to look for as you go. It names the *tortilla de Betanzos*, the omelette of the town, and the cabbage of Betanzos. It names the bread of Neda, and the baked specialities of Pontedeume: proia, costrada, melindres and almendrados. In the country between Betanzos and Santiago it lists trout from Oroso, and the cheeses of Abegondo, Paderne, Mesía and Ordes, some of which carry the Arzúa-Ulloa denomination. It also names dishes that belong to the whole route: *polbo á feira*, the Galician octopus, empanadas, honey and the *tarta de Santiago*, the almond cake. Near the coast it points to fish and seafood. Galician wines and *queimada* complete the list.

You do not need to eat all of it. Pick one local thing a day, and eat it without a screen.

Water matters as much. Fill your bottle in each town, because some stretches pass only through woods and hamlets, and take food for the long days, the fourth and the fifth. The Fonte da Santiña, an hour before Sigüeiro, is a named water point on the fifth day.

## Resting

The English Way is short, and it is easy to treat it as a sprint. Resist that. A day of rest is part of the walk.

If you have a day to spare, spend it in Betanzos, which has one of the best preserved old quarters in Galicia, with two great medieval churches, an old bridge and a medieval gate. It comes at the end of the third day, and the fourth, the next, is the hardest of the walk. If you can only rest once on the way, rest there.

The other good place is the end. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it deserves more than a night. The chapter on arriving says why.

On the road, listen to your body before you listen to your plan. Take the short days as short days. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
