# Before you go

## The credencial

Your *credencial* is the pilgrim's passport. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago describes it as a card in a concertina fold, issued by a confraternity or an authorised association, by a parish church, or by a pilgrim hostel, and available both in Spain and abroad. It does two practical things: it opens the doors of the pilgrim hostels, and it is your proof when you ask for the Compostela. The Office is plain about it: the credencial "does not generate any rights", so it is a document of welcome, not a ticket.

If you can, get it before you leave home from a confraternity or association in your own country. If not, the official English Way site says that in Ferrol it is available at the co-cathedral of San Julián, known locally as San Xiao. Opening hours change with the season, so do not count on a visit on your first evening: check ahead.

You fill it with *sellos*, the stamps. The Office says they are normally collected where you sleep, but also in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. Ask for each one to show the date, the place and the seal of whoever stamps it. The official site recommends at least two stamps a day over the last hundred kilometres, which on this route means almost every day of the walk. If you walk only the minimum distance for the Compostela, the Office asks for a stamp at the start and the end of each stage, with the date.

The Pilgrim's Office also mentions a digital credential, developed as an app. If you plan to rely on one, ask the Office first. Until you have heard otherwise, carry the paper credencial in a plastic sleeve, and treat the stamps in any other app as a keepsake.

## Getting there and back

Most pilgrims fly. Santiago airport (SCQ) is ~12 km from the city, with a city bus into town, and it has flights from Spanish cities and from London, Dublin, Paris, Frankfurt and others. A Coruña has its own airport (LCG), and the official site lists that one first for Ferrol, with Santiago second.

Ferrol has a train station and a bus station, and so does A Coruña. From an airport you will need a bus or a train to reach Ferrol, and the connections are not always direct. Timetables change, so look them up a few days before you travel. Give yourself a night in Ferrol before you start: it is a good way to find your credencial, eat properly and check your pack.

Going home is easier, because you will be in a city with an airport, a train station and a bus station. Do not book a flight for the morning after you arrive. A day or two in Santiago is not a luxury, as the chapter on arriving explains.

## Training

Six days is gentle, and you do not need to be an athlete, but your feet and legs need to know what is coming. Two months is plenty.

- Walk most days, a few kilometres at first, with the shoes and socks you will wear.
- Add a longer walk each weekend, building towards ~20 km with your loaded pack. Try to do one of them in the rain.
- Include hills or stairs. The fourth day starts with a climb, and the third day's first kilometre out of Pontedeume is steep.
- Walk two days in a row at least once. The second day is the one that teaches you.

If you have a health condition, or have not been active for a long time, talk to your doctor before you begin.

## Packing

Carry as little as you can. Everything you pack, you will lift every morning.

- A rain jacket or poncho, and a cover for your pack. The official site says the thing to prepare for is rain.
- Quick-drying clothes in layers, rather than cotton, and a hat for sun and for rain.
- A light sleeping bag or a liner for the hostels, a small quick-drying towel, and sandals for the evenings.
- Earplugs and a head torch, which help in a dormitory and on a dark morning.
- A water bottle, a small first-aid kit with blister dressings, and any medicines you take.
- Your phone, charger and a plug adapter, and a copy of your passport or identity card.
- Your credencial, in a plastic sleeve.

Download your map before you leave home, because signal is not guaranteed on a forest track.

## Shoes and feet

Your feet are the whole journey. Wear shoes that are well broken in, and not new, with room for the toes to swell a little over the day. Choose socks that wick moisture, and carry a spare pair. At the first sign of a hot spot, stop and deal with it, dressing it before it becomes a blister. Take off your shoes and let your feet air at lunch.

A short first day is a gift. Use it to find out what your feet think of the day.

## Money in general terms

Spain uses the euro. Carry a bank card, and some cash in small notes for the small places that do not take cards. Withdraw cash in the larger towns, before a stretch of hamlets, and not in the last village before a long day. The book gives no prices because they change; ask what things cost before you commit, and use the app for stays.

## Language

Galicia has two official languages, Galician and Spanish. You will see both on signs, and the official site is available in both and in English. Spanish will get you through, and a few words of it go a long way: *buenos días*, *gracias*, *por favor*, *¿tiene una cama?* Galician names look unfamiliar at first: the article is *A* or *O* ("the"), as in A Sionlla and O Hospital, and a place can be spelt Xuvia or Xubia on different maps. They are the same village.

Most people you meet in a small place will be glad of a greeting. Say it first.
