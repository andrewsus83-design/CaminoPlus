# Over the hills: Betanzos to Santiago

## The character of these days

After Betanzos the Camino turns its back on the sea and goes inland for good. The next three days are woodland, hamlets, small rural churches and a long, gradual descent into the valley of the Tambre. There are fewer towns and more country, so the evenings need as much planning as the days. The two branches of the Way, the one from Ferrol and the one from A Coruña, become one, and the last two days are shared by everyone who walks to Santiago this way.

### Day 4: Betanzos to Hospital de Bruma (~24 km)

The official site calls this the most demanding stage, and the hardest for most pilgrims, and in the same breath adds that this does not mean it is very difficult. It rates it medium and estimates about eight hours. The climbing starts almost as soon as you leave Betanzos, and it is the one day on this Camino that asks you to be patient with yourself.

You leave Betanzos by the bridge of As Cascas, which the official site says survived the war against Napoleon's soldiers. Then you climb the first hill, and the day goes on through hills and forest. Three rural churches mark the way: Cos, with its church of San Esteban; the sanctuary of Nosa Señora da Saleta at Presedo, which vegetation hides, ~100 m from the path; and Leiro. Nowhere does the Camino rise above ~500 m, the official site says, but this is the day when you will feel that you are climbing.

At the chapel of As Travesas, where there are tables and benches, the branch from A Coruña joins the Way. This is the place to sit down. After it you have just over ~2 km to Hospital de Bruma, and for part of that you walk beside the main road. The official site advises caution there. Where you must walk on a road, face the traffic, keep well to the edge, and do not let tiredness make you careless.

Hospital de Bruma, in the municipality of Mesía, keeps the chapel of San Lourenzo, the remains of a medieval pilgrims' hospital. The village's name remembers what stood here.

You can divide this day. The official leaflet splits it at Presedo, into ~12 km and ~11.5 km. Start early, eat before the long climb, and carry food and water for the day, since the path passes through woodland and small hamlets, not through towns. On this day, the pace of the slowest hill is the right pace.

### Day 5: Hospital de Bruma to Sigüeiro (~24 km)

After the hills, a gift. The official site rates this day low and describes it as a gradual descent from Bruma to Sigüeiro, the opposite of the day before. It estimates about six hours, but it is long, and a long downhill is hard on knees as well as being kind to lungs. Shorten your stride, and use your poles, if you carry them, on the steep places.

In the first two hours the path passes three churches: Ardemil, San Paio de Buscás and Calle. The official site recommends stopping at San Paio, which has a Romanesque façade and a polychrome figure of the saint. Then the way runs mostly through woodland. The site singles out a trail that starts in the village of Os Carrás, which keeps much of its medieval look.

About an hour before Sigüeiro you come to the Fonte da Santiña, a water point. The official site links it to Father Sarmiento, who stopped there in the eighteenth century. It is a useful place to refill your bottle. Then a medieval path takes you into the Carboeiro park and down to the River Tambre and its historic bridge at Sigüeiro. The day crosses the municipalities of Mesía, Ordes and Oroso.

The official leaflet splits this stage at Poulo, into ~10 km and ~15 km. It is the day where a plan helps most, because you will want to know before you start where you will sleep. The chapter on other ways to split the days says what the official site lists, and the CaminoPlus app is the place for the live answer.

### Day 6: Sigüeiro to Santiago de Compostela (~16 km)

The last day is rated low and estimated at about four and a half hours. It begins well. The day opens with the crossing of the Tambre bridge, which the official site says has stood in the same place for almost a thousand years. Take the stairs on the left if you want a better view of it.

Then small tracks with little traffic run parallel to the right-hand side of the N-550, a main road. You will hear it more than you use it. Keep to the tracks. Pine woods lead to A Sionlla, where the ground turns to earth and the path goes round a prehistoric castro, the fortified settlement of a people who lived here long before any pilgrim. Then comes an oak wood called the Enchanted Forest, and the path drops towards farmland.

Shortly after the farmland, the route enters the centre of Santiago and heads for the cathedral.

Do not be surprised if you feel less than you expected, or more. There is no need to hurry the last streets. The cathedral has waited for pilgrims for centuries, and the chapter on arriving tells you what to do when you get there: where to go for the Compostela, what to expect, and how to give the next day a gentle shape.
