# Day 3. Paying what is owed

*What would you settle, or set down, before you go on?*

## The passage

> Ignatius, starting from his father's house, set out upon his journey on horseback. … His brother desired to accompany him as far as Ogna, and during the journey was persuaded by the Saint to pass one night of watching at the shrine of Our Blessed Lady at Aruncuz. Having prayed some time at the shrine for new strength for his journey, leaving his brother at Ogna at the house of their sister, to whom he paid a short visit, he journeyed on to Navarre. Remembering that an official in the Duke's palace owed him some money, he collected it by sending in a written account to the treasurer, and distributed it among persons to whom he felt indebted. A portion of the money he devoted to the restoration of a picture of the Blessed Virgin. Then dismissing his two remaining servants, he rode forth alone from Navarre in the direction of Montserrat, a mountain town of Catalonia in the northern part of Spain.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter II (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

The evening before a long walk is full of small errands. A key to leave with a neighbour. A message to the person who will worry. A bill to pay before it is forgotten, a plant to water, a door to check twice. None of it is the walk, and yet most people feel that they cannot really leave until it is done.

Ignatius does the same, on a larger scale. He starts from his father's house on horseback. His brother goes with him as far as a place the edition calls Ogna, and agrees, at Ignatius's urging, to spend a night of watching at a shrine on the way. There Ignatius prays *for new strength for his journey*. So the first stage of leaving is not a hurry. It is a night in a quiet place, in a brother's company, asking to be strong enough.

The edition is brisk about the partings. He leaves his brother at the house of their sister, pays her a short visit, and goes on. There is no scene. Some people leave like that, with a practical briskness that is a form of tenderness, and some with a briskness that is a way of not feeling it. The passage does not tell us which this was. You might notice, tomorrow or at the next farewell, which kind of leaver you are.

Then the accounts. He remembers that an official in the Duke's palace owes him money, and he gets it back by sending a written statement to the treasurer. He does not pretend to be above such things. Next he gives the money away, to the people to whom he felt indebted, and puts a part of it toward the restoration of a picture. The passage names no sums and no reasons beyond that one word, *indebted*. What it shows is a man leaving tidily, collecting what is his and paying what is owed, in both directions, so that nothing hangs behind him.

Last, he dismisses his two remaining servants and rides on *alone*. The passage does not say where the servants went. A servant dismissed is a life rearranged, and the edition passes over it. It is that kind of account: one man's story, from inside, with everyone else at the edge. If today's walking is in Navarre, you are in the region that this passage crosses, though the edition does not say which roads he took.

There are things here that no walker should copy. Ignatius leaves with almost no money. A pilgrim today needs enough for a bed and a meal, a charged phone and a way to call for help, and in Spain and France the number is 112. The passage is not a lesson in packing. Nor should we slide past the sentence we have left out. The edition says that at about this time he began *the discipline* every night, which was a bodily penance. We have not quoted it, and we ask nobody to imitate it.

The idea worth taking from the passage is leaving cleanly. You cannot settle everything. But some things can be settled in a minute, and they weigh more in the pack than they do in the doing: the message you have been meaning to send, the thanks you owe, the apology you have rehearsed and not said. There is also the other direction. Some of what you are owed is not money. It is rest, a meal, a hand. Ignatius sent in his statement for what was due to him as well as paying what he owed. It is not selfish to ask for your own.

And there is a doubt to be honest about. A tidy leaving does not promise an easy road. He left with his accounts in order, and the account goes on to a hard year at Manresa, which we reach on Day 5. A clean departure is a kindness to other people and to yourself. It is not a charm.

## For the walk

- What is one thing I owe, or am owed, that I could settle soon? Choose something small enough for the next hour.
- At your next stop, think of one person to whom you owe a thank-you, and say it in your head, slowly, in whole sentences, as if they were sitting beside you. Tonight, if it still seems right, you can send it. If it does not, you have still said it once. And if nobody comes to mind, thank whoever made or grew your last meal, in the same way.

## At the end of the day

Tonight's errands are small: boots by the door, damp things hung up, water filled for the morning. There is another errand you can run in the five minutes before sleep, and it is to look at the day as a ledger.

What did the day take from you? Some of it was effort, and some was patience, and perhaps some was money. Now what did it give? A meal, a view, a bed, a word from someone on the path. A day is a long run of small exchanges, and most of them are made without thinking. They are worth a moment's attention, if only because we usually remember what we paid, and forget what we were given.

Was there something you were given today that has not been thanked? Not a gift, perhaps, but a courtesy: a door held, an answer to a question, a bit of shade, a shared seat. A thank-you can still be said tomorrow, to the person if you meet them, and otherwise to the air.

Do not forget the one creditor who is always in the room, which is the body. It is owed water, a meal, a stretch, an early night. It sends no reminders. It sends pain instead. And if someone at home is wondering how your day went, a short message tonight is another way of paying what is owed. Three lines will do, and they need not be eloquent.

Perhaps something was owed to you today that did not arrive: a courtesy, a word of thanks, a bed you had counted on. You may feel it as a small grievance. The passage shows a man who asked for what was his, plainly, and then let the matter go. You can do the first part tonight in your head, and leave the second part to the night.

The passage also has a night of watching at a shrine, and a prayer for strength. Notice how small the request is. It is for strength for the journey, and not for the whole of life. Tonight, ask for enough for tomorrow and no more, even if you ask nobody in particular. It is a good deal easier to be strong for one day.

Tiredness has its own arithmetic, and it is a poor accountant. Things look larger at the end of a day, and old debts look heavier at night. Do not try to balance everything now. A rested person settles in ten minutes what a tired one cannot settle in an hour.

- What was I given today that I have not yet thanked anyone for?
- Is there something small that I owe, and could settle tomorrow?
- What do I need, and no more than that, to get through tomorrow?

## A short prayer

*For what I was given today, thank you. For what I owe, give me the courage to begin paying it, and the sense to begin small. Let me ask for enough strength for tomorrow and no more. Let the people I left behind be well, and let the night be kind.*
