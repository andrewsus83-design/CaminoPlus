# Day 7. On foot, past Pamplona

*What is the next step, and not the whole road?*

## The passage

> Although at first he enjoyed good health, he afterward fell seriously ill. For this reason, after his recovery, he determined to depart in order to accomplish the business which he had undertaken for his companions. He resolved to set out on foot and without money. His brother was grieved at this, and looked on it as a disgrace to himself. Ignatius concluded to yield this point, and at last, toward evening, he consented to be carried to the boundary of the province in company with his brother and relatives. But as soon as he had left the province, he dismounted and without receiving any sustenance for the journey he set out for Pampeluna and thence to Almazonus, the birthplace of Father Laynez. Then he traveled on to Siguensa and to Toledo, and afterward from Toledo to Valencia. In all these cities, the birthplaces of his companions, he would receive nothing from their parents and relations, although they offered him a great many things, and begged him to accept them.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter VIII (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

The end of a walk is seldom the place where the road stops. You reach the town you were walking toward, and there is a square, a church, a café, a street that carries on out of the other side. The legs have not yet been told. The Baztanes ends in Pamplona, and the app's route notes say that it joins the Camino Francés there, so for anyone who wants it, the road does go on.

In this passage Ignatius leaves home a second time, and not for himself. After an illness and a recovery he decides to go and do business that he has undertaken for his companions, the friends he made as a student in Paris, in pages of the account we have not used. He means to go on foot and without money. His brother is grieved, and takes it for a disgrace to himself. Ignatius, the edition says, yields on that one point. He lets himself be taken to the boundary of the province, toward evening, with his brother and his relatives. And as soon as he has crossed it, he gets down, and goes on without any food for the journey.

So the stubborn man is also, here, a man who bends: a little, for a brother's sake, and not further. It is a small compromise, one evening's ride. The edition does not tell us what either of them felt at the boundary. The brother's grief stays in the account, and the account does not claim that it was set right. Some departures cost someone something, and a good purpose does not undo the cost.

Pamplona is only a name in the passage. He *set out for Pampeluna*, and from there to a town the edition spells Almazonus, then to Siguensa and Toledo and Valencia. Nothing is said about what it was like for a man to walk toward the place where a cannon ball had broken his leg. The passage says only that he went on. If you arrive there at the end of the Baztanes, you may find that the place means something to you, or nothing, and both are allowed. What the passage gives you is an example of a way on that does not wait for a feeling.

In every one of those cities, the edition adds, he would receive nothing from the parents and relatives of his companions, though they offered him a great deal and begged him to accept it. On Day 6 we met the same man learning to ask for bread. Here he refuses what kind people press on him. The two do not obviously fit. It may have been strength, or stubbornness, or both, and it may have cost some generous people the pleasure of giving. If someone begs you to accept something, you are allowed to say yes.

Nor does the account make the road dignified. A little further on, it has him crawling on hands and feet along a narrow path above a river, in fear, for a long time. The account calls it *the greatest of all the bodily labors that he ever experienced*. Then, entering Bologna, he falls from a little bridge into mud, to the amusement of a crowd, and begs through the whole city without receiving *so much as a farthing*. He rests there, because he is ill, and then goes on toward Venice. That is what the way on looks like in the book: fear, mud, laughter, nothing in the hand, a rest, and then the next town.

For today, the lesson is not to wait. You do not need to know what the whole road means before you walk the next stretch. You need only the stretch, and some patience with a body that has had enough. If your road ends today, there is still the walk to the station, or home, and that is a way on too.

## For the walk

- What is the next step, and not the whole road? You need only answer for the next hour.
- Walk the last stretch of today, the last few hundred paces or the last kilometre, at half your usual speed. Name silently three things you pass as you go: a gate, a wall, a piece of sky. When you stop, stay on your feet for one breath more, and thank your legs, in whatever words you like. A way on need not be far. It can be the next bend.

## At the end of the day

If you have walked as you read, tonight's tiredness has a week in it. It is different from the tiredness of a single day. It sits deeper, and it can make the ordinary things, a stairway, a fork, a menu, feel enormous. Take them slowly.

If you have come to the end of the Baztanes today, you might sit for a while somewhere in the city before you do anything else. A bench, a step, a quiet church if one is open. You need not feel anything in particular. Arrival often turns up late, after the shower, in the middle of an ordinary supper.

Look back at the day through the passage. Where did you go on without waiting to feel ready? And where did you bend, a little, for someone's sake, and was that the right place to bend? A point worth yielding is often small, and a point worth holding is often the one that is quietly yours. It is not always easy to tell them apart in the moment, and a day's end is a kind way to look.

Did anything happen today that made you look foolish? A wrong turning, a stumble, a coffee on your sleeve. Perhaps people laughed, or only noticed. The account tells of Ignatius in the mud at Bologna plainly, and makes no lesson of it. Some of our foolish moments are better left plain. With a little time, they may become the parts of the story we like best.

If today was hard, the account has some company for you. The way on is allowed to be ugly. It does not have to be graceful, or well timed, or admired by anyone who sees it.

The road also runs through other people's villages, fields and kitchens. Before you go on, you might thank, in your head or in person, whoever gave you water, a bed or a direction. A village is a home, and you have been a guest in it.

If someone is waiting to hear how it went, give them one true sentence tonight, and keep the long story for another day. The long story takes weeks to find its shape, and a tired person tells it badly. The legs have earned the evening, and so have you.

Before you sleep, decide only the first step of tomorrow, the one that gets you out of the door. Not the whole plan. If your walk is not over, nothing has to be settled tonight. And if one of these seven questions helped you, you could keep it, one a night, long after the days are done.

- Where today did I go on without waiting to feel ready?
- What small point did I yield today, and was that the right one?
- What do I want to carry with me from these seven days?

## A short prayer

*For the road behind me, thank you, and for the road that goes on. Let me walk the next stretch without waiting to feel ready. Let me bend where love asks it, and hold where I must. Let me be able to laugh at the mud. Rest my feet, and keep the people I love.*
