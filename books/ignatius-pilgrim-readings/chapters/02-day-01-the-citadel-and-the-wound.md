# Day 1. The citadel and the wound

*What are you holding on to at the start of this walk, and what would it take to put it down?*

## The passage

> Up to his twenty-sixth year the heart of Ignatius was enthralled by the vanities of the world. His special delight was in the military life, and he seemed led by a strong and empty desire of gaining for himself a great name. The citadel of Pampeluna was held in siege by the French. All the other soldiers were unanimous in wishing to surrender on condition of freedom to leave, since it was impossible to hold out any longer; but Ignatius so persuaded the commander, that, against the views of all the other nobles, he decided to hold the citadel against the enemy. … Ignatius stood fighting bravely until a cannon ball of the enemy broke one of his legs and seriously injured the other. When he fell, the citadel was surrendered. When the French took possession of the town, they showed great admiration for Ignatius.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter I (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

The first morning of a long walk is the most confident one. The pack is where you put it. Nothing has rubbed, nothing aches, and the whole road is still an idea, which is the lightest thing you will carry.

The young man in this passage was confident in just that way, and the account does not flatter him. His heart, it says, was held by the vanities of the world. He loved soldiering, and what he wanted was *a strong and empty desire of gaining for himself a great name*. Those are the words of an old man dictating near the end of his life, and an old man may judge his younger self more harshly than the younger self would have done. But he keeps the word *empty*.

Then the siege. The French have surrounded the citadel at Pamplona, and the other soldiers want to give it up on condition that they may leave freely, because nobody can hold out any longer. Ignatius persuades the commander to hold it. The passage gives him no plan, only the power to persuade. It does not say what that choice cost the men beside him, and we will not guess. It is the story as one man tells it, and he is the one who won the argument.

Then a cannon ball. It breaks one leg and badly hurts the other, and the citadel is surrendered as soon as he falls. A little further on, the edition says that he received *the best care from the physicians of the French army* before he was *borne on a litter to Loyola*. The man who would not yield was nursed by the people he had refused to yield to.

His recovery was slow. The bones had knit badly, so the doctors decided to break the leg again and set it properly. He let them cut him again, and gave no sign of suffering except *tightly clenching his fists*. Later, when a bone stood out below the knee and one leg was shorter than the other, he could not bear the look of it, because he meant to live at court. He asked for the bone to be cut away, though the doctors warned him that it would hurt more than everything before. He chose it. The wish for a great name had not gone anywhere. It was lying in bed with him.

Two things in all this are hard. The first is the verdict. Not every wish to be good at something, or to be known for it, is empty, and the passage should not be read as a ban on ambition. It is one man's judgement on one wish, made in hindsight. The second is the war. The account admires courage in a siege and says nothing of the dead. We read the passage for what comes after the siege, not for the siege.

The everyday version is quieter. You will not need a cannon ball. A blister, a knee, a night without sleep and a forecast of rain can do the same work. The plan for today was written by a more confident person than the one now walking, and it is easy to cling to it against all the evidence, out of the same stubbornness that held a citadel. Pace, distance, being first up, a number on a watch: everyone has their own great name to make. And nobody should do what he did with the bone. No look, and no plan, is worth choosing pain for.

There is also doubt to be honest about. The old story is tidy: the wound leads to the books, and the books to the road. Real interruptions do not always lead anywhere, and a bad knee is sometimes only a bad knee. All the passage can fairly offer is that a stop is not nothing, and that what you gripped hardest at the start may be the first thing you are glad to put down.

## For the walk

- What am I trying to prove on this road, and to whom? You need not answer it today. It is enough to notice that it is there, walking beside you.
- At your first stop, before you look at how far is left, ask your feet, your shoulders and your hips how they are, and answer honestly. Then change one thing: loosen a strap, smooth a sock, ease the pace a little. It takes two minutes, and the rest of the day will thank you. If everything is fine, say so and walk on. The practice was the asking.

## At the end of the day

Take your boots off first, and look at your feet before you look at anything else. They have been keeping the day's true account, and they do not exaggerate.

Go back over the hours between this morning and now. There was probably a moment when the plan said one thing and the body said another. Which of them won? Sometimes the plan should win, and the hill is simply there to be climbed. Sometimes the body is right and the plan was only pride in new boots. It is hard to tell in the moment, but the day is mostly over, so you can ask the question tonight without having to act on it.

There may also have been a moment when you wanted to be seen: to arrive before someone else, to be taken for fit, or experienced, or good company, or quiet and wise. The account is blunt about its young soldier, and also gentle. It does not scold him. It only says, in one word, that what he reached for was empty. You need not be harder on yourself than that. The wish to be admired is among the oldest we have, and it walks every road.

Then look for what came the other way. In the story, the French physicians were kind to a man who had held out against them. Was anyone kind to you today for no reason you had earned? Someone who held a gate, pointed out a turning, or made room on a bench? That kindness is as much a part of the day as your pace.

Name one thing that was good, too, and let it be small. The hour when the legs warmed up and the pack stopped being something you carried and became something you wore. A hot drink. The first stretch when you stopped checking and simply walked. A first day is mostly effort, and it is easy to go to bed with only the effort in mind. If other walkers were about, you may have measured yourself against them without meaning to. Let that go with the boots.

The passage leaves one question open, and it can be asked quietly tonight. What would have happened if the soldiers had been allowed to surrender? You might ask the same of a stubbornness of your own: a quarrel, a plan, an old refusal. You need not give it up. Only look, once, at what it has cost, and who paid.

A first day tires you twice over, in the legs and in the nerves, and it is often the nerves that keep you awake. If sleep does not come, do nothing clever with the questions below. Let them lie there like a second pillow, and leave them until morning.

- Where today did I push on when my body asked me to stop?
- What did I want people to think of me on the road?
- Who was kind to me today without being asked?

## A short prayer

*Let me set down what is only pride, and keep what is only courage. For the kindness I did not earn, thank you. For the feet that carried me, thank you. Let me sleep tonight, and begin again tomorrow a little lighter.*
