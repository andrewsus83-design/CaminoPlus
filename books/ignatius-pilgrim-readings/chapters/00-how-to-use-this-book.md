# How to use this book

This is a small book of seven days, made to go with the Camino Baztanes. In the CaminoPlus catalogue that road runs from Bayonne to Pamplona, through France and into Spain, and the app's route notes describe it as hilly, with forest paths, and rain common. The catalogue gives no length for it, and neither does this book. Seven days is the length of the reading, not a plan of stages. If your road is longer, read a day twice. If it is shorter, keep a day for the evening after.

## Why this book for this road

The book is the autobiography of Ignatius of Loyola, a soldier who became a pilgrim, and it starts where the Baztanes ends. The account tells of a young man defending the citadel of Pamplona against the French, struck by a cannon ball that broke one of his legs and badly hurt the other. Historians put the siege in 1521. The edition we use gives no year for it. Everything in the book comes out of that wound: a long time lying still, a change of mind, and then a road. The account calls him the pilgrim.

So you may finish the walk in the city where the story begins. The app's route notes say the Baztanes joins the Camino Francés at Pamplona. The edition names Bayonne once, in its last chapter, but it does not say what road Ignatius took, and this book does not claim that he walked this one.

## How a day works

Each day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from the autobiography, in the exact words of a 1900 edition, spelling and all. It writes *Pampeluna* for Pamplona, and we leave it as it is. Read the passage twice, the second time aloud if you can.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Ignatius's. It starts from something on the road and lets the passage open it. It says what the passage says, where it is hard to accept, and what it might mean for an ordinary day.
- **For the walk.** A question and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. Both are better done on your feet than on paper.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have lived, with two or three questions to lie down with. You need not write anything.
- **A short prayer.** Original and brief. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish.

## A Catholic book, and a strange one in places

The autobiography is a Catholic book, and this is a Catholic edition of 1900. It tells of visions, long fasts and scourging, written by a man who believed in all of it, and some of it is hard to read. We chose passages that a walker can follow, and where one is strange, the reflection says so. Nothing here asks you to copy the penances, and nobody should hurt themselves to read this book well. If you carry a pain you cannot shake, talk to someone you trust or to a doctor. In Spain and France, 112 is the emergency number.

People walk for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Ignatius believed to recognise a man who was hurt, bored, vain, afraid, and starting again.

## The shape of the seven days

The days keep to the order of the account, and they also follow a walk.

- **Day 1**, the soldier and his wound.
- **Day 2**, the long lying-still, and two kinds of thought.
- **Day 3**, setting out.
- **Day 4**, the vigil, and the road from Montserrat.
- **Day 5**, Manresa, and a river.
- **Day 6**, begging and trusting.
- **Day 7**, the way on.

These are a shape, not a timetable. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, or in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off. At home it works the same way: the morning with your tea, the evening before bed.

Two words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well.
