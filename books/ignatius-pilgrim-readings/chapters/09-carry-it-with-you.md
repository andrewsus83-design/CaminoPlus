# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the seven days. Perhaps you read them on seven days of walking, or over a quiet week at home, or all at once on a train. Any of those is fine. The book has no certificate to give you. It was made to keep a little company on a road, and a road has no use for a book once it is over, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep. Not everything. Three small things will do, one for the head, one for the hands and one for the feet.

- **For the head: a test.** After a thought, a conversation or an hour of music, ask what it leaves behind. Lighter, or drier? It is the plainest thing in the book, and Ignatius found it by lying in a bed with nothing else to do. It is as useful on a train as on a path.
- **For the hands: one small settling.** Pay what you owe, or ask for what you need. A thank-you sent, a bill closed, a glass of water requested. A day with one of these in it is a tidier day.
- **For the feet: a stop, and a next step.** Ten minutes beside something that moves, with nothing to produce. And, when the road is too long to think about, only the next stretch.

If you keep all three for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from a book.

## How to go on

Come back whenever you like. The days are short, and a day that said little the first time may say more the fifth. The evenings can be read alone.

You might also read Ignatius himself. The account is in the public domain, in the edition we used, at Project Gutenberg, ebook number 24534. It runs to about eighteen thousand words, a few evenings' reading, and it tells much that we left out: the voyage to Jerusalem, the prisons, the years in Paris, the first friends, the road to Rome. Some of it is strange, and some of it will not suit you. That is allowed. A book of this kind is not a test, and some passages will have asked for more than you could give, or less than you hoped for. Leave those, and take what helped.

## A last word

The account stops where it stops, in Rome, with work still going on, and with a short, plain line that hands the rest to someone else: *Master Natalis can tell the rest*. It is an odd way to close the story of a life, and a good one. The pilgrim does not announce an arrival. Others will carry on the telling.

You may or may not have reached Pamplona. Either way, the road goes on from where you are. The man who once wanted a great name tells his story, in these pages, with a great deal of being helped in it: by French physicians, by a confessor with practical advice, by a lady of rank, by a brother who rode with him to the boundary. It may be that the helped part deserves the last word.

Walk again. Next year, if you can. Or tomorrow, round the block, with your phone left behind and your hands open.

*Buen Camino.*
