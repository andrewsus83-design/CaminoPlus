# About the book and its author

## The man

Ignatius of Loyola was born in 1491 at the château of Loyola, in the north of Spain, and died on 31 July 1556. Those two dates are the ones the appendix of our edition gives, and we keep to them. He was a soldier and then a student, and in the end the founder of the Society of Jesus, whose members are called Jesuits. Catholics honour him as a saint, and the title of the edition calls him St. Ignatius. He also wrote a book called the *Spiritual Exercises*. This book does not use it and does not explain it.

## The book

The account of his life was not written by Ignatius's own hand. In the preface of the scribe, Father Louis Gonzalez, we read that friends in the Society had often asked Ignatius for the story of his life, and that he had put them off. In 1553 he agreed, and began to tell it that September. He was in very feeble health by then, and it went on in pieces, between other business, until 1555. The scribe says that his custom was to dictate while walking. He also says that he wrote down nothing he had not heard from Ignatius, and kept so close to his words that afterwards he could not always explain them.

Ignatius spoke of himself in the third person, and this edition writes *Ignatius* most of the time and *the pilgrim* now and then. The pages are not always a pure voice, either. Someone else can be heard in them, the scribe or the editor, as in the phrase "we dare not state it". That is why this book says "the account" or "the edition" where a plain "Ignatius said" would claim too much.

The edition's numbers do not always fit together. The account puts the end of his worldly years in his twenty-sixth year, and the appendix gives his age at Montserrat as thirty. We do not try to settle that.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

*Pilgrim* is the account's own word for him. It is a story of walking: of begging, sleeping outdoors, storms, fevers and being laughed at. Ignatius is not shown as a hero who knows the way. He is often afraid, ill, wrong or alone, and the account says so. It is also the story of a mind changing slowly, in a bed, in a church, beside a river, and a long walk gives a person that kind of time.

## What is hard, and what we left out

The account has visions and penances that nobody should copy. It also has an early episode on the road, in which Ignatius nearly follows a stranger to do him harm after a quarrel about belief. The edition speaks of that stranger, and of others, in the language of 1900, which is sometimes unkind. We left those places out.

Beyond that, we took seven short passages from four of the account's eight chapters, and skipped the middle: the voyage to Jerusalem, the prisons of Alcalá and Salamanca, and the years in Paris. The account goes on to Venice and Rome, and our last day stops at the beginning of that road.

## The edition and the licence

The text is *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., printed in New York, Cincinnati and Chicago by Benziger Brothers in 1900. The title page carries church approvals dated December 1899 and a copyright notice of 1900 in the name of the printers. The edition is old enough to be in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 24534, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

The edition names an editor, but no translator. It does not say who put the account into English, or from which language. The only translation it mentions is a Latin one, in a heading above the scribe's preface. So the passages here are credited to the edition and its editor, as the edition gives them, and no more.

We changed nothing in the passages. We kept the edition's spellings, such as *Pampeluna*, and its quotation marks. Where a passage skips a stretch, a mark like this shows it: …. We quote only the account itself, never the editor's preface, the scribe's preface or the appendix. The choice and order of the passages, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours.
