# Day 6. Bread, and the asking for it

*What do you need today, and who could you ask?*

## The passage

> Many offered to accompany him, but he refused, as he wished to go alone. He expected to derive great advantage from placing his whole trust in God alone. … If he took a companion, when hungry he would look to his companion for food; if exhausted, he would call on his companion for help; and so he would confide in his companion, and have some affection for him: whereas he wished to place all this confidence, hope, and affection in God alone. … When he thought of procuring bread, he was much troubled with scruples. "Is this your hope and faith in God, who, you were sure, would not fail you?" The force and violence of the temptation were such that he was greatly distressed. Good reasons on both sides presented themselves. Finally, in his perplexity, he determined to leave the matter to his confessor. He told him first of his great desire to go to Jerusalem, and to do everything for the greater glory of God. Then he gave the reasons for not taking provisions for the voyage. His confessor decided that he ought to beg what was necessary and take it with him. He went to a lady of rank to ask for what he needed.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter III (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

At some point every day on the road the question of food arrives. A shop is shut when you reach it, or the café you counted on has no kitchen, and you open the bag to find half a loaf. The app's route notes call the Baztanes a small route and advise you to plan your stops, and food is a good thing to plan. This passage is about a man and his bread.

Ignatius is setting out for Barcelona, to take ship for Jerusalem, and many people offer to go with him. He refuses them all. The reason he gives is close to bookkeeping. If he took a companion, then when he was hungry he would look to him for food, when he was exhausted he would call on him for help, and he would come to rely on him and grow fond of him. He wanted to put all that trust, hope and affection in God alone. The edition adds, so that we do not take it for a figure of speech, that his words *were not a mere expression of the lips*.

Then a practical hitch. The captain of a ship will take him without a fare, on one condition: that he brings enough sailors' bread to live on. Ignatius would have refused even that, and the thought of bread plunges him into doubt. A voice in his head says, *Is this your hope and faith in God*, and goes on to remind him that he had been sure God would not fail him. Good reasons seem to stand on both sides, and he is, the edition says, greatly distressed. So he does something that the man of the first paragraph would not have predicted. He asks someone else. He puts the matter to his confessor, who decides that he should beg what he needs and take it with him. And he goes to a lady of rank to ask.

The man who would not lean on a companion is told, within a page, to go and ask for bread. His first idea of trust was to need nothing from anyone. The second, which his confessor gave him, was closer to the truth: to ask, to take what is given, and to go on. It is worth noticing who changed his mind. It was not a vision or a theory. It was one person, who listened, and said something practical.

And the edition shows him still working out where the line falls. Before he boards, he leaves five or six coins, which had been given to him as alms, on a bench on the wharf. Bread, yes. Coins, no. Nobody could call it a system, and he does not claim that it is.

There are three honest cautions. The first is that refusing a companion was one man's rule for one stage of his life, and the account does not hold it up as a law. Later in the same book Ignatius has many companions, among them the nine friends who meet at Venice. On the Camino most people walk beside others, and many are glad of it. The second is that trust is not the same as having nothing. A walker who carries water and plans a lunch is not failing at faith. His confessor said as much, in effect, when he told him to take bread. The third is that the voice that asked *Is this your hope and faith in God* belongs to what the account calls scruples, an anxious, accusing voice of the kind that tormented him at Manresa. If you hear a voice like it, you are allowed to put the question to someone else, as he did.

Nothing heroic is needed. Name one thing you need today, and ask someone for it. Water at a café counter, a pointer at a fork in the path, a pinch of salt, the time of the last bus. Say thank you, and take it. Asking is one of the harder small things on a long walk, and it often costs less than we feared.

## For the walk

- What do I need today, and who could I ask?
- Ask one person for one small thing: a refill of water, directions you half know already, the time of the next shop. Look at them as you ask, and say thank you in full when you are given it. Notice how asking feels in the body just before the words, and how it feels just after. If asking comes easily to you, practise the other half instead: say yes to the next thing that is offered, even if you would have managed without it.

## At the end of the day

Whoever is cooking tonight, or passing the bread, or pouring water into someone else's glass, is doing what the passage is about without giving it a thought. Meals are where help changes hands without being called help.

Look back over the day for the places where you asked, and the places where you did not. There may have been a moment when you wanted something, directions, a plaster, a seat, and decided it was easier to go without than to ask. Easier for whom? Sometimes the reason is independence, which is a real good. Sometimes it is only a dislike of owing. The two feel alike from the inside. The account shows a man who refused every companion on principle, and who was turned round by one short conversation. He was not weaker afterwards.

Was there also a moment when someone offered, and you waved it away? A hand with a strap, a share of food, a lift for the last stretch. Perhaps you were right to. Perhaps you will wish tomorrow that you had said yes.

And a question the other way round: whom did you trust today? A stranger's directions, a line on a map, the forecast, a word that the next village has a shop. Most of a day's walking runs on a trust so ordinary that we do not call it that. It was mostly honoured, or you would not be sitting here.

If you walked alone today, the passage may sound different tonight from the way it sounded this morning. Being alone can be a great gift and it can be heavy, and often it is both within the same hour. You do not have to decide which it was. But you might tell someone one thing about your day, at the table or in a message. It is a small way of not being entirely on your own, and it costs only a sentence.

If asking comes easily to you, turn the question round. Did you let anyone help you today, or did you do the helping and keep your hands clear of owing? Giving can be a way of staying in charge, and it is worth knowing if that was your way today.

Before you sleep, think for a moment about tomorrow's bread. A roll saved from supper, a piece of fruit, the stops you mean to make. It is a small act of trust in tomorrow and in your own good sense, and it is not a failure of faith to look ahead.

Asking is harder when you are tired, and so is accepting. If you could do neither today, forgive it, and try again after breakfast, when most things are easier to ask for.

- Where did I ask for help today, and where did I decide not to?
- Did I turn down something that was offered, and why?
- Whom did I trust today without noticing?

## A short prayer

*For every hand that gave me something today, thank you. Let me ask for what I need without shame, and take what is given without hurry. Let me be easy to help and glad to help. Keep those who walk alone tonight, and keep those who walk beside me.*
