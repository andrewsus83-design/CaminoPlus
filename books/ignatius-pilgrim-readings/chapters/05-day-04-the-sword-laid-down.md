# Day 4. The sword laid down

*What would you put down for a day, and who else would feel it if you did?*

## The passage

> He begged and obtained leave of his confessor to give up his horse, and to hang up his sword and his dagger in the church, near the altar of the Blessed Virgin. … The eve of the Annunciation of Our Blessed Lady in the year 1522 was the time he chose to carry out the project he had formed. At nightfall, unobserved by any one, he approached a beggar, and taking off his own costly garments gave them to the beggar. He then put on the pilgrim's dress he had previously bought, and hastened to the church, where he threw himself on his knees before the altar of the Blessed Mother of God, and there, now kneeling, now standing, with staff in hand, he passed the entire night. … At about a league's distance from Montserrat, he was overtaken by a man who had ridden after him at a rapid pace. This man accosted him and inquired if he had given certain garments to a poor man, as the latter had declared. Ignatius answered that it was true that he had given them to a beggar. On learning that the latter had been ill-treated because he was suspected of having stolen the clothes, the eyes of Ignatius filled with tears, in pity for the poor man.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter II (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

In the first evenings of a long walk you empty your pockets onto the bed and find that you have been carrying a different life: a work pass, a loyalty card, a key to a door you will not see for weeks. Nobody needs any of it on the road. Putting it down is quiet and a little strange, like taking off a coat you had stopped noticing.

Ignatius puts down more. In this passage he gets leave from his confessor to give up his horse and to hang up his sword and his dagger in a church, near an altar. A soldier's two tools, hung up and left. He has already bought what he needs for the road, a pilgrim's staff and a gourd to drink from, and on the night he has chosen he gives his own costly clothes to a beggar and puts on the pilgrim's dress. Then he spends the night in the church, *now kneeling, now standing*, with the staff in his hand.

The lines just before the passage show where the idea came from. He had read stories of knights who kept watch before an altar all night on the eve of being knighted, and he was, the edition says, *filled with these chivalric fancies*. So he meant to keep his vigil in the same manner, neither sitting nor lying down, and to lay aside his worldly dignities in order to take up *the arms of Christ*. There is something tender in that. The man is leaving the world of soldiers, and he does it in the costume and the manner of a soldier's story. He cannot yet imagine a new life except in the clothes of the old one. Most of us begin that way. We set out on a pilgrimage as the sort of person we already are, with the same hopes in different boots, and the road sorts the rest out slowly.

Then comes the dawn, and a small catastrophe. A man rides after him, about a league from Montserrat, and asks whether he gave certain clothes to a poor man, because the poor man has said so. Ignatius says that he did. The beggar, it turns out, has been badly treated on suspicion of having stolen the clothes, and the eyes of Ignatius fill with tears, *in pity for the poor man*. The passage stops there. It does not tell us whether the beggar was freed, or what Ignatius did next.

It is a small, true thing about giving. A gift made in secret can land on someone with less power than the giver to explain it. The gesture was generous, and the cost fell on the person it was meant for. Nothing in the account says that Ignatius was wrong to give. It says only that the generosity was not the end of the matter, and that he wept.

Three things here are not for imitation. A night on your feet before a day on your feet is not advice. Sleep. The page before this one describes a rough sackcloth robe and a cord tied below one knee, for penance, and we have left that page out. And giving away the clothes you need to a stranger is not a practice for the road, either.

The simpler question the passage leaves is what you are ready to put down. Not a sword. Perhaps a role, a title, the habit of being the competent one, the need to look as though you know where you are going. You can hang it on a hook for a day. It will probably still be there at the end, and you will know it better for having been without it. And, as the passage shows without quite saying so, you can watch who else is touched by a putting-down. Leaving a habit behind can land on a travelling companion, on someone at home, or on the person who always relied on you to decide.

## For the walk

- What would I put down for a day, and who would feel it if I did? Let the answer be small and true: a habit, not a possession.
- At the next gate, stile or view, stand still for one full minute, with your pack on and your phone in your pocket. Do not sit. That minute is the whole vigil. When it is over, walk on at the pace you came with, and see whether anything in you has moved. If a minute feels very long, that is worth knowing too.

## At the end of the day

Tonight, unlike the man in the passage, you should lie down. Vigils belong to people who have slept enough, and the whole of tonight's work is a bed.

Before you do, think about what you set down today, even for an hour. It need not have been grand. You may have put the phone away. You may have let somebody else choose the place to eat, or walked behind instead of in front, or said nothing when you had a correction ready. Small things of that size are the true material of the day. If you set nothing down, notice that too, with interest and without blame.

Then think about the other half of the passage. Did anything you did today, meant kindly, land differently on someone else? A suggestion that sounded like a criticism. A favour that put someone in your debt. A cheerful word at a bad moment. We cannot always know, and the account does not claim that Ignatius could have known. But it is worth the minute to look, because good intentions are poor at noticing their own effects. If you find something, the answer is rarely a speech. A short, plain word tomorrow, or a changed habit, is usually enough.

The tears are worth a thought as well. The passage ends on pity, which is neither a plan nor a principle. Was there someone today for whom you felt that plain, unprofessional pity: a stranger limping, a walker who looked lost, an animal by the road? What did you do with the feeling? There is no right answer. Sometimes the most we can do is to feel it, and let it make us a little gentler with the next person.

And think about the costume. What old story about yourself did you walk in today: the capable one, the quiet one, the funny one, the one who never needs anything? We all have a favourite suit, and the first days of a walk are when it fits least. Perhaps there was a moment today when it pinched. You do not have to take it off yet.

If you did hang something up today, look in the morning to see whether you want it back. Often you will find that you do not, and sometimes you will. Either is information.

Be as tired as you are tonight. Nothing about the road requires you to lie awake trying to improve. The day has already done what it can.

- What did I put down today, even for an hour?
- Did anything I did today, meant kindly, land differently on someone else?
- What old story about myself did I walk in today?

## A short prayer

*Let me put down what I can, and see who is standing near when I do. Where my kindness has landed badly, let me notice it and not look away. Tonight let me lie down like someone with nothing left to guard. Let morning find me rested.*
