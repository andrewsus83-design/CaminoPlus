# Day 5. Beside the river

*What do you see when you stop, and what do you owe the body that carried you here?*

## The passage

> One day he went to the Church of St. Paul, situated about a mile from Manresa. Near the road is a stream, on the bank of which he sat, and gazed at the deep waters flowing by. While seated there, the eyes of his soul were opened. He did not have any special vision, but his mind was enlightened on many subjects, spiritual and intellectual. So clear was this knowledge that from that day everything appeared to him in a new light. Such was the abundance of this light in his mind that all the divine helps received, and all the knowledge acquired up to his sixty-second year, were not equal to it. From that day he seemed to be quite another man, and possessed of a new intellect.
>
> — Ignatius of Loyola, *The Autobiography of St. Ignatius*, Chapter III (edited by J. F. X. O'Conor, S.J., 1900)

## Reflection

Somewhere on most roads the path runs beside water, and you stop without having decided to. The app's route notes for the Baztanes mention rivers and beech forests, and a stream, a bridge or a low wall will do. You sit. You are not doing anything. For a few minutes the road's usual arithmetic of distance, time and what comes next goes quiet.

That is more or less what the passage describes. Ignatius goes to a church about a mile from Manresa, and on the way he sits on the bank of a stream and watches the deep water go by. Then *the eyes of his soul were opened*. The account is careful about what did and did not happen. He had no special vision, it says. His mind was lit up on many subjects, spiritual and intellectual, and from that day everything looked new. It goes further, and this is an old man's claim: that everything he had been given and had learned in all the years up to his sixty-second year was not equal to what he was given that day. It says that he seemed *quite another man*.

Look at where it happens. The Manresa of the account is not a far-off retreat. He lives in a hospital and in a monastery cell, he begs his food from day to day, and he goes to Mass every day. The river is a short walk from all that. His clearing was not a far country. It was an ordinary place near an ordinary life, which is some comfort to anyone who cannot get away.

What it does not say is what he understood. The edition keeps that to itself.

It is easy to read the passage as a prize. The pages before it do not read like one. At Manresa, before the river, the account describes months of torment. He was tormented by the fear that he had not confessed everything, however many times he confessed. He prayed for hours, scourged himself, and once went a week without food or drink, until his confessor told him to eat. At the worst he cried out, *I find no remedy among men*, and was tempted several times to throw himself from the window of his cell, and he answered the temptation with a prayer.

If you carry thoughts like that, please do not carry them alone. Tell someone you trust, or a doctor, today. In Spain and France, 112 is the emergency number.

The river did not repay those months. It came afterwards, sideways, in a place he had not chosen, while he was doing nothing. Some of the changes the account names are more practical than any light. He decided that he had given enough of his time to prayer, and chose *to give sufficient time to sleep*. After a time he gave up his harshest penances and trimmed his nails and hair. So one of the things a man does after the worst of it is to start looking after his body. It is not the smallest lesson at Manresa, and a walker can borrow it without any theology.

We should be honest about the size of the claim. A man seems to himself to become another man by a stream, and the edition tells it as fact, in the voice of a story told many years afterwards. We quote it because its shape is familiar. It often seems that the answer, when one comes, arrives sideways, after the effort, in a place you did not pick. It is not a promise about your river.

For an ordinary day it comes down to a stop. You do not need a stream, and you do not need an insight. Ten minutes with nothing to produce is enough. Nothing may happen, and that is not a failure. The only thing owed to a stop is that you let it be a stop, and do not turn it into a task. Ignatius found his clearing when he sat down. It came after, and not instead of, learning to take care of himself.

## For the walk

- What do I see, if I look at one thing for a long time?
- At the next piece of water, a stream, a fountain, a ford, stop for ten minutes. Sit if you can. Put the phone away, watch the water or the light on it, and let your mind do what it likes. Do not hunt for an insight. If the path offers no water, any slow thing will do: a cloud, or a tree moving in the wind. If someone is walking with you, they can sit beside you and say nothing, and that is a generous thing to do for each other. Afterwards, drink something, and eat if you can, before you go on.

## At the end of the day

If today was a day for stopping, the evening may bring a small guilt: kilometres you did not walk, a schedule that slipped. Set it against what you saw. A stop is a kind of arrival, and it is hard to measure on the same scale as distance.

Try to name one thing you noticed when you stopped, and let it be a real thing and not a lesson. The sound of the water. A colour in the stones. How the light moved when a cloud went over. It need not mean anything. It is enough that you saw it, and that nobody else saw it quite that way.

One practical change the account names after the dark months was to give more time to sleep. So this is a good evening for the small acts of care that no one ever notices. Look at your feet. Drink a glass of water. Stretch the places that complained today. Pack for the morning now, so that the morning is easier. Set the alarm a little later than your first instinct. None of that is glamorous, and all of it is what a long walk is made of.

A river goes on flowing whether or not you watch it. That can be taken as a mercy. The world does not need your attention in order to continue, so you can give it freely. If you notice that you are more tired than usual tonight, it may be that sort of tiredness, which comes from having been still and a little exposed for a while, and not from the kilometres.

If you saw something today, say it aloud to someone at the table, in a single sentence. A thing seen is a little more real once it has been said. And if rain came, as the route notes say it often does, it counts as water too. A wet hour with the hood up is also a way of watching the world go by.

If you sat by the water and nothing came, nothing has gone wrong. Insight is not owed to anyone, and it is not a measure of the day. A day on which you drank, ate, rested and looked at a stream has done a good deal.

And a quiet word, in case tonight is one of the dark ones. The account is honest that Ignatius had such nights, and that he went looking for someone to help him, with mixed results. If tonight is heavy, tell someone you trust, or speak to a doctor tomorrow, and if you are in danger now, call 112. A hard hour does not mean the road was a mistake.

- Where did I stop today, even briefly, and what did I notice?
- What did I do for my body today that had nothing to do with getting further?
- Whom could I tell, if a dark hour came?

## A short prayer

*For the places where the road stopped me, thank you. For water, and light on water, and the long minutes beside it, thank you. Let me care for this body as for a friend. If a dark hour comes, let me not keep it to myself. Let me sleep.*
