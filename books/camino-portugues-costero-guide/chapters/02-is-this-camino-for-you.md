# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

Not very, and not nothing. Both authorities that rate it say the same thing. The official portal of the Portuguese municipalities gives the Portuguese part a difficulty of Medium-Low (*Média-Baixa*) and an average of seven days. The Xunta rates the Galician part Medium-Low overall: five of its eight stages are Low and three, Redondela to Pontevedra, Pontevedra to Caldas de Reis and Caldas de Reis to Padrón, are Medium. The CaminoPlus catalog gives the route a 2 on its scale of 1 to 5.

There are no mountains. What makes the days demanding is length and exposure. The Portuguese days run to ~26 to ~29 km on the leaflet's figures, and the longest Galician day, Baiona to Vigo, is ~27 km on the Xunta's. Of the shore between Portecelo and Oia, the Xunta says that it is flat, with hardly any shade, and that the sea breeze takes the edge off the summer sun. The Confraternity of St James adds that the coastal route is "undulating" because it crosses several rivers. And there is traffic: on the Baiona to Vigo stage the Xunta names the C-550, a regional road, as a constant presence.

## Who it suits

It suits a first-time walker who has trained a little and wants a Camino with a clear shape: a start at a cathedral, a long run beside the sea, a boat across a border river, an arrival at another cathedral. Twelve days with a rest day or two fit inside a fortnight, and the road passes through towns often enough that a day can be cut.

It suits people who love the sea and can live with weather. The wind, the sun and the rain all matter, and a wet, windy day on an open shore asks more than the kilometres suggest.

It suits people who want company in the second half. The Office statistics, as Wikipedia reports them, show a third of those who finish the Coastal route starting in Vigo or Baiona, and from Redondela the road is shared with the Central route.

## Who might choose another

If you want mountains, forest and silence, look elsewhere. The Original Way, in this series, climbs through the mountains of Asturias, and the French Way crosses the Pyrenees and the Meseta.

If you would rather have an inland road of old bridges and old towns, the Central route is *The Portuguese Way*, in this series. If you want a much longer coast, *The Northern Way* follows the north of Spain for ~820 km in the catalog. If you want the end of the land, the walk from Santiago to Finisterre and Muxía is a short one of its own.

If you hope to find the Atlantic empty in July, think again. The Confraternity says the coast is a popular holiday area that can become crowded in summer, with pressure on services and accommodation.

## Questions worth asking yourself

- Can you walk ~25 km on a hard surface, two or three days in a row, with your pack?
- Does a day in wind and sun, with little shade, sound like an adventure or a trial?
- Do you want the whole road from Porto, or the Galician coast from A Guarda or Vigo?
- Are you comfortable with a plan that depends on a small boat?
- What do you want from the walk: sport, solitude, the sea, a rest, prayer, a promise kept? Every answer is a good one, and the road does not ask.

If most of your answers are yes, and the others are "we shall see", you are ready for the next chapter.
