# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office lists access to "hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way" as the first practical use of the credencial, notes that hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute, and asks groups travelling with a support car, or by bicycle, to look for other shelter. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

Which hostels exist, which are open this month, whether you may book a bed and how beds are given out differ from town to town and change from season to season. This book does not list them. The Confraternity of St James warns that the coast is a popular holiday area, crowded in summer, with pressure on services and accommodation. So use the CaminoPlus app for where to sleep tonight, ask locally for tomorrow, and do not rely on one source for the long days.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and pack your rucksack in the hall, not at dawn beside sleepers.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed. Keep your valuables close.
- Earplugs go a long way. Somebody will snore, and if it is you, apologise at breakfast.

**Bedbugs** are a risk in any shared bed anywhere. Check the seams of the mattress, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is not a reason to avoid hostels, only a reason to look before you lie down.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating. A good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

Some places along the Way offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what is on it and what it costs before you sit. A set meal is a good way to eat well after a long day, and an easy way to try what the coast cooks.

The sea is the larder here, and the sources say so in their way. Wikipedia describes Póvoa de Varzim as a town of fishermen and seafarers, with a fishing history that reaches to Newfoundland. In Galicia the Xunta points to A Pedra, in Vigo, as known for its restaurants and for the oysters of the ría, and shows mussel rafts on the water at Rande. In Redondela it names the Festa do Choco, a cuttlefish festival, each May. You do not need to eat all of it. Pick one local thing a day, and eat it without a screen.

Water matters as much. The Xunta says the shore beyond Portecelo has hardly any shade, and the summer sun asks for water. Fill your bottle in each town, and carry something for lunch on the long days: Day 1, Day 4 and Day 7.

## Resting

It is easy to treat the walk as a timetable to be kept. Resist that. A day of rest is part of the walk.

If you have a day to spare, there are candidates. Baiona is a place to slow down: the Xunta says the Cíes Islands, in the Illas Atlánticas National Park, can be reached by boat from there. Viana do Castelo is another, with its old hospital for pilgrims and its hill. Pontevedra is the one that *The Portuguese Way* suggests, and it lies on this road too. The last place is the end. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it deserves more than a night.

On the road, listen to your body before you listen to your plan. Take the short days as short days. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
