# Arriving

## The cathedral

The Coastal Way ends where every Way ends: at the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, the reputed burial place of St James. The Xunta says the traditional entrance to the cathedral for pilgrims on the Portuguese route is through the Praza das Praterías, and that the way into the old quarter runs by the Porta Faxeira and the Rúa do Franco. Wikipedia dates the start of construction to 1075, the Praterías façade to 1103 and the great Baroque façade on the Praza do Obradoiro to 1740.

The cathedral keeps a Holy Door, which Wikipedia says is opened only in a Jacobean Holy Year, and the next Holy Years are 2027 and 2032. Wikipedia adds that in a Holy Year the great censer, the botafumeiro, is swung at all the Pilgrims' Masses. Check the cathedral's own pages for the times of the Mass: they change.

Take a moment in the square before you do anything else. Sit on the stones if you like. The arrival may be quieter or noisier than you expected; there is no right feeling.

## The Pilgrim's Office and the Compostela

The *Compostela* is the certificate of your pilgrimage, and the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago issues it. The rule it follows is in the chapter on the Compostela rule, and it is the Office's to apply: if you are in any doubt, contact it. The Office says that both its reception service and the issuing of the Compostela are completely free.

When you arrive, bring your credencial. You also take a ticket with a QR code, which lets you follow your place in the queue on your phone. The Office warns that in times of great influx it cannot guarantee that you will receive the Compostela on the same day, so do not book a flight home for the afternoon. The Office's website gives its address and its current hours, which are not in this book because they change.

If you walked for other reasons, or are not sure that yours count, the Office also offers a separate *Certificate of Distance*, issued by the Chapter of the Cathedral. It records the kilometres you travelled, whatever your starting point. It carries a fee, and the Office gives the amount. Whichever document you ask for, the Office meets a great many people in a day. Be patient, be warm, and if you have a doubt about your credencial, ask before you queue.

## The next day

Try not to leave on the morning after you arrive. A day in Santiago lets the walk settle. Wash your clothes, sleep late, and look at the old town. On the cathedral square stands the building that the Catholic Monarchs founded as a royal hospital for pilgrims; the Office says those who showed their Compostela there once had the right to stay free for three days, and that it has been a hotel since 1954. The Office adds that the cathedral is open all day, with a chapel where many people sit to think or pray.

If you still have legs and time, some pilgrims walk on to Finisterre and Muxía on the coast, ~90 km farther, a route you will find in the CaminoPlus app's list, and that has a book of its own in this series, *To the End of the Earth*. If you do not, think about the way home. The Confraternity of St James notes a bus service of about four hours between Santiago and Porto; Santiago also has an airport. Check the current times. And buy a postcard, and write to someone you walked with in your head.
