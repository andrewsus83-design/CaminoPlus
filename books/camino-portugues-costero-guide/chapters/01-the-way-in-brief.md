# The Way in brief

## Where it starts and ends

The Coastal Way starts at the Sé of Porto, as the Central route does, and ends at the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela. In Portugal the route that the government has certified passes through Porto, Matosinhos, Maia, Vila do Conde, Póvoa de Varzim, Esposende, Viana do Castelo, Caminha, Vila Nova de Cerveira and Valença. In Galicia the Xunta de Galicia, the regional government, describes the coastal route as entering at A Guarda, "after crossing the mouth of the River Miño", then passing Oia, Baiona and Vigo to join the Portuguese Way at Redondela. After that it is the same road through Pontevedra, Caldas de Reis and Padrón.

## How long is it?

Nobody agrees, and that tells you how this road is built.

- The CaminoPlus catalog gives ~260 km from Porto to Santiago. Wikipedia gives the same ~260 km for the Portuguese Way from Porto.
- The Xunta gives ~163 km for the Galician part, from A Guarda to Santiago. The leaflet's legs add up to ~166 km, and one published plan counts ~151 km.
- For the Portuguese part, the government order of 2022 that certifies the route, and the national tourism authority, give ~138 km from Porto to Valença, in nine stages. The official portal of the ten municipalities says ~149.5 km. An official guide leaflet from the municipalities, whose file dates from 2017, marks its legs at ~29, ~27, ~26 and ~27 km from Porto to Caminha, which is ~109 km, and a further ~32 km on to Valença. The three official figures for Porto to Valença are therefore ~138, ~141 and ~149.5 km.
- Published walking plans put the whole road at ~271 to ~280 km.

Part of the gap is variants: the road has a traditional line, a seaside line on boardwalks, and three ways to cross into Spain. Part of it is where each source starts and stops counting. The twelve days of this book follow the leaflet's legs in Portugal and the Xunta's stage pages in Galicia, and add up to ~272 km on foot, plus the boat. Treat every figure as a good guide and not as a measurement.

## Where it meets its neighbours

The Confraternity of St James says the Coastal route follows the Central one out of Porto as far as Padrão da Légua, then branches off towards Vila do Conde. Wikipedia puts the split "in the countryside of Vila do Conde". Either way, after the first hours you are on a road of your own.

It touches the Central route again at Valença, which the tourism authority of Porto and the North calls the meeting point of the two. That is where the route that Portugal has certified ends. The route the Xunta describes crosses to A Guarda by boat instead, and joins the Central route at Redondela, ~85 km from Santiago by the Xunta's stage figures. A later chapter sets out these joins.

## What it feels like

It is a road of sea, estuaries and small rivers. Wikipedia describes a seaside version that follows beach boardwalks, and pictures a way of ~1.5 km over the dunes at Ofir. The Confraternity calls the Portuguese coast "an interesting if undulating journey", with mixed farms and pine and eucalyptus woods behind the shore. In Galicia the Xunta describes cliffs, Cape Silleiro and the Ría de Vigo, then the inner ría near Redondela with mussel rafts on the water. The Xunta rates its Galician part Medium-Low overall.

## Why it exists

The honest history is shorter than the legend, and the sources do not fully agree.

The oldest well-attested stretch is the first one. The certification text says the route partly follows the Via Veteris, named in the royal inquiries of 1258 and for centuries the chief link between Porto and Vila do Conde. North of Vila do Conde, the same text says, a very old road network is proven along the coastal plain, but pilgrims' use of the route gains prominence only from the 18th century. The official portal says much the same: the road gained weight in the modern age, above all from the 18th century, used by coastal people and by those who landed at the ports. Wikipedia dates its prominence earlier, to the 15th century and the Age of Discovery.

The Municipality of Viana do Castelo calls the Coastal Way a derivation of the Central Way, an alternative link to Galicia. The Portuguese state certified it as a Camino itinerary in February 2022, the third route to be certified, and renewed the certification in 2025. In Galicia the Xunta notes another name, the Monastic Route, from the Cistercian monastery at Oia.

## Who walks it now

Wikipedia, reporting the Pilgrim's Office statistics for 2025, says the Portuguese coastal route was the third most walked route to Santiago, with 17 per cent of pilgrims. Of those who received the Compostela on it, 51 per cent started in Porto, 21 per cent in Vigo and 12 per cent in Baiona. Of all the pilgrims who started in Porto, it adds, 45 per cent went by Vigo and 55 per cent by Tui. Most finished in May and September.

Read together, those figures say that a third of the pilgrims who finish the Coastal route started in Galicia, at Vigo or Baiona, so the Galician stretch may feel busier than the Portuguese one. That is a reading of the statistics, not a promise.
