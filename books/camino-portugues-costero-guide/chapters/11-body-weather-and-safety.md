# Body, weather and safety

## Feet

Look after your feet first. Take your shoes off at lunch and let your feet breathe. Change your socks. Treat a hot spot at once, before it is a blister. Boardwalks and paving are kind to nobody's feet for twenty-seven kilometres, so spend money on good shoes and time on breaking them in.

## Sun and heat

The Xunta says the shore beyond Portecelo is flat with hardly any shade, and that the sea breeze lessens the summer sun. A breeze can make a hot day feel milder than it is, so wear a hat and sun protection anyway. The averages are mild but the record highs are not: ~38.6 °C at Porto airport and ~40.8 °C at Vigo airport. The Confraternity of St James warns of heat with high humidity. Start early, carry more water than you think you need, and plan the hottest hours for a café or a bench. If you feel dizzy, sick or confused, stop in the shade, drink, and ask for help; if it does not pass, call 112.

## Wind and rain

The sources give no wind speeds, but they say the beaches are windier and cooler than the land inland. Carry a windproof layer, and keep your hat on a string. This is also a wet coast: Vigo averages ~1,700 mm of rain a year, on ~126 days, and the Confraternity says that in periods of low pressure the rain can be heavy and prolonged. A rain jacket and a cover for your pack are not optional, and a spare pair of dry socks in a plastic bag is the cheapest comfort on the road. Wet boardwalks and paving can be slick: shorten your stride. Rain on a long day is also a reason to take a shorter one.

## Roads and paths

The Coastal Way meets roads. On the Baiona to Vigo stage the Xunta names the regional road C-550 as a constant presence, and on the shared days it warns of the N-550, which the route weaves with, and of the new high-speed railway that has altered the landscape, and asks you to watch the signs. At the crossing of the N-550 beyond Padrón it says a branch to the right takes you out of the traffic. The official portal of the Portuguese municipalities says the same in one line: for your safety, do not walk on roads with heavy traffic, and use the certified, signposted route. Where you must walk a road, the Confraternity advises you to use the indicated strip between the road and the drainage ditch.

Where there is no path, walk facing the traffic, in single file, and never in the dark. Look at the arrows at every junction.

## The boat and the sea

The sea is beside you all day, and it is not a swimming pool. Do not assume there is a lifeguard or a flag on the beach. Ask locally, follow any signs, and do not swim alone or when you are tired. On the boat across the Minho, follow the instructions of the crew, and keep your phone and your credencial dry.

## Border and papers

Wikipedia lists Portugal and Spain as members of the Schengen Area, so there are normally no checks between them, though member states can bring in temporary controls. Carry your passport or identity card anyway, as you should in any country.

## When to stop

Stop when something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, when you are dizzy, when the weather turns, or when the light is going. A short day costs you an afternoon; a bad decision can cost you the Camino. Tell someone your plan. The emergency number is 112, in Portugal and in Spain.

Before you go, check for diversions, closures and, in summer, heat and fire warnings. A route can change between this book's date and your walk.
