# Day 8. A little bird with weak wings

*How large a hope are you allowed to carry, and how small a step does it need?*

## The passage

> We must have great confidence; because it is very necessary for us not to contract our desires, but put our trust in God; for, if we do violence to ourselves by little and little, we shall, though not at once, reach that height which many Saints by His grace have reached. … His Majesty seeks and loves courageous souls; but they must be humble in their ways, and have no confidence in themselves. I never saw one of those lag behind on the road; and never a cowardly soul, though aided by humility, make that progress in many years which the former makes in a few. I am astonished at the great things done on this road by encouraging oneself to undertake great things, though we may not have the strength for them at once; the soul takes a flight upwards and ascends high, though, like a little bird whose wings are weak, it grows weary and rests.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XIII (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Some mornings start with a hill that looks too big at breakfast. If you cross the Sierra de Guadarrama, the app's route notes mention the Navacerrada pass on the way into the province of Segovia, and that is the sort of morning where you look at the line on the map and say nothing. A simple question arrives. Is this too much? There are two ways of answering it wrongly, and Teresa's chapter has something to say about both.

The first wrong answer is the timid one, dressed as modesty. In the same chapter she warns that the devil hinders those who start to pray by giving them a false notion of humility. "He makes them think it is pride to have large desires, to wish to imitate the Saints, and to long for martyrdom." She calls this a trick. A person is told that it would be presumptuous to hope for much, and the hope is quietly put away. Any walker who has said "I'm not the sort of person who does this" has been given the same line. It sounds humble. It is mostly fear.

Against it she sets the passage. We must have "great confidence", she says, and not shrink our desires, but put our trust in God. We will reach, "though not at once", the height that many saints have reached. Then comes her line about courage: God "seeks and loves courageous souls; but they must be humble in their ways, and have no confidence in themselves". Notice the two halves. Courage and humility are not opposites. The courage is to want a great deal. The humility is to know the wanting does not rest on your own strength. A walker has a version of this: you decide to cross the mountain, and you also know that you need water, a forecast, and a good start.

She then gives a picture that suits tired people better than any slogan. The soul "takes a flight upwards and ascends high", she writes, "though, like a little bird whose wings are weak, it grows weary and rests". The bird rests. It does not come down in disgrace. Resting is part of the flight, and the weak wings are not hidden. In the sentence before she says that great things are done by "encouraging oneself to undertake great things, though we may not have the strength for them at once". The word to keep is "at once". Nothing here asks for a leap. It asks for a direction, and for the long business of getting there a bit at a time.

Her chapter has fences around all this, and they matter. A little earlier in it she writes "Discretion is necessary throughout". Further on, in the same teaching, she says it would be wrong for a person who is weak and sickly "to undertake much fasting and sharp penances", or "any austerities of this kind". She means it. She does not want large desires turned into harm to the body. She also has a sharp joke against advisers who are too cautious: she does not want a director who would "teach us to crawl like toads", nor one who is satisfied when the soul "shows itself fit only to catch lizards". Courage with discretion, then. And the passage's own phrase, "do violence to ourselves by little and little", belongs with those fences. As she uses it here, it means the steady effort of choosing the harder good thing a little at a time. It does not mean harshness.

She leans for support on three short remembered lines. One is from Paul: that all things are possible in God. Another is Augustine's prayer: "Give me, O Lord, what Thou commandest, and command what Thou wilt." The third is a picture: "St. Peter lost nothing by throwing himself into the sea, though he was afterwards afraid". Notice that last clause. Peter was afraid afterwards, and still lost nothing. Courage is not the absence of fear. It is moving while afraid, and Teresa gives herself that permission.

Be straight about the risk. Large hopes can lead to bad decisions on a mountain. Weather can change, and a pass in cloud is not a pass in sun. Start early, carry more water than you think you need, look at the forecast, and be ready to turn round without shame. 112 is the number for emergencies in Spain. Courage here includes the courage to stop. The same Teresa who says "not at once" would say it to you.

Day to day, then, name the large hope, perhaps only to yourself. Then reduce it to the next step you can see: the next bend, the next tree. Rest as the bird rests. And when someone, perhaps your own voice, says that it is pride to hope for much, ask whether it sounds more like humility or like fear.

## For the walk

- What large thing am I allowed to hope for from this walk, and what is the next small step toward it?
- On the first climb of the day, pick a tree, a corner or a post a little way ahead. Walk to it, breathe, look back at how far you have come, then pick the next. If the weather turns or the air is hot, shorten the steps rather than the plan.

## At the end of the day

Think of what you dared today. It need not have been a mountain. It may have been a conversation with a stranger in halting Spanish, or walking a kilometre further than you meant to, or deciding to stop when you wanted to push on. Courage has no fixed size. It is measured by how far it took you from what was easy.

Teresa has one more sentence for the evening, from her thirty-first chapter. She says it is very necessary for our weak nature that we have great confidence, and that we should not suppose "if we do our best" that we shall fail "to obtain the victory at last". It is a kind sentence, because it is conditional. It does not say you will win. It says that doing your best is not a doomed thing, and that you need not walk as though it were.

A hard day shows courage and caution as friends. By evening the day's decisions are visible: where you started early, where you took the longer way round, where you turned back. If one of them looked like a retreat, set it beside the bird. Nothing in Teresa's passage says the wings must carry the bird the whole way in one go, and nobody asked it to.

The evening also shows the sentences you said to yourself. "I am not the sort of person who does this." It might have been about hills, or Spanish, or asking for a room, or sleeping in a dormitory. The voice that says it is quiet and sounds sensible, which is just how Teresa describes the false humility that makes people shrink their wishes. Today's walk may have contradicted it somewhere. If you can find the place, keep it. It is evidence.

The bird with weak wings rests. How did you rest today? Resting is easy to do badly: sitting on a wall and scrolling, or lying on a bunk in a knot of worry. A real rest has a loosening in it. If you had one, however brief, give it credit. If you had none, make a small one now, with your feet up and your hands open.

If you want a large hope for tomorrow, name it in a single sentence tonight and leave it on the pillow. Hopes are better kept in a rested head, but a sentence put down now will still be there at breakfast, and you will not have to find it again.

After a day of daring, the evening can bring the thing Peter felt afterwards. The fear arrives once the danger is behind you. Your knees may shake in the bunk when they did not on the path. That is not weakness returning. It is the body catching up with what it did.

- What did I dare today, and what did it cost me?
- Where did I rest like the bird, and did I let myself?
- Where did I say "I am not the sort of person who..." today, and was it true?

## A short prayer

*Give me a large hope and a small step. Let me trust the ground and the plan, and know when to turn back. Where my wings are weak, let me rest and not call it failure. Carry me a little, as far as the next bend.*
