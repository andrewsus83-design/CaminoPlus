# Day 7. This house is not mine

*What do you do when the long thing stays long?*

## The passage

> Now and then everything seemed to fail; particularly one day, before the Provincial came, when the prioress ordered me to meddle no more with it, and to give it up altogether. I betook myself to God, and said, "O Lord, this house is not mine; it was founded for Thee; and now that there is no one to take up the cause, do Thou protect it." I now felt myself in peace, and as free from anxiety as if the whole world were on my side in the matter; and at once I looked upon it as safe. … Writing thus briefly, it is impossible for me to explain what took place during the two years that passed between the beginning and the completion of the monastery: the last six months and the first six months were the most painful. … In this way they came to approve of what they had condemned; and so, by degrees, they withdrew from the lawsuit, and would say that they now felt it to be a work of God, since His Majesty had been pleased to carry it on in the face of so much opposition.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XXXVI (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Every long walk has a middle. The start is behind you, with its nerves and its fresh socks. The end is out of sight. What is left is a long run of days that look much alike. The app's route notes describe the last part of this road as flat stages across the Castilian plains, and a flat stage has its own kind of trial: nothing to climb, nothing to conquer, only the same field and the same sky and the road going on.

Teresa's passage comes from the middle of the longest trouble in her book. She wanted to found a small house of nuns in Ávila, very poor and very strict, and nearly everyone told her not to. Her own monastery disliked the idea. In her words, people said it was folly. The Provincial, who had approved, changed his mind. Later, the town authorities met and resolved to break the new house up, and a lawsuit began. She had no money, she says, and no idea what to do. When she looks back she says that she cannot explain what happened in the two years between the beginning and the completion, except this: "the last six months and the first six months were the most painful".

Hold on to that last phrase, because walkers know it. The hardest stretches of a long task are often the very beginning and the very end. The beginning is hard because nothing has yet shown that it will work. The end is hard because you are tired and it is not quite over. The middle may be long and dull, and still easier on the spirit than either.

The moment she describes in the first part of the passage is the lowest. Everything seemed to fail. The prioress, her superior, ordered her to meddle no more and to give it up altogether. Teresa went to God, as she says, and spoke to Him plainly: this house is not mine, it was founded for You, and since nobody is left to take up the cause, You protect it. What follows is the part to notice. She felt peace "as if the whole world were on my side in the matter", and at once she looked upon it as safe.

Read that as a walker. Nothing outside had changed. The prioress had not relented, the lawsuit was still running, the money had not arrived. The change was in her hands, which had been clenched around the whole thing and now were open. She did not stop working. A few lines before our passage she writes that the servants of God inside the house "did more by their prayers than I did with all my negotiations, though the affair needed the utmost attention". Both statements stand: the matter needed attention, and she could not carry the outcome. She did her part, and she handed back the rest.

There is a doubt that belongs here, and it should be said. Letting go can be a polite name for giving up. How would you tell? Teresa's answer is in her habits rather than her feelings. She went on taking advice, and she was prepared to be told she was wrong. She says that if wise men had found a fault in the whole matter, she "would have given up the founding of a thousand monasteries". She also admits, at the height of the opposition, that she "thought that people were partly in the right". She was not a woman who thought she could not be mistaken.

Then the end, in the last stretch of our passage. The people who had resisted the new house came round. They "came to approve of what they had condemned", she writes, and in time they "withdrew from the lawsuit". Hear this as one woman's report, told by the person for whom it worked. A road does not always end that way. Many hard things are abandoned, and some of them rightly. Her story is not a promise. It is an example of someone who kept working at the thing that was hers, took good advice, and let go of the thing that was not.

One last caution, since the house she founded lived under a strict rule. Nobody should read this day as a call to hard fasting or to any harshness toward the body. The stubbornness in the passage is for a project, in the open, with advisers. It is not an ordeal chosen for its own sake.

Put plainly, the middle of a road asks for this. Do your part for the hour you are in. Let the finish be someone else's business for today. And when the road is long and flat and the same, let the sameness be what it is, a kind of weather that passes.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying today that is not mine to carry?
- At the next bench, post or bend in the road, name one worry you cannot act on before tonight. Say, silently, "not mine, for now", and leave it there. Do the same again at the next landmark, with whatever you have picked up in between. By the end of the stage the pack may feel lighter, though it holds exactly what it held.

## At the end of the day

The middle of a long walk has a particular tiredness. It is not the first-day soreness or the last-day eagerness. It is a flat, steady wear, the feeling of being a long way from both ends. If that is where you are tonight, do not take it as a sign that something is wrong. It is what a middle feels like.

Think back to what in today was yours to do. The legs, the feet, the water, the pace, the greeting, the decision about where to sleep. Those were yours, and you did them. Think, then, about what you carried that was not yours: next week's problem at home, a message that cannot be answered until tomorrow, a worry about something miles away. You may have carried it all day, and it weighed the same as a stone in the pack.

Teresa has one more detail worth keeping, from three chapters earlier. When the first plan collapsed and her confessor told her to drop it, she did. She says she remained quiet "for five or six months, neither thinking nor speaking of the matter". It would be easy to pass over that, but it is an act of discipline, and rarer than starting. She did not nag it, or revive it every morning. She waited, and she says she never lost the sense that it would be done. You may have something that wants that kind of waiting, a decision, a hope, a conversation. Tonight is not the time to push it.

One more thing from the same chapter, for evenings. On the day the house was founded, Teresa says, she was so happy she was beside herself. About three or four hours afterwards a crowd of doubts came down on her: had she done a foolish thing, would the nuns bear the rule, what business was it of hers? She says her anguish was "like one in the agony of death", and "one of the most distressing moments I ever passed in all my life". Then it lifted. She puts it down to the devil. You may put it down to being tired and keyed up, after a day that asked a lot. Either way, she draws the lesson that "No joy is lasting; everything is liable to change". If the evening brings doubts about the whole walk, they may be the tail of the day and not a verdict on it.

There is also the other side of persistence, which is stopping. Some tiredness is not weather, it is a message. A foot that hurts more each day is information. So is a cold, or heat that has left you dizzy. If a rest day would help, take it, and do not think of it as failing. A person you trust, or a doctor, can tell you better than the road can. Teresa's persistence was a wise person's persistence, with advisers beside it.

- What did I do today that was mine to do, and did I do it?
- What did I carry that was never mine?
- Whom could I ask whether to press on or to rest?

## A short prayer

*Let me do my part and hand back the rest. Where the road is long, give me a pace I can keep. If I am told to stop, let me hear it kindly. What is not mine to carry, let me put down tonight, and let me sleep.*
