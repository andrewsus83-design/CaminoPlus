# Day 3. The picture in the corner

*What finally makes you stop?*

## The passage

> My soul was now grown weary; and the miserable habits it had contracted would not suffer it to rest, though it was desirous of doing so. It came to pass one day, when I went into the oratory, that I saw a picture which they had put by there, and which had been procured for a certain feast observed in the house. It was a representation of Christ most grievously wounded; and so devotional, that the very sight of it, when I saw it, moved me--so well did it show forth that which He suffered for us. So keenly did I feel the evil return I had made for those wounds, that I thought my heart was breaking. I threw myself on the ground beside it, my tears flowing plenteously, and implored Him to strengthen me once for all, so that I might never offend Him any more. … But this last time, before that picture of which I am speaking, I seem to have made greater progress; for I was now very distrustful of myself, placing all my confidence in God. It seems to me that I said to Him then that I would not rise up till He granted my petition. I do certainly believe that this was of great service to me, because I have grown better ever since.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter IX (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Some days a thing you have passed a hundred times stops you. A wall, a tree, an old man's wave from a doorway. Nothing about it has changed. What has changed is you, and the reason is usually tiredness. A rested walker walks past. Someone who has been going on for days, and is tired of the sound of their own thoughts, may find the road has given them a wall to lean on.

Teresa's turn came like that. Her ninth chapter begins with six plain words: "My soul was now grown weary." She had been a nun for about twenty years. The annals printed with this edition set the scene in 1555, when she was about forty. She had been praying and not praying, going in and drifting out, and the habits she had picked up "would not suffer it to rest". Then, one day, she went into the oratory and saw a picture that had been put there for a feast. It was an ordinary object. Someone had set it in a corner.

What she saw was Christ badly wounded, and the picture, she says, was so well made that it moved her. She felt how poorly she had repaid those wounds, and thought her heart would break. She threw herself on the ground beside it and begged to be strengthened "once for all".

This is a Christian picture, and the book says so. The guilt in it is heavy, and phrases such as "the evil return I had made" may jar if you do not share her faith or her way of speaking about herself. Notice, though, what the picture does. It does not lecture. It ends the bargaining. For years she had been trying to arrange a life that kept both prayer and pleasure, and in front of this image she could not keep the arrangement up. If you do not pray, you may know a version of it: a photograph, a letter, a stretch of road that strips away the excuses for a minute.

Look at the second half of the passage. She says "this last time", and the words matter. There had been earlier tears, earlier resolutions; earlier in the same chapter she describes placing herself in thought at Christ's feet, after the example of the Magdalene, in the hope that her tears would not be despised. So this was not the single moment of a story. It was one of a series, the one that held. What was different? She says she was "very distrustful of myself, placing all my confidence in God". The earlier turns had been her own efforts. This time she stopped relying on them.

And she was stubborn. She seems to remember telling Him that she would not rise until her petition was granted. It is a child's kind of prayer, and an old woman's. You may not pray at all, and still recognise the posture: sitting down in the road and refusing to go further until something is settled.

A little later in the chapter she is given Augustine's *Confessions*. She writes: "I thought I saw myself there described." What troubled her was that Augustine and the other saints she loved had been called once, and had stayed called, while her own relapses were "now so many". She took courage again, she says, from remembering how much she was loved: "Of His mercy I never doubted once, but I did very often of myself."

Be careful not to make the turn bigger than she does. She does not say she was cured. She says "I have grown better ever since", which is modest, and the chapters that follow still hold fear, doubt and long dry spells. A turn is where a road bends, and the road goes on. The morning after it is usually plain. The picture is in its corner, the boots are where you left them, and the first hour has to be walked like any other.

In practice it comes to letting yourself be stopped, which is harder than it sounds on a walk that is mostly about going on. And it comes to being wary of the plan that rests on your own strength alone. Whatever help you reach for, a friend, a doctor, a prayer, a bench, Teresa's report is that the turn came when she stopped trying to manage it by herself.

## For the walk

- What would stop me today, if I let it?
- When something catches your eye on the way, a view, a door, a picture in a church, stop for one full minute. Do not move on until the minute is over. Let the thing be what it is, and notice what it asks of you, if anything. If nothing catches your eye in the first hour, choose something plain and give it the minute anyway: a gatepost, a puddle, the line of the next hill.

## At the end of the day

You have probably stopped today, whether you meant to or not: at a crossing, at a fountain, at a bar for coffee, at a bench when the legs said so. Most stops on a long walk are practical. A few are something else. The difference is hard to explain, and easy to notice afterwards. A stop that was practical leaves you ready to go. A stop of the other kind leaves you slightly changed.

Teresa has a gentle note on this in the same chapter. She says it helped her "to look on fields, water, and flowers", and that the sight of these things "was as a book unto me". They woke her up and brought her back to herself. If you were outdoors today, you may have had a little of that without looking for it: a ditch with water in it, a field, a flower you could not name. The evening is a good time to bring one of them back.

She mentions a small habit from the same years. For a long time, "nearly every night before I fell asleep", she thought for a little while about one scene, and it became as fixed as the sign of the cross before sleep. A turn needs something small like that to keep it from fading, a few lines at the end of the day that do not depend on how you feel. It might be a name, a view, a line of a song. It does not have to be a prayer.

Look, too, at the way she asks. She does not make a vow in front of the picture. She begs to be strengthened, so that she may never fall again. That is a request, and a vow is a promise. It matters at the end of a long day, when it is easy to promise the moon: tomorrow you will walk slower, eat better, call home, stop being short with the person in the next bunk. A request is gentler, and more honest about what you can do alone. It leaves room for the help that may come.

The turn she describes is not a thing to wait for tonight. You may be tired of something in yourself: a habit, a mood, a way of talking to people you love. You do not have to settle it before you sleep. It is enough to admit it, once, to yourself. Teresa put it off for years, and she is plain about the cost.

The body at the end of a long day is a poor judge of its own life. A decision made on a bunk at nine o'clock is often wrong by breakfast. So do not decide anything big tonight. Note only what stopped you, and what you are tired of, and let the rest sleep. And if nothing stopped you today, that is a day too. Some stretches of road simply go by. The picture was in its corner for the feast, and nobody knows for how long before she looked at it.

- What stopped me today, even for a moment?
- What am I tired of in myself, and have I said so to anyone?
- Where did I let a field, a stream or a flower say something to me?

## A short prayer

*Let me be stopped by what matters. Where I have been going in circles, let me sit down. I cannot mend myself by trying harder. Hold what I cannot hold, and give me rest tonight, and a clear head in the morning.*
