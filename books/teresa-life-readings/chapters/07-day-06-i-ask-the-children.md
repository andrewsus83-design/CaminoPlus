# Day 6. I ask the children

*What would change if you said plainly what you do not know?*

## The passage

> One of my faults was this: I had a very imperfect knowledge of my Breviary and of my duties in choir, simply because I was careless and given to vanities; and I knew the other novices could have taught me. But I never asked them, that they might not know how little I knew. … My singing of the Office was bad, and I felt it much if I had not learned the part intrusted to me,--not because I made mistakes before our Lord, which would have been a virtue, but because I made them before the many nuns who heard me. I was so full of my own reputation, that I was disturbed, and therefore did not sing what I had to sing even so well as I might have done. Afterwards, I ventured, when I did not know it very well, to say so. At first, I felt it very much; but afterwards I found pleasure in doing it. So, when I began to be indifferent about its being known that I could not sing well, it gave me no pain at all, and I sang much better.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XXXI (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

You come to a junction with no arrow. The phone says one thing and the ground another. A boy is walking a dog along the lane. You can go on and hope, or you can ask. Asking costs a moment of looking like someone who does not know the way, and that moment can feel surprisingly expensive.

Teresa spent years paying that price in advance. In this passage the thing she does not know is small: the Breviary, the book of daily prayers, and her part in the Office, the set of prayers the sisters sang together in choir. Other novices could have taught her. She says so. But she "never asked them", she writes, "that they might not know how little I knew". It is hard to read that without a small laugh of recognition. The thing she was protecting was her reputation, and it was a flimsy thing.

Then she lets us watch what the protection cost. Her singing was bad, she says, and she felt it. The worst of it was where the pain came from. It was not that she made mistakes in front of God, "which would have been a virtue", but that she made them in front of the other nuns. She was "so full of my own reputation" that she was disturbed, and so she sang worse than she might have. Fear of being found out took her breath and her pitch.

And then the turn, which is the funniest and most hopeful part. She began to say, when she did not know a part well, that she did not. At first it hurt. Then she found pleasure in it. When she grew indifferent to people knowing she could not sing well, it gave her no pain at all, "and I sang much better". In a sentence just before the stretch we have quoted, she says that now, "even when I know a thing, but yet am very slightly in doubt about it, I ask the children". She has lost nothing by it, she says, "neither honour nor credit". She suspects her memory improved.

Humility, in this account, is not self-contempt. It is saying what is so: I know this, I do not know that, I need a hand with the other. The young novices she asked had a small and useful gift, and she was glad of it. The fear had been about status, and it spoiled the singing.

This matters because Teresa herself, elsewhere in her book, can sound as if humility meant calling herself wicked on every page. She offers a test for telling the real thing from the other. In her thirtieth chapter she says that "true humility is not attended with trouble; it does not disturb the soul; it causes neither obscurity nor aridity: on the contrary, it consoles". Of a false one, which pictures the worst and leaves the soul in the dark, she says it gives "no light for any good work". In the same chapter as our passage she looks back at a fear of being praised and says "I understood afterwards that this was not true humility, because it disturbed me so much". So when she is hard on herself, you may remember that she warned against exactly that, and you do not have to join in.

There is one more small picture in this chapter, which is a different sort of humility altogether. She used to fold up the sisters' mantles as they left the choir, secretly, thinking of herself as serving the angels who had been there praising God. When the sisters found out she was ashamed. The shame came, she says, not because she was humble but because she was afraid they would laugh. Her virtue, she says, "was not strong enough to bear that they should know of it". Even the secret kindness had her vanity in it, and she says so cheerfully.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Ask the question. Say "I am lost" and "I can't manage the last four kilometres" and "I don't know what that bird is", plainly, to the person next to you. And hold the other side loosely too. There is no prize for being the most self-effacing walker on the road. If you are praised, say thank you and be done. If you are wrong, say so and carry on. Most of what looks like humility is truthfulness. Most of what is hard about it is the first moment.

## For the walk

- Where am I pretending today to know something I do not?
- Ask one real question of someone you meet: the way, the name of a plant, how their day has been. Mean it, and listen to the answer. Or do one small service that nobody will see, and let it stay unseen. Pick it up from the road: a bottle in the verge, a loose stone moved off the path, a word of encouragement to the walker behind you. Then walk on without telling anyone.

## At the end of the day

At the end of a day there is a small ledger that has nothing to do with kilometres. It records the times you smoothed something over. Said "fine" when you were not fine. Nodded as if you knew what was being said. Took the credit for a good guess. These are very small lies, and they cost about what Teresa's cost her, which is some of your breath.

She is a good guide to this, because she does not stop at the shame. Later in the same chapter she catches herself doing the opposite. When she saw that someone thought well of her, she would find some roundabout way to let them know of her sins, "that seemed to relieve me". Her confessor told her to stop. She decided it was not humility at all but "the result of temptation". In the next paragraph she says, with an edge: "I was therefore not humble, but a coward." It is worth remembering at the end of a day when you have been busily putting yourself down. Playing small can be a way of hiding. It protects you from being asked for more.

Notice, too, whom she asked. She says she asks "the children", the youngest and least regarded of the house. There is a lesson in the choice. We tend to ask the person who looks as if they know: the one with the guidebook, the one in the good boots. Often the person who knows is the one who lives here, the child on the corner, the old woman at the window. Think whether today you asked the right sort of person, or only the sort who would not make you feel small.

So the truth runs between two errors: the show of knowing and the show of not knowing. Your day probably had some of each. You may have bluffed at breakfast and played yourself down at supper. Nothing needs to be settled tonight. Just see which of the two you lean towards, and how it feels in the body, because the body usually knows first.

There is a small thing to try over supper, if there is a supper. Ask the person beside you a question to which you do not know the answer, and mean it: where the day began for them, what they carry, what they are walking toward. You may find that the wish to look as if you knew things was a weight you had not noticed carrying all day.

An honest word about tiredness. Fatigue is a great breeder of small untruths, since it is easier to say "fine" than to explain. If you can say one true thing tonight to the person nearest you, "I am done in", you will have done a day's work in humility. The rest can wait for rest.

- Where did I pretend today to know the way?
- What small service did I do that I would not mind being unseen?
- Where did I play myself down to avoid being asked for more?

## A short prayer

*Let me say what I know, and no more. Let me say what I do not know, and not be afraid. Keep me from the show of knowing and from the show of modesty. Let the small things I did today be enough, seen or unseen. Let me rest in what is true.*
