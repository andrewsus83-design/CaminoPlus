# How to use this book

This is a small book of ten days, made to go with the Camino de Madrid. In the CaminoPlus catalogue that road runs from Madrid to Sahagún, about 320 km, and the app's route notes describe it this way: from the city north over the Sierra de Guadarrama, by way of Segovia, then across the flat Castilian plains to Sahagún, where it joins the Camino Francés. It is a road of about two weeks. Ten days is the length of the reading, not a plan of stages. Read a day twice if you like it, or on a rest day, and nobody will mind.

## Why this book for this road

Teresa of Ávila is a Spanish saint, born in Ávila in 1515, and for this road across the Spanish interior she seemed the right companion. She is Spain's own saint, and her book is about walking a long way slowly, with a great deal of doubt along the road. A caution, so that nobody is surprised: the app's route notes for the Camino de Madrid name Madrid, the Sierra de Guadarrama, Segovia and the plains to Sahagún, and Ávila is not among them. This book does not claim that the road passes her town. The title names where her story arrives, the small house she founded in Ávila, which you will meet on Day 7.

## How a day works

Each day has a morning reading of about five minutes and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from Teresa's *Life*, in the exact words of David Lewis's English translation, as printed in 1904. Read the passage twice, the second time aloud if you can. The English is old, and it slows the eye, which is half its use.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Teresa's. It starts from something on the road and lets the passage open it. It says what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. They are better done on your feet than on paper.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have lived, with two or three questions to lie down with. You need not write anything down.
- **A short prayer.** Original and brief. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular.

## A Christian book, and a strange one in places

The *Life* is a Catholic book, written by a Carmelite nun for her confessors in the sixteenth century. It tells of visions, voices and raptures, which she describes as best she can and says she cannot always put into words. It tells of a strict religious house and of her own harsh judgements on herself. We chose passages a walker can follow, and where one is strange, the reflection says so calmly. Nothing here asks you to look for such experiences or to copy any penance, and nobody should hurt themselves, or go without food or rest, to read this book well. Teresa herself says that people who are weak and sickly should not undertake heavy fasting. If you carry a pain you cannot shake, talk to a doctor or to someone you trust. In Spain, 112 is the emergency number.

People walk for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Teresa believed to recognise a woman who was tired of herself, who asked for help, who was laughed at, and who started again.

## The shape of the ten days

The days follow the shape of her life, and they also follow a walk.

- **Day 1**, the child and her family.
- **Day 2**, the struggle to pray, and years of going through the motions.
- **Day 3**, the turn.
- **Day 4**, her best-known picture of prayer: a garden watered four ways.
- **Day 5**, friends and thanks.
- **Day 6**, humility and plain truth.
- **Day 7**, the long road and its trials.
- **Day 8**, courage and trust.
- **Day 9**, the dark and the dry.
- **Day 10**, coming home to the work of each day.

These are a shape, not a timetable. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off. At home it works the same way: the morning with your tea, the evening before bed.

Three words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks. The *credencial* is the pilgrim's passport, stamped along the way. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly, may your Way go well.

Read slowly. The *Life* was written in snatched hours by a busy woman, and it can be read the same way.
