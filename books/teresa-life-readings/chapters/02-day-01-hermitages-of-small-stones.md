# Day 1. Hermitages of small stones

*Who put the first stones of your road in place?*

## The passage

> I had a father and mother, who were devout and feared God. Our Lord also helped me with His grace. All this would have been enough to make me good, if I had not been so wicked. My father was very much given to the reading of good books; and so he had them in Spanish, that his children might read them. These books, with my mother's carefulness to make us say our prayers, and to bring us up devout to our Lady and to certain Saints, began to make me think seriously when I was, I believe, six or seven years old. It helped me, too, that I never saw my father and mother respect anything but goodness. … As soon as I saw it was impossible to go to any place where people would put me to death for the sake of God, my brother and I set about becoming hermits; and in an orchard belonging to the house we contrived, as well as we could, to build hermitages, by piling up small stones one on the other, which fell down immediately; and so it came to pass that we found no means of accomplishing our wish.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter I (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

If you leave from Madrid, the first hour is streets: crossings, kerbs, a city still waking up. The app's route notes call it a city start, and after that the road turns toward the Sierra de Guadarrama. Wherever your own first hour is, one thing holds for it. You did not invent this road, or the boots, or the idea of walking so far. Someone lent you a map, or told you a story, or left a book on a shelf. The walk began before you did.

Teresa starts her Life in the same place, with the people who set her going. She was born in Ávila in 1515, and her book opens on a house. The picture is plain. Her father liked good books and kept them in Spanish so that the children could read them. Her mother made sure they said their prayers. Nothing in it is grand. What Teresa picks out is what the children saw: that she never saw her father and mother "respect anything but goodness".

The same shelf, as she says in her next chapter, held another kind of book too. Her mother was fond of tales of chivalry, and the children read them, taking care that their father never saw. A house can set you going in two directions at once, and the good one is not always the louder.

Then comes the part that makes people smile, or wince. Between the two halves of the passage, she tells how she and the brother nearest her in age read the lives of the saints together. When they read of martyrs, it struck her that the vision of God was "very cheaply purchased". So they planned to go to the country of the Moors, "begging our way for the love of God", and be beheaded there. Their difficulty, she says, was their father and mother. An editor's note in this edition, drawing on an early biographer, adds that the two had crossed the bridge outside the city gate before an uncle met them and brought them home.

Take that plan calmly. It is a child's plan, and a dark one: two children hoping to be killed for God. Teresa does not praise it. Looking back, she says it was "not out of any love of Him of which I was conscious". She wanted to arrive quickly. Nobody should read this and wish for martyrdom, and nobody should copy any severity from these pages. What you can take is smaller and sunnier: a child who read about a road and wanted to walk it.

When that failed, the children did something they could manage. They built hermitages in the orchard from small stones, "which fell down immediately". It is hard to think of a better first picture for a long walk than a tower of loose stones that will not stand. Stone on stone, and down it comes. Teresa does not say they tried again. She does say the wish stayed. A few lines later she writes that she contrived to be alone "for the sake of saying my prayers", and that there were many of them.

There is a harder thing in the passage, and it is better said at once. On her first page Teresa says that a good home "would have been enough to make me good, if I had not been so wicked". She will say something like this again and again, and it can wear on a reader. She was writing for confessors who had told her to describe her prayer. In her prologue she says she would have liked to speak of her sins in detail, and was held back. Yet the faults of her girlhood that she actually names are a taste for books of chivalry, fussing over her hair, and gossip with cousins. She is hard on herself. You do not have to agree with every verdict to keep reading, and in a few days she will say something about that habit herself.

What does it ask of an ordinary morning? Mostly to look back before you look forward. On the first day of a long walk the mind runs ahead to the end, to Sahagún, to the bed tonight. Teresa's own first page is about the beginning: the shelf, the bedtime prayers, the brother, the orchard, the stones that would not stay up. It suggests that your road also began with small things, set in place by people who may not know that you are here.

## For the walk

- Who set the first stones of this walk in place: the one who lent a rucksack, who told the story, who is waiting at the other end?
- For the first ten minutes, count your steps in tens. At each ten, name one person who is behind this walk, and let that be the whole thought. If you run out of names, begin again with the first. If a name brings something sore with it, keep it company for one step more, then go on to the next.

## At the end of the day

The first evening of a walk has a taste of its own. The legs report in, one by one. The pack comes off and the body seems to float for a minute. Whatever the day was, it is behind you, and a long road is ahead of you. That is a good moment for the people who set you going. They are mostly absent now, and they are easiest to think about when you are sitting down.

Teresa's mother comes back into the Life a little later in the same chapter. She died young. Teresa says that her mother was only thirty-three, and that she herself was about twelve, a little less. In her grief she went to an image of Our Lady, and, in her words, "with many tears implored her to be my mother". Reading that, you may think of your own loss, or of nobody in particular. Some people walk for a reason of that kind, and many walk for no such reason. Either way the evening can make room. If you carry someone, you can set them down beside the bed for the night. They will still be there in the morning.

It is worth remembering that not everyone had a house like hers. The passage is not a reproach. Teresa counts three sisters and nine brothers, a noisy house, and she says she was "the most cherished of my father", which did not make her path easier. If your own first house was cold or thin, the road still began somewhere. Some people set out because of where they came from, and some in spite of it. Both are walking.

Think also of the stones. Most days, something goes up and something falls down. A plan for the afternoon that was too ambitious. A conversation that did not build into anything. A resolve about pace, made at breakfast and abandoned by eleven. The children in the orchard could not make a wall that stood. They gave up that plan and kept the wish behind it. If today's plan changed (the distance, the bed, the hour you left), look at what lies underneath. You wanted to walk, and you walked. That may be enough to call the day well spent. It is no great comfort to be told so, and it is some comfort to be shown it, in a book written more than four centuries ago.

Before you sleep, lay out tomorrow's clothes, fill the bottle, and put the boots where you can find them in the dark. These are small stones, set the night before, and they stay up.

On the first night the body is not sure what has been asked of it, and it may take a few days to settle. Sleep comes before any big thought. If this evening's questions feel heavy, answer one and leave the other.

- Who set me on this road, and have I thanked them?
- What did I try to build today that would not stand, and can it wait until tomorrow?
- What did I long for as a child that I could still try, in a smaller form?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for those who set me going. For the shelf of books, the bedtime voice, the hand on the shoulder at the door. Let what I build today be small enough to stand. Where something fell, let me begin again in the morning. Keep close the ones I carry, and let me sleep.*
