# About the book and its author

## The woman

Teresa was born in Ávila on 28 March 1515. The translator's preface says that in the world, and even as a nun, she was known as Doña Teresa Sanchez Cepeda Davila y Ahumada. She entered the Carmelite monastery of the Incarnation in Ávila as a young woman. The edition's notes disagree about the year, so we give none.

In 1562 she helped begin a small, very poor and strict house in Ávila, the monastery of St. Joseph, and the preface dates the start of the Reform of the Carmelites to its opening on 24 August that year. A censor who examined her book in 1575 believed she had founded a dozen monasteries in the previous thirteen years. She died in 1582. The Church honours her as a saint, and the edition's title calls her St. Teresa of Jesus.

## The book

The *Life* is the story of her soul, "Written by Herself", as the title page says. In her Prologue she says that she had been "commanded" to describe her way of prayer, and she writes "this account of myself which my confessors command me to give". She addresses one confessor as "my father" again and again. According to the translator's preface, a Dominican friar, Pedro Ibañez, ordered her to put the story of her spiritual life in writing. She began in the monastery of the Incarnation and finished a first account in Toledo in June 1562. The preface supposes that this account ended at the thirty-first chapter, and that the later chapters were added when another Dominican told her to write the history of the new foundation.

The edition has forty chapters and a Prologue: childhood and struggle first, then her teaching on prayer from the eleventh chapter, then visions, the founding in Ávila, and four last chapters of further graces.

Her contemporaries were careful about it. A respected spiritual master, Juan of Ávila, read it in 1568 and wrote that its teaching on prayer was for the most part sound, and that it should not be put in everybody's hands. The Inquisition kept a copy for years, and Teresa never saw it again. A Dominican, Domingo Bañes, who examined it for the Inquisition, wrote in 1575 that he found nothing erroneous in its doctrine. He doubted the visions, but not her goodness. It was first printed after her death, in Salamanca in 1588, edited by Fray Luis de León. The original, in her own hand, is kept at the Escorial.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

The *Life* is written from the middle of things. Teresa is ill, tired, doubtful, short of time and opposed, and still going. She describes the days when she did not want to go in, the day something stopped her, the people who held out a hand, and a plan that everyone told her to drop. It is also unexpectedly funny: she laughs at herself, at her singing, at her excuses. For a walker who is bored on the third day or discouraged on the eighth, that is useful company.

## What is hard, and what we left out

She is very hard on herself, and calls herself wicked on page after page. Where it comes up, we say what she says about it herself: that a humility which torments is not the true kind. The visions and raptures are in the book, and we have left nearly all of them aside. The *Life* also holds a vision of hell and some frightening accounts of the devil, which we have not used. It speaks of Protestants in the language of a sixteenth-century Catholic, and we have left those places out. And it describes a way of life with fasts and penances that this book does not recommend to anyone.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is by David Lewis, who, according to the edition's introduction, was born on 12 November 1814 and died on 23 January 1895. His translation was first printed in 1870, with a second edition in 1888. We use the third edition, enlarged, published in London by Thomas Baker and in New York by Benziger Brothers in 1904. It carries a re-imprimatur, the Church's permission to print, dated 27 September 1904 and signed Franciscus, Archbishop of Westminster. Its introduction and notes are by Fr. Benedict Zimmerman, a Carmelite, who says the translation was made from Don Vicente de la Fuente's Spanish edition of 1861 and 1862. Lewis and Teresa are long dead, so the translation is in the public domain. It is on Project Gutenberg as ebook number 8120, and every quotation here was checked against that text, word for word.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. We kept the old spellings and forms, such as *Thou* and *Thy*. The edition numbers its chapters with Roman numerals, as the attributions do, and numbers its paragraphs; we left out the paragraph numbers and the footnote numbers. Where a passage skips a stretch, a mark like this shows it: …. We quote only Teresa's own words, never the introduction, the translator's preface, the annals or the notes, though some facts on this page come from them.

The choice and order of the passages, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and original. Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
