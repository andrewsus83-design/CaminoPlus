# About the book and its author

## The woman

Teresa was born in Ávila on 28 March 1515. Her father was Don Alfonso Sanchez de Cepeda and her mother Doña Beatriz Davila y Ahumada. The translator's preface says that in the world, and even after she became a nun, she was known as Doña Teresa Sanchez Cepeda Davila y Ahumada. She entered the Carmelite monastery of the Incarnation in Ávila as a young woman. The edition's own notes cannot agree on the year, and put it anywhere from 1533 to 1536, so we do not give one.

In 1562 she helped begin a small, very poor and strict house in Ávila, the monastery of St. Joseph, and the translator's preface dates the start of the Reform of the Carmelites to its opening, on 24 August that year. A censor who examined her book in 1575 believed she had founded a dozen monasteries in the last thirteen years. She died in 1582. The Church honours her as a saint, and the edition's title calls her St. Teresa of Jesus.

## The book

The *Life* is the story of her soul, told by herself. The title page says "Written by Herself", and she tells us in her Prologue why she wrote it. She had been "commanded", she says, to describe her way of prayer, and she writes "this account of myself which my confessors command me to give". She addresses one confessor as "my father" again and again. According to the translator's preface, a Dominican friar, Pedro Ibañez, ordered her to put the story of her spiritual life in writing. She began in the monastery of the Incarnation and finished a first account in Toledo in June 1562. The same preface thinks that the first account went no further than the thirty-first chapter, and that the later chapters were added when another Dominican told her to write the history of the new foundation. It supposes that the work took her nearly six years.

The book is divided into forty chapters, plus the Prologue, and it runs like this. First her childhood and the years of struggle, to the ninth chapter. Then, from the eleventh, her teaching on prayer, with the image of the garden. Then visions and the graces she believed she had received. Then the founding of the house in Ávila, and four last chapters of further graces.

It was not an easy book to have written. Her contemporaries were careful about it. Juan of Ávila, a respected spiritual master, read it in 1568 and wrote that its teaching on prayer was for the most part sound, and that it was not a book to put in everybody's hands. The Inquisition held a copy for years, and Teresa never saw it again. A Dominican, Domingo Bañes, who examined it for the Inquisition, wrote in 1575 that he found nothing in it erroneous in doctrine. He had doubts about the visions, though not about her goodness, and he said it should be shown only to learned and prudent people. It was first printed after her death, in Salamanca in 1588, edited by Fray Luis de León. The original, in her own handwriting, is kept at the Escorial.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

The *Life* is written from the middle of things. Teresa is ill, tired, doubtful, short of time, opposed, and still going. She describes the days when she did not want to go in, the day something stopped her, the people who held out a hand, and the long years of a plan that everyone told her to drop. It is also unexpectedly funny. She laughs at herself, at her singing, at her excuses. For a walker who is bored on the third day, or discouraged on the eighth, that company is useful, and she speaks to you in plain sentences.

## What is hard, and what we left out

She is very hard on herself, and calls herself wicked on page after page. We have taken that in stride and, where it comes up, we say what she herself says about it: that a humility which torments is not the true kind. The visions and raptures are in the book, and we have left nearly all of them aside. The *Life* also holds a vision of hell and some terrifying accounts of the devil, which we have not used. It speaks of Protestants in the language of a sixteenth-century Catholic, and we have left those places out. And it describes a way of life with fasts and penances that this book does not recommend to anyone.

We took ten short passages from nine of her forty chapters. Where she tells of a vision, the reflection says so and does not argue.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is by David Lewis, who, according to the edition's introduction, was born on 12 November 1814 and died on 23 January 1895. His translation was first printed in 1870, with a second edition in 1888. The book we use is the third edition, enlarged, published in London by Thomas Baker and in New York by Benziger Brothers in 1904. It carries a re-imprimatur, the Church's permission to print, dated 27 September 1904 and signed Franciscus, Archbishop of Westminster. The new introduction and extra notes are by Fr. Benedict Zimmerman, a Carmelite. His introduction says the translation was made from the Spanish edition of Don Vicente de la Fuente (Madrid, 1861 and 1862), and that this printing is a reprint of Lewis's second edition. Lewis and Teresa are long dead, so the translation is in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 8120, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. We kept the old spellings and the old forms, such as *Thou* and *Thy*. The edition numbers her chapters with Roman numerals, as the attributions do, and divides each chapter into numbered paragraphs. We left out the paragraph numbers and the small footnote numbers. Where a passage skips a stretch, a mark like this shows it: …. We quote only Teresa's own words, never the introduction, the translator's preface, the annals or the notes, though some of the facts on this page are drawn from them.

The choice and order of the ten passages, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original. Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
