# Day 2. Watching the hour-glass

*What do you do on the days when you do not want to go in?*

## The passage

> When I was in the midst of the pleasures of the world, the remembrance of what I owed to God made me sad; and when I was praying to God, my worldly affections disturbed me. This is so painful a struggle, that I know not how I could have borne it for a month, let alone for so many years. … And certainly the violence with which Satan assailed me was so irresistible, or my evil habits were so strong, that I did not betake myself to prayer; and the sadness I felt on entering the oratory was so great, that it required all the courage I had to force myself in. They say of me that my courage is not slight, and it is known that God has given me a courage beyond that of a woman; but I have made a bad use of it. In the end, our Lord came to my help; and then, when I had done this violence to myself, I found greater peace and joy than I sometimes had when I had a desire to pray.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter VIII (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

There is a moment, early on a hard morning, when you take the phone out to see how far you have come. Four kilometres. It felt like forty minutes of nothing. You put it away, and six minutes later you take it out again. Soon the walking is mostly the looking, and the road is something to be got through. You may know that kind of hour. Perhaps it comes after a bad night, or on a flat stretch where nothing changes, or on the third day, when the novelty has worn off and the end is not yet near.

Teresa knew a version of it that lasted years. She was a nun, and a person of prayer by habit, and she says she spent nearly twenty years "on this stormy sea, falling and rising, but rising to no good purpose". Her chapter has a picture of it that every walker can read. For some years, she says, she was "more occupied with the wish to see the end of the time I had appointed for myself to spend in prayer, and in watching the hour-glass", than with anything else. She sat there to be finished.

The passage names the cause of the trouble, and it is a divided heart. When she was among the pleasures of the world, she remembered what she owed to God and was sad. When she knelt to pray, her worldly affections disturbed her. So she had neither. Later in the chapter she puts it in a short line: she had "no sweetness in God, and no pleasure in the world". Whatever you make of her particular pleasures, the shape is familiar. It is the state of being at the desk and wanting the beach, and then on the beach thinking about the desk. She says she does not know how she bore it for a month, "let alone for so many years".

In the lines just before, she says what she means by courage. She knows of nothing "which requires greater courage than plotting treason against the King, knowing that He knows it, and yet never withdrawing from His presence". It is a stern picture, and the point of it is the staying. She came to prayer with her divided heart in her hands and did not hide it or leave.

What interests her is what she did. The sadness she felt on entering the oratory was so great, she says, that it took all her courage "to force myself in". That is the whole story of the courage. It is not a heroic act. She walked through a door, again, on a day when she did not want to. She adds, with a flash of dry humour, that people say her courage is "not slight", and that she has made a bad use of it.

Then comes the line that gives the passage its weight. When she had done this violence to herself, she says, she "found greater peace and joy than I sometimes had when I had a desire to pray". The days of forcing herself in were worth more than the days of wanting to. A word on "violence": as Teresa uses it here, it means a push against her own reluctance, a hand on the door. There is no fasting in this passage, and no hurting of the body, and it would be a misreading to look for any.

Be honest about the limits. She gives her trouble two names within one sentence: the violence with which Satan assailed her, or her own strong habits. A modern reader may add others: guilt, tiredness, a mind pulled two ways. Her body was often ill in those years, as her sixth and seventh chapters say plainly. And not every reluctance deserves to be overruled. A sharp pain in a knee, or dizziness in the heat, is the body telling you to stop, and listening to it is not weakness. The courage she means is for the ordinary dread of beginning, the one that sits at the door, not for the warning that comes from the foot.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Probably something small: go in. Do the first ten minutes before you decide anything. The hour-glass will go on running whether you watch it or not, and a watched one is slow. You may find that the stretch you most dreaded is the one you can hardly remember at the end of the day. Teresa claims no more than that, and no less. She says she did not go in because she wanted to. She went in, and afterwards she was glad.

## For the walk

- Is this reluctance telling me something I should heed, or is it only asking me to start?
- For the first ten minutes, put the phone away and keep no count of distance or time. Walk by feel. At the end of the ten, ask yourself whether you still want to stop. If the answer is yes because something hurts, stop, and look after it.

## At the end of the day

Somewhere in the day you probably looked at the clock more than you would like to admit. It is the oldest habit of the road. How far now, how long to the next village, how many hours until the bed. There is nothing wicked in it. The only question worth asking is what it did to you. Did it help you pace yourself, or did it empty the hour of everything but the number?

Think back to the doors you did not want to go through. The first step out of the room this morning. The long afternoon stretch. The café you were tempted to sit in until the day was over. At each of them you either went in or did not. Teresa makes no claim that going in always feels good. She says only that the peace she found when she forced herself was sometimes greater than the peace she found on the days she wished to pray. Whether that held for you today, only you know. If you did go in, on a day with no glow in it at all, give that a quiet nod. It is the kind of thing that does not get a stamp in any book.

A day measured in kilometres looks different when you remember it as a day. The numbers are in the app. The day itself is somewhere else: a smell, a dog behind a gate, a bench, a stranger's good morning. Try to find three things from today that have no number attached. They are usually still there when the figures have faded.

An honest word about tiredness. In the evening the body argues for skipping everything, and a small practice can feel like one demand too many. Keep this one small. A reflection that takes two minutes and is done lying down is a reflection. If you have to choose, choose sleep over thought, and let the questions wait for the morning.

Dread of tomorrow's start can arrive in the evening too. The sadness Teresa describes came before she reached the door of the oratory. If the first hour of tomorrow is already heavy in your mind, notice that it is the picture of the hour that is heavy. The hour itself is ten minutes of walking.

There is one more thing in Teresa's account that deserves a quiet nod. She says she could not have borne the split life for a month, and she bore it for years. Nobody should ask that of themselves, and this book does not. It asks for the next ten minutes. If a longer heaviness has settled on you, one that does not lift with rest and a good meal, it is worth saying so to a doctor or to someone who knows you. On the road in Spain, 112 is the number to call in an emergency.

- When did I most want today to be over, and what was I doing then?
- What did I begin today that I did not feel like beginning?
- Was there a moment when stopping was the wise thing, and did I listen?

## A short prayer

*Be with me at the doors I do not want to enter. Give me the first ten minutes of tomorrow, and I will find the rest. Where I am pulled two ways, make me whole enough to walk. Where I am only tired, let rest be answer enough.*
