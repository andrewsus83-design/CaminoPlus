# Day 5. A hand held out

*Who is walking beside you, and have you said thank you?*

## The passage

> It is a great evil for a soul to be alone in the midst of such great dangers; it seems to me that if I had had any one with whom I could have spoken of all this, it might have helped me not to fall. I might, at least, have been ashamed before him--and yet I was not ashamed before God. … For this reason, I would advise those who give themselves to prayer, particularly at first, to form friendships; and converse familiarly, with others who are doing the same thing. It is a matter of the last importance, even if it lead only to helping one another by prayer: how much more, seeing that it has led to much greater gain! … I had many friends to help me to fall; but as to rising again, I was so much left to myself, that I wonder now I was not always on the ground. I praise God for His mercy; for it was He only Who stretched out His hand to me.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter VII (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Some of the best minutes of a walking day happen off the road. A table where somebody passes the bread. A bench where two strangers compare feet. On a busy way you may share a long table every evening. On a quiet one, a nod across a counter may be the whole of the company. Either is enough to change an afternoon, and it is odd how little it takes.

Teresa has spent six chapters on her youth, her illness and her first falls, and in the seventh she stops to say what would have helped. It is not a technique. It is a person. She says it is a great evil for a soul to be alone in the midst of such great dangers, and that if she had had anyone to talk to, it might have kept her from falling. Then she adds a reason that is not at all grand: "I might, at least, have been ashamed before him". She says she was not ashamed before God. She was, though, perfectly capable of being ashamed before a friend.

There is honesty in that. Part of what a friend is for, she suggests, is that you cannot easily pretend in front of them. A secret kept in your own head can be argued into almost any shape. Said aloud to someone who has walked a similar way, it shrinks or it hardens, and either is useful.

Her advice follows at once: "form friendships", and talk freely with others who are doing the same thing, even if it comes to no more than helping one another by prayer. You can see how practical it is. She does not say find a master or join a society. She says find someone on the same road, and speak plainly.

Then comes the line that makes her smile ruefully. She had "many friends to help me to fall", she says, but for rising again she was "so much left to myself" that she wonders now she was not always on the ground. It is a good warning against a lazy reading of her own advice. Not every friendly person is a friend in her sense. Earlier in this same chapter she tells of long conversations with visitors that wasted her years, and says she was fond of the person who distracted her most. The question is which way a friend leans you when they lean.

She is also careful to point to the people who did help. In her third chapter it is a nun who slept in the same room and talked to her about God: "How well she used to speak of God!" It is an uncle, whose books she read aloud to him, though she did not like them much at the time. It is a Dominican confessor, who "took upon himself the task of helping my soul in earnest". None of them is a hero. They do the dull, reliable thing of paying attention.

The passage ends in thanks. "I praise God for His mercy", she says, "for it was He only Who stretched out His hand to me". You may read the hand in two ways. If you pray, it is God's. If you do not, it may be the hand of the person who happened to be there, or a stranger on a road who passed on the right word. Teresa goes one way, and you may be glad of her gratitude without going with her. What stays true either way is that the hand is held out, and you take it.

Gratitude is almost a habit of breathing in her book. After many hard admissions she writes "May He be blessed for ever!" and goes on. In her last chapter she records a worry she had: whether it showed a lack of detachment to enjoy the company of the people who looked after her soul. The reply she wrote down is this: "It is not a virtue in a sick man to abstain from thanking and loving the physician who seems to restore him to health when he is in danger of death." She says those words were spoken to her in prayer. However you take that, the sentence is sound by itself. It is not noble to refuse help, or to be too proud to say thank you.

Three small things follow from all this. Accept the help that is offered, the shared plaster, the direction, the seat. Thank people exactly, by naming what they did. And, if you are carrying something heavy, let one person know. This does not forbid solitude. Teresa loved it and sought it, as she says more than once. It says only that the heaviest things are not meant to be carried alone.

## For the walk

- Who has helped me up this week, and have I thanked them in words?
- Say one thank-you today that is exact. Not "thanks for everything" but "thank you for holding the gate", or "thank you for the water". If you meet no one to say it to, write the message and send it tonight. If it is hard to find the words, begin with the plain fact of what the person did and let the rest follow.

## At the end of the day

Think through the day for hands. Not the large ones. The woman at the counter who found you a seat. The walker who pointed out the arrow you had missed. The friend at the other end of a message who answered at once. A good deal of kindness goes by in a day without being counted, because most of it is small enough to fit into a single sentence.

Then think about the other kind of company. Some talk at the table lifts everyone, and some talk pulls everyone down. You may have joined in a round of complaints about the road, or the beds, or other walkers, and felt lighter for a moment and heavier afterwards. Teresa's phrase about friends who help you fall was not a joke only at her own expense. It asks you to notice which evening conversations were good for you and which only used up the time. A simple test is whether you stood a little straighter when it ended.

The hand goes both ways. In the same seventh chapter Teresa is the one who helps. She had given her father books about prayer and talked with him about it, and he made such progress, she says, that she praised God for it. When he fell ill, she says, "I went to nurse him", though she was ill herself. A friend is not only someone who steadies you. Think whether you steadied anyone today: held a door, shared the last of the water, listened a minute longer than you wanted to.

Being a good companion at the end of a long day can be one more job. You are allowed to go to bed early. You are allowed to say that you are going to rest, and to leave the table kindly. A friend who sleeps well is better company tomorrow than one who stays up to be nice.

If you walk alone, tonight may feel quiet in a way that stings. Quiet is not an accusation. A friend need not be in the room. A message, a memory, or a person you plan to see at the end can be company enough for an evening. Teresa's whole book is a long letter to a man who was somewhere else, whom she addresses as "my father", the way one speaks to a priest. Company can travel by page.

Before you sleep, try a small practice that asks for no writing. Name three people from today, not three blessings, three people, and say thank you to each of them in your head, one sentence apiece. If it is only the person who sold you the bread, that is fine. It is easy to thank a day in general, and gratitude in general is thin. Gratitude with a face is not.

- Who held out a hand to me today, and did I see it at the time?
- Whose company left me lighter, and whose left me heavier?
- Whom could I thank tomorrow morning, by name and for something exact?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the hands held out to me today, the ones I saw and the ones I missed. Give me good company on the road, and the sense to be good company. Where I am alone tonight, let me be alone gently. Where someone needs a word from me in the morning, wake me in time.*
