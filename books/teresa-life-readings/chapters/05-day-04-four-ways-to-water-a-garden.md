# Day 4. Four ways to water a garden

*Which kind of water is this morning asking for?*

## The passage

> A beginner must look upon himself as making a garden, wherein our Lord may take His delight, but in a soil unfruitful, and abounding in weeds. His Majesty roots up the weeds, and has to plant good herbs. … Let us now see how this garden is to be watered, that we may understand what we have to do: how much trouble it will cost us, whether the gain be greater than the trouble, or how long a time it will take us. It seems to me that the garden may be watered in four ways: by water taken out of a well, which is very laborious; or with water raised by means of an engine and buckets, drawn by a windlass--I have drawn it this way sometimes--it is a less troublesome way than the first, and gives more water; or by a stream or brook, whereby the garden is watered in a much better way--for the soil is more thoroughly saturated, and there is no necessity to water it so often, and the labour of the gardener is much less; or by showers of rain, when our Lord Himself waters it, without labour on our part--and this way is incomparably better than all the others of which I have spoken.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XI (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Where the summers are dry, whoever keeps a garden thinks about water before anything else. You may pass a vegetable plot on the way, a stone trough beside it and a hose coiled on a nail, and see at once that the plot is a daily argument with the sky. There are only so many ways to get water to a plant. They differ mostly in how much of the work is yours.

Teresa chose a garden for her best-known picture of prayer. She says she is not sure where she found it: "I have either read or heard of this comparison; but as my memory is bad, I know not where". She also apologises for using it at all, "because I am a woman, and write simply what I have been commanded". She is writing for a confessor, on his orders, and she expects him to smile: "your reverence will be amused when you see my stupidity". The humour is part of the picture. She is not laying down doctrine. She is reaching for whatever will make the matter plain.

Here are her four waters, plainly. First, water drawn from a well by hand, which is laborious. Second, water raised by a wheel and buckets turned with a windlass, less work and more water. She adds that she has "drawn it this way sometimes", so she knew what it was to turn that handle. Third, a stream or brook running to the garden, which soaks the soil and leaves the gardener far less to do. Fourth, rain, "when our Lord Himself waters it, without labour on our part".

She then applies them to what she calls four degrees of prayer. The well is where everyone begins, when you must work to gather yourself, and the effort is great because the mind has been used to wandering. The three that follow are, in her account, increasingly a matter of gift. And here the book changes. The chapters on the three higher waters describe states of absorption that she says came to her unasked, and her language grows strange. She calls one of them "a glorious folly, a heavenly madness". Again and again she says she cannot find the words. We have not quoted those chapters, and we do not pretend to explain them.

It is worth knowing how she herself handled them. She warns against trying to force them: "it is of great moment not to raise our spirit ourselves, if our Lord does not raise it for us". Her own contemporaries examined such experiences with care, and she wanted them examined. Nothing in this book asks you to look for anything of that kind. The most ordinary part of the picture is the most useful: the well, and the person turning the handle.

Then there is a reading of our own, which is ours and not hers. On a walking day you may know all four waters. The well is the plain work: the first hour, the pack, the blister you tape. The wheel is whatever eases the work, a good rhythm, a pole, the right socks. The stream is the stretch where the walking walks you, and you hardly notice the kilometres go. The rain is what you could not arrange: a view at a bend, a kindness from a stranger, a morning that is simply good. You cannot order any of the last two. You can keep the garden ready for them.

On an ordinary day this comes to three things. First, be honest about which water you have. A day at the well is not a failed day. Teresa says "the labour is greatest at first", which is true of the first day of a walk and the first week of anything. Second, do not despise the work. She tells the gardener to do what lies in his power and leave the rest, even when the well is dry. We will come back to that on Day 9. Third, be ready for rain without waiting for it. A gardener who sits indoors until it rains has a poor garden. A gardener who is out in the beds when it comes has a good one.

If you do not pray, try reading "garden" as attention, the thing you tend, and "Lord" as whatever you call the part of life that you did not make. The picture still holds. The work is yours. The weather is not.

## For the walk

- Which of the four waters is this morning: the well, the wheel, the stream or the rain?
- Wherever you see water on the way, a trough, a ditch, a tap, stop to drink and name which of the four your day is at the moment. Do not try to change it. Only name it, and go on. If there is no water in sight, drink from your own bottle. The exercise is the naming, not the place.

## At the end of the day

Which water was today? Most days are a mixture. The morning at the well, the afternoon on the wheel, ten minutes of stream, and perhaps one unasked drop of rain. It helps to be exact about it, because the mind likes to flatten a day into good or bad. A day with one drop of rain is not a bad day, and a day at the well all the way is not a wasted one.

Teresa has a sentence to hold on to when the comparisons begin. One of the men who had told her to write, she says, had been raised in four months to a greater height than she had reached in seventeen years. Her advice, to herself and to anyone: "Close the eyes of your imagination, and do not ask why He gives devotion to this person in so short a time, and none to me after so many years." Walkers know that temptation well. Someone passes you who seems to float, or you hear of a person who walked twice your distance and cried at the end. It is easy to feel behind. The road does not keep a league table, and neither does the garden.

She also says something about weeds. The beginner, in her picture, is making a garden in poor soil full of them, and she says that His Majesty "roots up the weeds". The gardener's task is to water. It is a relief to read it that way. You are not required to pull up every fault in yourself by nightfall. Some of it is not yours to do. Keep the buckets going and let the rest be.

Her garden is also not for the gardener alone. It is made "wherein our Lord may take His delight", not for the one with the bucket. If you do not pray, you can still ask who your day's attention might refresh. A greeting at a gate is water for someone else's garden, and it costs a breath.

Notice, too, how little she asks for. She hopes the water will be given "drop by drop", and she says that when she drew "but one drop of water out of this blessed well", she considered it a mercy. A garden lives on drops. It may help to count yours: one real conversation, ten quiet minutes, a meal eaten slowly with nothing else going on. None of them will look like much in a diary. They are what the roots take up.

A long day at the well takes it out of the arms. If today was all work and no rain, let tonight be rest. A dry well is not a fault in the gardener. It may only be a hot week.

- Which water did I draw today, and which came without my drawing it?
- Whom did I compare myself with, and what did it cost me?
- What did I try to do today that was not mine to do?

## A short prayer

*Let me do the watering that is mine. Let me not demand the rain. Where the well was dry, keep the roots alive. Where something came that I did not earn, let me say thank you and not hold it too tight. Water the garden tonight while I sleep.*
