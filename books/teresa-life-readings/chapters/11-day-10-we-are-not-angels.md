# Day 10. We are not angels

*What goes home with you, and what does an ordinary Tuesday need?*

## The passage

> We are not angels, for we have a body; to seek to make ourselves angels while we are on the earth, and so much on the earth as I was, is an act of folly. In general, our thoughts must have something to rest on, though the soul may go forth out of itself now and then, or it may be very often so full of God as to be in need of no created thing by the help of which it may recollect itself. But this is not so common a case; for when we have many things to do, when we are persecuted and in trouble, when we cannot have much rest, and when we have our seasons of dryness, Christ is our best Friend; for we regard Him as Man, and behold Him faint and in trouble, and He is our Companion; and when we shall have accustomed ourselves in this way, it is very easy to find Him near us, although there will be occasions from time to time when we can do neither the one nor the other.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XXII (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

There comes a day, a few days after a walk, when the rucksack is emptied onto a bedroom floor. The shirts smell of the road. The boots stand by the door looking puzzled. The kettle is the same kettle, and the post has piled up. If your walk ends at Sahagún, where this road joins the Camino Francés, or goes on beyond it, or if you are only reading this at your own table, the question is the same. Where does all of it go now?

Teresa's answer, in this passage, is one of the plainest things in her book. "We are not angels, for we have a body." She says it in a chapter that answers certain books on prayer, written by people she respects. Those books advised the advanced to put away every image and every human support. Teresa says that this seems right enough now and then, but that to withdraw altogether is more than she can endure. She also tells how she tried the advice herself and came to think it a mistake for her, adding at once, "still, it may be that it is I who am deluded". To try to make ourselves angels while we are on the earth, "and so much on the earth as I was", she says, is an act of folly. It is a small joke at her own expense, from a woman who was often ill, often tired and always busy.

"In general, our thoughts must have something to rest on." Think of what that means to someone who has been walking. A bench. A shell on a rucksack. A route you know in your legs. A cup of coffee at a certain bar. These are not distractions from the pilgrimage. They are the things that held it up. And coming home is a matter of finding the new benches: the chair by the window, the walk to the shops, the usual hour.

Then comes her list, which is as good a short account of an ordinary life as any: "when we have many things to do, when we are persecuted and in trouble, when we cannot have much rest, and when we have our seasons of dryness". Busy, pressed, short of sleep, and dry. That is a Tuesday. It is also, in a way, the whole of this book, since you have met every item on the list. In such times, she says, "Christ is our best Friend", for we see Him as a man, "faint and in trouble", and He is our companion. This is a Christian sentence and says so. Its sense for a reader outside the faith is still clear: you do not have to be at your best to be accompanied. A companion who knows what it is to be tired and in trouble is better than a lofty one.

There is a gentle rebuke in the lines around the passage, aimed at anyone who comes back from a pilgrimage wanting the holy life without the washing-up. In the same chapter she warns against "seeking to be Mary before it has laboured with Martha". The reference is to the two sisters in the Gospel, one of whom sat and listened while the other got on with the meal. Teresa's point is that the work of the house is not the enemy of the inner life. It is where you begin. You may not be ready for the quiet, and the dishes may be the proper start.

She is also honest about how much of her own life was spent in the middle of things. Near the end of the book she writes, "I have so little time", and says that writing the account was a trouble. She hopes it will prove "a blessed trouble" if it does some good. The book you have been reading was made in the gaps of a busy life, by someone with little time to spare. That seems the right note on which to end a pilgrimage: not arrival at a summit, but a return to work with something added.

And the book does not end on a summit. In the last chapter she records words that were said to her, she says, to console her: that "in this life we could not continue in the same state". She says she was told to expect it to be like this: "At one time I should be fervent, at another not; now disquieted, and again at peace, and tempted; but I must hope in Him, and fear not." You can hear these as spoken from outside or as the conclusion of a shrewd woman looking back over her life. In either case they remove a pressure. You may not be able to hold on to the best day of the walk, and nothing says you should. What you can keep is smaller and more durable.

What goes home with you is mostly small and bodily. Unpack. Wash the clothes. Eat at a table. Call the people who have wondered. Then pick one habit from the road that will fit into a kitchen, and put it where you will see it.

## For the walk

- What from this road will I carry into an ordinary Tuesday?
- Choose one habit from the walk that fits in a house: ten quiet minutes at the start of the day, a short walk after supper, one exact thank-you every evening. Say it aloud once. Then do it tomorrow at the usual hour, for a week, before deciding anything else. If it is hard to keep, make it smaller until it is easy, and then keep that.

## At the end of the day

If this is your last evening on the road, or the first at home, it can feel odd. The days had a shape, and the shape is about to go. The body that learned to rise early and walk will wake up tomorrow with nowhere to go. You may find the quiet louder than you expected.

Look back over the ten days of this book, as you would look back down a road from a rise. A house with a shelf of books and stones that fell down. An hour-glass watched. A picture in a corner. Four waters. A hand held out. A question put to a child. A lawsuit and a handing back. A small bird that rests. A well with no water. And now a body, and a kettle. There is a road in it. It is not a road to a mountain top. It is the road of a person who kept on going in, who kept on asking, who set her hand to the work in front of her, and who wrote it down in the spare hours.

Soon someone will ask, "How was it?" and the easy answer is "fine". Have one true sentence ready instead, small enough to say at a door or over a coffee. Not the whole walk. One picture: the plains in the evening, a stranger's bread, the day the well was dry. Teresa's whole book is that kind of act, a person telling a few trusted listeners what happened and what it was like, as plainly as she could. A true sentence is a gift to the one who asked.

Teresa does not promise that the best of it can be kept. The words she records are that we do not stay in one state. That can be read as a weariness, or as a mercy. After a good walk, the mind wants to hold the good. A gentler wish is to carry its manner, the early start, the patience, the habit of saying thank you, and let the feeling come and go as the weather does.

A last small thing for the evening is to mark the end of the walk, however quietly. Wash the boots. Put the shell, the credencial or the ticket where you will find it. Say thank you, to somebody or to nobody, for ten days of a road and a book. Endings need a little ceremony, even a kitchen-sized one, or they leave the day open.

The body often catches up after the walk is over. It may be heavier for a day or two than it was on the road. Rest is not a betrayal of the pilgrimage. It is a part of it. And if a long low mood follows the end of the walk, one that does not lift after some days of ordinary life, talk to a doctor or someone you trust.

- What do I most want to carry from this road into an ordinary week?
- Who needs to hear that I am home, or that I am grateful?
- What small, plain thing will I do tomorrow at the usual hour?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road, for its long middle and its dry afternoons. Let me carry home something small enough to fit in a kitchen. Give me the plain tasks, and the grace to do them well. Be with me on ordinary days as you were on the road. Let me rest, and let me begin again.*
