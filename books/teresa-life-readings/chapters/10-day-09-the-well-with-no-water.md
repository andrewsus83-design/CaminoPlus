# Day 9. The well with no water

*What do you do on the days when nothing comes?*

## The passage

> God grant there may be water in it! That, however, does not depend on us; we are drawing it, and doing what we can towards watering the flowers. So good is God, that when, for reasons known to His Majesty--perhaps for our greater good--it is His will the well should be dry, He Himself preserves the flowers without water--we, like good gardeners, doing what lies in our power--and makes our virtues grow. … What, then, will he do here who sees that, for many days, he is conscious only of aridity, disgust, dislike, and so great an unwillingness to go to the well for water, that he would give it up altogether, if he did not remember that he has to please and serve the Lord of the garden; if he did not trust that his service was not in vain, and did not hope for some gain by a labour so great as that of lowering the bucket into the well so often, and drawing it up without water in it? It will happen that he is often unable to move his arms for that purpose, or to have one good thought: working with the understanding is drawing water out of the well.
>
> — Teresa of Ávila, *The Life of St. Teresa of Jesus*, Chapter XI (tr. David Lewis, 1904 edition)

## Reflection

Some days have no weather in them and no view. The sky is the colour of the road. The legs are working, the feet are fine, and still nothing arrives: no pleasure, no sense of meaning, only the next step and the one after. If you are on the flat stages that the app's route notes describe, such a day may come in the second week, when the plain stretches out and the early excitement has gone. Nobody has done anything wrong. The well is simply dry.

You met Teresa's well on Day 4. This is the same garden on a worse morning. Here the gardener comes to the well and lowers the bucket and draws it up empty, and does it again. She says what this feels like in a list that reads like a doctor's note: "aridity, disgust, dislike, and so great an unwillingness to go to the well for water". He would give the whole thing up, she says, if he did not remember that he serves the Lord of the garden, and trust that his service is not in vain. And on some of those days, she says, he cannot even move his arms for the work, or have one good thought.

The line before it is the one that frees the gardener: "God grant there may be water in it! That, however, does not depend on us." We are drawing it, and doing what we can. When God, for reasons of His own, wills the well to be dry, He "preserves the flowers without water". That is a generous idea. The flowers do not die on the day the water fails. The roots have more in them than the gardener can see.

Her advice in the next lines is a little sweeter than most of us can swallow. She asks the gardener to take comfort in the trust that has been shown him. It is a great favour, she says, to labour in the garden of so great an Emperor, to be trusted with the work when no reward shows. Some readers will take to that and others will not. If it does not help you, leave it. Her second thought is plainer: it is not the feeling that counts. She says "the love of God does not consist in tears, nor in this sweetness and tenderness which we for the most part desire". A person who is "determined not to care much, neither to rejoice nor to be greatly afflicted, whether sweetness and tenderness fail it, or our Lord grants them", she says, "has already travelled a great part of the road". You can read that as trust in a different form: the walk counts whether or not it feels like anything.

Then comes the part many modern readers will be glad of. A few paragraphs on, she takes up the question of why the dry times come, and she is practical to a degree that surprises. She says dryness "comes most frequently from bodily indisposition". The changes of the seasons and the humours of the body, she writes, can compel the soul "without fault of its own, not to do what it would, but rather to suffer in every way", and "this poor prisoner of a soul shares in the miseries of the body". Her advice follows. Do not force it: the more we force the soul on these occasions, "the greater the mischief, and the longer it lasts". "The poor soul must not be stifled." Make a change in the hour of prayer. "Take care, then, of the body." Rest, eat, and let there be recreations, such as conversation, or "going out into the fields". The soul, she says, should not be dragged but carried gently.

You are already on that prescription. A walker goes out into the fields every day. So when the day is dry, her advice is first of all domestic. Eat properly. Drink. Take your shoes off at the stop. Sit with someone, or sit alone, and look at the fields. Do not hold yourself responsible for a mood that may only be a hot week, a poor night, a blister, or an empty stomach. And do not turn dryness into a verdict on the whole walk.

Be honest about the limit. If the dryness goes on for weeks and takes the colour out of everything, that is a matter to take to a doctor or to someone you trust, and not to a garden image. Teresa herself says more than once that a person who suffers in this way needs a guide with experience, and she sought one out again and again. A long flat spell is a reason to ask someone, not to endure alone.

The whole of it, for a dry day, is to lower the bucket anyway. It is to do the plain, near, next thing and not demand that it taste of anything, and to look after the body as though it were a friend who is also tired.

## For the walk

- What is one small, plain thing I can do for the garden today, whether or not I feel anything?
- Lower the bucket: do one small thing as well as you can without asking how it feels. Fill the bottle. Tape the heel. Say good morning and mean it. Then, at the next stop, take her advice and look after the body: sit down properly, eat something, drink, and let your eyes rest on a field.

## At the end of the day

Dry days are the ones that do not make it into stories. Nothing happened. You walked, you ate, you slept, and the whole day went by with the sound turned down. If today was one of them, give it a nod. It is the commonest kind of day on any long road, and the kind that does the most to make the rest possible.

Teresa has an image for it in another chapter that may help. She writes about a person who has often travelled a certain road and who, "though it be night and dark", knows by that experience "where he may stumble", because "he has seen the place by day". On dry days the feet are doing what the days of light have taught them. You did not feel like it, and you walked, because you had walked before. Your habits carried you, and that is not a lesser thing than inspiration. It is what inspiration leaves behind.

She has one more suggestion for the days when the main thing will not come. There are "exterior works, as of charity", she says, that can be done instead, though at times the soul will not manage even those. In plain terms: when you cannot feel, you can still do. Buy the bread for the person beside you. Hold the door. Carry something for someone who is struggling. A kindness done on a dry day has the same weight as one done on a good day, and it often has the added benefit of getting you out of your own head.

One thing to be careful about is the verdict you pass tonight. A tired mind in a quiet room is a harsh judge. It may tell you that the whole walk is pointless, or that you are a person who cannot feel anything, or that the thing you hoped for from this road is not coming. Teresa's reply, in effect, is that you are being asked to judge a day on one bucket. The garden is bigger than the bucket, and the weather will move.

Dryness is also lonelier when it is hidden. A sentence said aloud, "this was a flat day", to whoever is near, often loosens it. You do not need a remedy from them. You may find that they know the feeling too. And if nobody is near, say it to the page, to a message, or to the dark. It stops being a secret, and that is half of its weight.

Dry days and tired days are close cousins, and a good night's sleep clears some of the dryness for no reason anyone can name. If you can, go to bed a little earlier than usual. If you cannot sleep, do not argue with the dark. Lie on your back and listen to the building for a minute.

- What did I do today without feeling it, and what did that teach my feet?
- What did my body ask for today, and did I give it?
- Whom could I tell, simply, that today was dry?

## A short prayer

*Be with me when the well is dry. Let me lower the bucket anyway, and not ask for a drop. Teach me to look after this body as a friend. Keep the flowers alive tonight without water, and let me not be ashamed of a plain, empty day.*
