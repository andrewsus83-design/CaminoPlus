# Down from the pass: Somport to Jaca

## How the days are counted

Stage counts and lengths vary by source. The day lengths in this book are rounded from a Spanish consumer foundation's stage guide, which cuts the road into six days from Somport. They were checked against Navarre's own brochure for the two Navarre days, and against Aragon's tourism brochure for the villages and the distance to the border. Other guides differ by a kilometre or two, and where they differ by more the book says so. The six days add up to ~164 km, and the catalog says ~160 km. The guide's practical notes, such as where to eat, are several years old, so treat them as a question for the app and for the people you meet.

## The character of this day

One long day takes you from the top of the road to its first city. You start at ~1,640 m, at a frontier pass of ski slopes and old customs buildings, and finish in Jaca at ~820 m, after a drop of about 800 m in ~31 km. The Aragonese government's planning study, which describes this stretch section by section, calls the valley V-shaped, cut deep by the river, with pale limestone peaks above dark conifers. The V opens into a U near Villanúa, the hills soften, and by Jaca there are fields.

### Day 1: Somport to Jaca (~31 km)

At the pass, Aragon's brochure says, a plain monolith at the roadside announces the Camino through Aragon, and the path starts on the left of the N-330. The brochure puts about 858 km between you and Compostela. Steps lead down beside the river. Within about 800 m, a stage guide says, you pass the excavated ruins of the hospital of Santa Cristina. Navarre's leaflet says it was founded in the eleventh century and is mentioned in the Codex Calixtinus. It is a pile of stones now, but stop for a minute. Spanish Wikipedia says that pilgrims who had climbed the pass, often tired, hungry and ill, found rest at the hospital, and that the Codex says they were given lodging for three days.

Below it the path passes Candanchú, crosses the N-330 several times around the Rioseta ravine, and runs among the old concrete bunkers built to defend the frontier. The Aragonese study says the path crosses the road three times at the bends of the ravine. Take each crossing slowly. At ~7 km you reach Canfranc Estación. Its great international station opened in 1928, and trains stopped running to France in 1970. Just beyond the village the path passes the mouth of the road tunnel that opened in 2003, and the brochure asks pilgrims to take great care there and for the next ~500 m.

Canfranc itself comes at ~11 km, a long single street with a medieval bridge. Villanúa is at ~16 km, where the valley widens. The Aragonese study says that the holm-oak woods near it are the most northerly in Europe. After Villanúa the path follows the river or droving tracks beside the road to Castiello de Jaca, at ~23 km.

The last ~8 km follow the river and the edge of the N-330 to the hermitage of San Cristóbal, ~28 km from the pass. After that comes a steep little climb into Jaca. The stage guide estimates the whole day at 8 hours 30 minutes.

If it is too much, the day can be cut at Canfranc or Villanúa, and the places are named above. If you can, start early, take the descent gently, and keep an eye on the weather over the pass. The Confraternity of St James says Somport is snow-covered in winter.

## Jaca

Jaca deserves an evening and, if you can spare it, a day. Aragon's brochure calls it the first city of the Camino, the capital of the kingdom of Aragon and the seat of its bishops. Wikipedia says it was the first capital until 1096. The cathedral was begun in the second half of the eleventh century on the initiative of King Sancho Ramírez. The citadel, a perfect pentagon with moat and bastions, was ordered by Philip II in about 1592. The brochure says you can walk the perimeter along the glacis.

Aragon's brochure advises drawing cash here, and the Aragonese study finds fewer services beyond Puente la Reina de Jaca, so Jaca is the place to stock up.
