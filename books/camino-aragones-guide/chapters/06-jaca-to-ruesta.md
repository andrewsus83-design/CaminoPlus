# The Canal de Berdún: Jaca to Ruesta

## The character of these days

Two days take you across a wide, open valley that the river has cut through grey marl and sandstone. Flat-topped hills carry the villages, wheat fills the terraces, and the white peaks of the Pyrenees stay on the northern horizon. Peña Oroel, which Aragon's brochure puts at ~1,770 m, stays behind you for a long time. The Confraternity of St James describes the Canal de Berdún as broad arable farmland anticipating the meseta, with dramatic rocky "moonscapes".

After Puente la Reina de Jaca the Aragonese government's planning study finds the least altered landscape of the Aragonese road, with a calm rhythm over the wide valley and no road noise. It also calls the stretch more remote, with fewer services, and the stage guide lists few places to stop. Carry what you need for each day, as the chapter on sleeping and eating explains.

### Day 2: Jaca to Arrés (~25 km)

The way leaves Jaca beside the N-240 and keeps close to it for most of the day. A stage guide says it follows broad droving tracks, the *cabañeras*, along the verge, and it warns that they are hard on the feet and that you must cross the main road in several places with great care. Santa Cilia comes at ~15 km, a village laid out in a neat grid, and the guide calls it the official intermediate stop of the day. From there the path runs beside the N-240 again to Puente la Reina de Jaca, at ~21 km.

This is not the Puente la Reina where you will finish. It is a small village in Huesca, named for its stone bridge over the Aragón, and the two are ~110 km apart. In 2018 the Aragonese study found the main road here very hard for a walker to cross, with no pedestrian-crossing signs, so cross with care. Pilgrims have built little towers of pebbles on the river bank here, and Aragon's brochure admires them.

Then comes the climb: ~3 km, of which the first is the hardest, on a path up the flank of the mountain to Arrés, which stands at ~700 m on a rocky crest. Aragon's brochure says it was a royal town and that the ruins of its castle look out over the Canal de Berdún. It is a good place to end the day.

**Detours.** Two famous places lie off this road above Jaca. Santa Cruz de la Serós is ~4 km up a side road from the N-240, and has a Romanesque church of Santa María, which Navarre's leaflet calls the only remnant of the royal women's monastery that King Ramiro I founded. San Juan de la Peña, above it, is the old monastery built under a great rock, with a Romanesque cloister and the burial place of the early kings of Aragon, according to Aragon's brochure, which puts it ~22 km from Jaca. The Aragonese study says a branch of the GR 65.3 climbs there by Atarés and returns by Santa Cruz. The stage guide calls the climb from the road terribly demanding, and says the visit can also be made by taxi. On foot by the monastery, it counts ~29 km from Jaca to Santa Cilia, against ~15 km direct.

### Day 3: Arrés to Ruesta (~29 km)

This is a long day, ~29 km, and the stage guide says that only its last four kilometres have shade. Start at first light. The path leaves Arrés on a stony track through box bushes, then follows farm tracks across the plain, past grey eroded gullies the guide calls badlands, and crosses two ravines on walkways.

Mianos is a village on a hill to one side. Artieda, at ~18 km, stands on a hilltop at ~650 m, and the guide lists it as the only place to eat on the day, so leave Arrés with food and water. It is also the end of a shorter day: the guide suggests ~18 km as an easier alternative. Beyond Artieda, ~11 km remain with no village. The way follows the A-1601 for a while, climbs through an oak wood that offers the first real shade, and passes the ruined Romanesque chapel of San Juan before coming down to Ruesta.

Ruesta is the village the reservoir emptied. The guide says the waters of the Aragón, Esca and Regal were dammed in 1959 and flooded ~2,400 hectares, and that three villages were abandoned. Wikipedia says it was inaugurated in 1960, so sources differ by a year. Aragon's brochure says that in 1988 the Ebro water authority gave Ruesta over to be brought back to life, and it describes a ruined castle with two slender towers. It is a quiet place, and a strange one to walk through.

**The reservoir and the path.** The Aragonese study of 2018 says that two stretches of the road here, ~2.8 km before Ruesta and ~500 m after it, would be affected by works to enlarge the Yesa reservoir, and that the Ebro water authority proposed a new line. *Check before you go* which line is open, and ask where you sleep.
