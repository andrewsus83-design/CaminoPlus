# The Way in brief

## Where it runs

The road starts at the Somport pass, which Aragon's tourism brochure puts at ~1,640 m (Wikipedia gives ~1,632 m). From there it drops down the valley of the River Aragón to Jaca, and the river stays beside you as far as Sangüesa. Between Jaca and Navarre the road crosses the Canal de Berdún, runs along the south shore of the Yesa reservoir, leaves Aragon at a marker stone beyond Undués de Lerda, and climbs and falls through the hills of Navarre by Sangüesa and Monreal. It ends where it joins the French Way, at Puente la Reina.

How long is it? The catalog says ~160 km. The sources give a range. The Aragonese brochure says that about 858 km to Santiago remain at the pass, and the Navarre tourist board gives ~56 km from Sangüesa to Puente la Reina. Stage tables add up to ~164 km. The Confraternity of St James writes ~170 km for the Spanish part of the Arles road, and a Spanish Wikipedia table of distances to Santiago (870 at Somport, 692 at Puente la Reina) implies ~178 km along the route it lists, which takes in the detours by Santa Cruz de la Serós and Lumbier. Take ~160 to ~170 km as the honest range.

## One road, several names

Pilgrims call it the Camino Aragonés. The Aragonese government's pages do not: they call it the Camino Francés as it passes through Aragon, and Aragon's tourism brochure adds that many people call it the Aragonese Way. Navarre calls it the second branch of the Camino Francés. In France the same road is the Via Tolosana, the Chemin d'Arles, waymarked as the GR 653. In Spain the Confraternity says it is waymarked with yellow arrows and also with red and white bars as the GR 65.3. The Camino de Santiago entered UNESCO's World Heritage list in 1993, and the Aragonese government takes the route of that inscription as the reference for its stretch of the road.

## Why the road is here

Somport is the old *Summus Portus*, the Latin "highest pass". The twelfth-century pilgrims' guide in the Codex Calixtinus describes four roads that start in France and, according to Wikipedia, converge at Puente la Reina. The Confraternity says that the southern one, from Arles by Toulouse, is among them. Pilgrims on it crossed here and not at Roncesvalles, and Wikipedia says that Somport was arguably the most popular Pyrenean pass for pilgrims until the Roncesvalles road became safer in the twelfth century. Aragon's brochure adds that the first crossing into Aragon used a Roman road over the Palo pass and down the Echo valley, and that the Somport route was promoted later.

A hospital at the pass looked after them. The Navarre tourist board's leaflet says the hospital of Santa Cristina was founded in the eleventh century and is already mentioned in the Codex, and Spanish Wikipedia adds that the Codex ranks it among the three great hospitals of Christendom, with Jerusalem and the Great St Bernard pass. Today it is a ruin you walk past in the first hour.

The kings of Aragon shaped the rest. Jaca, Aragon's brochure says, was the first city of the Camino, the capital of the kingdom and the seat of its bishops, and Wikipedia says it was the first capital until 1096. The cathedral was begun in the second half of the eleventh century on the initiative of King Sancho Ramírez, says the Navarre leaflet. The monastery of San Juan de la Peña, off the road above Jaca, became their royal pantheon.

## What it feels like

It is a road of changing landscape in six days. At the start you are in a narrow, steep valley of white limestone peaks and dark conifers. The Aragonese government's planning study describes the valley opening out near Villanúa, with Mediterranean crops and warmer air appearing after Jaca. Then comes the Canal de Berdún, which the Confraternity describes as broad arable farmland anticipating the meseta, with rocky "moonscapes" and the huge reservoir. In Navarre the land turns to wheat, vines, pinewoods and wind turbines, and a day of hills brings you down to Eunate and the bridge.

The twentieth century is on this road too. The great international station at Canfranc opened in 1928, and the rail link with France ended in 1970. A road tunnel opened under the pass in 2003. The Yesa reservoir, built around 1960, drowned farmland and emptied villages, and you will walk through one of them.

It is also a road with few walkers on it. Spanish Wikipedia says that few pilgrims use it and, citing the Pilgrim's Office, reports that 404 of the pilgrims who received a Compostela in 2018 began at Somport and 116 at Jaca. The Confraternity says the road has relatively few pilgrims, though their number is growing.
