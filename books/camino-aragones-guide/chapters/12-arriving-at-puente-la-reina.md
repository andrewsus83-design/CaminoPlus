# Arriving at Puente la Reina

## Where the roads meet

Navarre's tourist board says that the Aragonese road joins the one that enters Spain by Luzaide/Valcarlos at Puente la Reina, and Aragon's brochure agrees. The Confraternity of St James says the roads join at Obanos, just before Puente la Reina, and so does the stage guide, which puts Obanos ~3 km short of the town. Wikipedia's French Way table notes that the Aragonese road joins just before Puente la Reina. The guide adds that there has been much speculation about which of the two it is, and that official signs lead up to Obanos, though some pilgrims turn left onto the NA-6064 road and go straight to Puente la Reina.

For a walker it comes to a few kilometres either way, and it hardly matters. The catalog ends the road at Puente la Reina, and so does this book.

## The bridge

Puente la Reina, Gares in Basque, takes its name from its bridge. Navarre's leaflet calls it an eleventh-century Romanesque bridge ~110 m long, with seven round arches, the easternmost buried, and a protected Site of Cultural Interest. Wikipedia counts six arches. The stage guide says it was built for the passage of pilgrims at the order of a queen. Walk across it slowly. After the quiet of the road from Somport, the main street, with its churches, makes a gentle end to the day.

## The Compostela

The Compostela is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules. The Office owns the rules, and they can change. This book says what the Office says, and sends you to the Office in any doubt.

- The Compostela is for those who come to the tomb of the Apostle for Christian purposes, and the Office says that in modern times it is limited to those who come for religious and/or spiritual reasons and follow the routes of the Way of St James on foot, by bicycle or on horseback.
- It asks for at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred kilometres by bicycle, shown by the credencial, stamped along the route.
- On foot or on horseback, that means at least a hundred continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago that the Office recognises. The final stage must include at least the last one leading into the cathedral.
- You may walk the Way in stages, if they follow in chronological and geographical order. A pilgrim who walks only the minimum distance must get the credencial stamped at the start and at the end of each stage, with the date.
- A pilgrim who has already walked part of a recognised route outside Spain needs no less than seventy kilometres in Spain.
- The Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special and exceptional cases.

The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.

## What this means for this walk

From Puente la Reina to Santiago the sources give ~690 to ~700 km. That is far more than the last hundred kilometres the Office asks for. The walk from Somport to Puente la Reina, ~160 km, is not the last hundred kilometres of any road to Santiago, and it does not end at the cathedral. The Camino goes on along the French Way.

The Office's pages read for this book do not mention the Aragonese Way, or list the routes it recognises. So this book does not say what the Office would record for a walk that stops at Puente la Reina, and it does not say whether any other start counts. If you walked the Via Tolosana in France before Somport, the Office's rule of seventy kilometres in Spain is written for pilgrims who walked a recognised route outside Spain, and how it applies to you is a question for the Office. Ask it before you go, in any doubtful case, whether you are stopping here or going on.

## Then what?

You have three choices. You can stop, and enjoy having walked a whole road. You can rest a day and walk on along the French Way. *The French Way*, the first book in this series, covers the rest of the road to Santiago. Or you can go home and come back for the rest another year. Pamplona is ~24 km behind you on the French Way, and Estella ~22 km ahead.

Whatever the Compostela means to you, it is a record of a road you walked, not a measure of it.
