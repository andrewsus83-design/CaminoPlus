# Before you go

## The credencial

The *credencial* is the pilgrim's passport. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago describes the official model as a cardboard card of sixteen concertina-folded pages. It says you can ask for one in person at the Office or at institutions the cathedral authorises: parish churches, associations of Friends of the Way of St James, pilgrim hostels, confraternities and others. It also describes a digital credential, an app for Android and iOS.

The Office says the credencial gives you no rights as such, and names two practical uses: access to the hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way, and proof when you apply for the Compostela. Stamps, the *sellos*, are normally collected where you sleep, but also in parish churches, monasteries, town halls and other places. Get your credencial before you leave home, or at a place that issues it, and do not assume you can buy one at the pass.

## Getting there

Somport is a frontier pass in the mountains, and you will probably spend the night before in Canfranc or Jaca. Public transport changes, so this book gives no times. The Confraternity of St James mentions train and bus services over the Pyrenees between Oloron-Sainte-Marie and Jaca, and Wikipedia says the station at Canfranc has been served by trains from Zaragoza. Check what runs now, and ask in Jaca or Canfranc about the last stretch up to the pass.

Plan to arrive the day before, so that you can start Day 1 early. It is one of the two longest days of the road.

## Getting home

If you finish at Puente la Reina and go on along the French Way, you need no plan. If you stop there, Pamplona is ~24 km back along the French Way. The Navarre tourist board lists a bus line from Pamplona to Sangüesa, which helps if you stop at Sangüesa instead. Do not plan a tight connection for the day you finish.

## Training

You will walk six days of ~22 to ~31 km, and the first day is almost all downhill. Walk with your loaded pack for a few weeks beforehand, on as many hills as you can find, and practise descents, which punish the knees more than climbs. Break in your shoes. If you have a health worry, ask your doctor before you book.

## What to pack

Navarre's tourism pages say the lighter your luggage the better, and that is the first rule.

- **Layers for a cold start.** You begin at ~1,640 m, where the morning can be cold even in summer. Pack a warm layer, a waterproof, a hat and gloves.
- **Sun and water.** A wide hat, sun cream and bottles that hold about two litres between them. The shadeless days are long.
- **Food for the gaps.** The chapters on the days say where the villages are far apart.
- **A head torch,** for dark rooms and early starts.
- **Poles,** if you use them, for the long descent of Day 1.

## Shoes and feet

A Spanish consumer foundation's stage guide warns that the broad droving tracks, the *cabañeras*, of the second day can wear the soles of the feet and bring on sore tendons, especially in heavy, high boots. It recommends light trail shoes. Whatever you choose, walk in them before you start, carry blister dressings, and treat a hot spot the moment you feel it.

## Money

Take cash. Aragon's tourism brochure, a 2009 print, advises drawing money in Jaca because cash machines are scarce until Sangüesa, and some small places may not take cards, so ask. Carry some small notes and coins.

## Language and signs

Spanish will do everywhere. In Navarre the official signs also carry Basque forms: Sangüesa is also Zangoza, Monreal is also Elo, and Puente la Reina is also Gares.

The road is waymarked with the yellow arrow and, as the GR 65.3, with red and white bars. The stage guide says that in Aragon wooden posts mark the path, and that in Navarre these give way to stone markers with a shell and a yellow arrow. The Aragonese planning study found in 2018 that some stretches, for example the northern line by Berdún beyond Puente la Reina de Jaca, had no signs at all. Keep a map saved on your phone, because signal can fail in the valleys.

## Health

If you fall ill, the Navarre tourism pages point to the European Health Insurance Card for visitors who may need care. Check what your own cover includes. In an emergency, call **112**.
