# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

The CaminoPlus catalog rates the Camino Aragonés 3, the same as the French Way and the Winter Way. A Spanish consumer foundation's stage guide rates each of the six days "medium". Neither tells you where it bites, so here it is.

- **Day 1 is long and goes downhill.** It is ~31 km, with a drop of about 800 m from the pass to Jaca. Fit walkers finish it, and their knees remember it.
- **The middle is exposed.** On the day to Ruesta, that guide says, only the last four kilometres give shade. The Confraternity of St James calls summer on the Arles road too hot.
- **There are long gaps between villages.** On the way to Ruesta the only place to eat that the guide lists is Artieda, and on the way to Monreal it finds no village between Rocaforte and Izco for ~15 km. The Aragonese government's planning study calls the stretch after Puente la Reina de Jaca more remote, with fewer services. The app's own route notes agree that services are sparse in places.
- **Some of it runs beside a main road.** Between Somport and Berdún the path keeps close to the N-330 and the N-240, and the Aragonese study describes its many crossings.
- **The last day starts hard.** Navarre's brochure warns that the first kilometres out of Monreal are demanding.

No day is rated above medium. What the road asks for is steady walking, a full bottle and a plan for lunch.

## Who it suits

- **Someone with a week.** Six days is a common plan, seven is possible, and the road is short enough to walk in one go.
- **Someone who wants quiet.** Few pilgrims use it, and the Confraternity calls the Arles road solitary but rewarding.
- **Someone who comes from France.** This is the Spanish end of the road from Arles.
- **Someone who means to reach Santiago by the French Way** but would rather begin in the high Pyrenees than at Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port. The roads join at Puente la Reina, and the Camino goes on from there.
- **Someone who likes old stones.** On or near the road are a ruined pilgrims' hospital, the cathedral at Jaca, Romanesque churches at Santa Cruz de la Serós, Sangüesa and Eunate, and an abandoned village.
- **A self-sufficient walker.** You will carry water and food, follow a map, and be comfortable with silence.

## Who might choose another road

- **If you want company,** the French Way is the most popular of all the routes, according to Wikipedia, and you will meet many more people there.
- **If you want the Compostela from this walk alone,** this is not the road. The chapter on arriving explains why, and sends you to the Pilgrim's Office.
- **If you walk slowly and need short days,** the chapter on other ways to split the road offers a seven-day plan, but there are only a few places to stop.
- **If you plan to walk in winter,** the Confraternity advises against crossing the pass in snow or mist, unless you are an experienced mountain walker.
- **If you dislike walking beside traffic,** you will meet the N-330 and N-240 more than you may like.

## How it compares

In general terms, the French Way is longer and busier, and this road is shorter and quieter. It starts higher and ends by feeding into the other. Leaving Obanos, just before Puente la Reina, the consumer foundation's guide notes a line of pilgrims much longer than usual. You will feel the change.
