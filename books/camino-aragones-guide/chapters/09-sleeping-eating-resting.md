# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office names access to "hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way" as the first use of the credencial, and says that hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

The Confraternity of St James says it is now possible to walk almost the whole Arles road using only pilgrim accommodation, gîtes in France and albergues in Spain. On this road the places are small and few, and their seasons vary. A stage guide says that in one village volunteer hosts usually run the albergue from March to October, and that the communal dinner there is paid for by donations. Ask before you rely on a bed, and call ahead on the days with few villages.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and pack your rucksack in the hall in the morning.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. If you are the one who snores, apologise at breakfast.
- Keep your valuables close.

**Bedbugs** can be a risk in any shared bed. Check the seams of the mattress, keep your pack and clothes off the bed, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is a reason to look before you lie down, not a reason to avoid hostels.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating.

## Eating

Many places on a Camino offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what is on it, and what it costs, before you sit down. On this road the villages are small, and a bar or shop may be closed when you arrive, so do not leave the evening meal to chance.

## Food and water in the gaps

This is the part of the road that needs planning. The stage guide lists Artieda as the only place to eat on the day from Arrés to Ruesta, and it counts ~15 km with no village, and only a couple of springs, on the way from Rocaforte to Izco. The Aragonese study says the stretch after Puente la Reina de Jaca has fewer services, and Aragon's brochure advises drawing cash in Jaca.

- **Fill your bottles at the last village,** and carry more than you think you need on a shadeless day.
- **Carry lunch,** and something for the evening on the two sparse days.
- **Ask the evening before.** A small village's bar or shop may be shut when you pass, so ask your host what to expect on the next day.
- **Do not drink from a stream or trough** unless you know it is meant for people.

## Resting

A day of rest is part of the walk. This road offers two natural places for one.

- **Jaca,** after the long first day. See the chapter on Somport to Jaca.
- **Sangüesa,** before the long hills of Navarre.

Listen to your body before you listen to your plan. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop and rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
