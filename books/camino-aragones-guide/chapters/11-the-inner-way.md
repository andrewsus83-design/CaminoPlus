# The inner Way

## Every reason is a reason

People walk the Camino Aragonés for faith, for sport, for grief, for curiosity, for a rest from work, or for no reason they can name yet. This book does not rank them. The Pilgrim's Office describes the credencial as meant for those who walk with a Christian sentiment, "even if it is only with an attitude of search". You are welcome on the road whatever you bring to it, and the road does not ask you to explain.

## A road of beginnings

This road begins at a frontier, on a high pass, with a ruined hospital a few minutes below it. The Codex Calixtinus, says Spanish Wikipedia, ranks that hospital among the three great hospitals of Christendom, a place where tired pilgrims who had climbed the pass were taken in. You walk down past the stones where they rested, and the whole first day is a long descent.

You may find that this changes the walking. A climb makes you earn each hour, and a descent may leave the head clear while the knees do the work. Later, on the open plain of the Canal de Berdún, there is little to look at but sky and field. A road with few walkers on it leaves plenty of room for whatever comes up.

## Ruins and returns

Ruesta is an empty village that has been partly brought back, and Navarre's leaflet says the Romanesque hermitage of Santiago near it was begun at the start of the eleventh century. The reservoir took the fields that fed these villages. Aragon's brochure says that when the water falls at the end of summer, the ruins of the drowned village rise out of it. You do not have to make anything of this. But a road that passes places lost and places remade may suit someone who is also in the middle of something.

## Where the roads meet

At the end, the road joins another. A stage guide says that in Obanos pilgrims from Roncesvalles and pilgrims from Somport come together, and it describes a pilgrim-shaped monument on the way in to Puente la Reina inscribed, in translation: "And from here all the Ways to Santiago become one." After several quiet days you will walk into the company of a great many people. Some may find it a gift and some a shock. Either is allowed. You can keep walking alone among them.

If you walk in grief, the road holds you without asking. If you walk with doubt, walk with it. If you walk for sport, walk well, and notice who is walking beside you. If you walk with others, leave a day of the road to yourself.

## Questions for the walk

Take one a day, and let it ride in your pocket. You do not have to answer.

- What are you carrying that you could put down?
- What did you come here to leave behind, and what did you come to find?
- What would you do with this hour if you were not in a hurry?
- Who do you wish were walking beside you, and what would you say to them?
- What in your life has been emptied out, and what could be returned to it?
- When did you last feel that you had enough?

## A companion for the road

The Library has a small book made to go with this road, *Songs for the Climb*: eight days with the Psalms of Ascent, a morning reading and an evening reflection for each. You can read it before you go, on the road or at home.

## When the road is hard

Some days the descent is only a descent and the plain is only a plain. On those days the kindest thing is a short plan: the next village, the next marker. Walk to that, and ask for help when you need it.
