# Body, weather and safety

This chapter is plain on purpose. Nothing in it is a reason to stay at home. It is what a careful walker does on a road with a mountain start and long gaps.

## Feet and legs

Your feet decide how the walk goes. Stop at the first hot spot and dress it. Change your socks at lunch if they are damp, rest for a few minutes each hour, and eat and drink before you are hungry or thirsty.

Day 1 is a descent of about 800 m in ~31 km. Shorten your stride, use poles if you have them, and rest your knees at Canfranc or Villanúa if they complain. A stage guide says the hard droving tracks of the second day can wear the soles and bring on sore tendons. If you feel a sharp or growing pain in a knee, shin or foot, stop. A day lost to rest is better than a week lost to an injury.

## Mountain weather

The pass is where mountain weather matters most. The Confraternity of St James says the Somport is snow-covered in winter, that winter journeys over it in snow or mist are not advised except for experienced mountain walkers, and that Aragón can be chilly and windy even in summer.

- **Look at the forecast the evening before and again in the morning.** AEMET, the Spanish state weather service, publishes forecasts. Look at the high ground, not only the town you are leaving.
- **Start early,** so that a slow morning does not leave you on the mountain at dusk.
- **Wet clothes cool you quickly.** Carry a waterproof, a warm layer and a hat, and keep one dry layer in a bag.
- **In mist, stay on the marked way.** If you cannot see the next marker, stop, and go back to the last one you saw.
- **If the day looks wrong,** change the plan. Wait in Canfranc, or stay a night in Jaca.

## Heat and shade

The Confraternity calls summer on the Arles road too hot, and the stage guide says that on the day from Arrés to Ruesta only the last four kilometres have shade. On a hot day, start at first light, wear a hat, put on sun cream, drink often and rest in the middle of the day. A headache, dizziness or nausea in the heat are signs to stop, get into the shade, and drink. If they do not pass, ask for help. In dry summers, ask locally about closed paths and fires.

## Roads

Much of the first two days runs beside trunk roads. The Aragonese study shows the path crossing the N-330 several times near Canfranc, and Aragon's brochure asks pilgrims to take great care at the mouth of the road tunnel, and for the next ~500 m beyond it. The study found the main road at Puente la Reina de Jaca very hard for walkers to cross in 2018, and the stage guide says to cross the N-240 with the greatest caution.

The Policía Foral de Navarra, in its 2026 advice leaflet for pilgrims, says that on roads you should walk on the left, facing the traffic, using the shoulder if there is one. Keep to a single file in a group. At night or in poor light, use lights or reflective gear. Better still, do not walk on a road in the dark or in thick mist.

## The reservoir

The Aragonese study of 2018 says that works to enlarge the Yesa reservoir would affect two stretches of the path near Artieda and Ruesta, and that the Ebro water authority proposed a new line. *Check before you go*, and ask where you sleep.

## Water and food

The gaps between villages are the main practical risk of this road: the day from Arrés to Ruesta, and the way from Rocaforte to Izco. Leave with full bottles and food for the whole stretch, and tell your host where you are going.

## Staying safe on your own

Tell someone where you plan to sleep each night, and message them when you arrive. Keep your phone charged, carry your map saved on it, and know the name of the last village you passed. Signal can fail in the valleys.

## When to stop

Stop, and get help, for chest pain, a fever, severe dizziness, a fall with a blow to the head, a swollen joint, or anything that frightens you. Stop too if you are cold and cannot warm up, if you are lost in mist, or if you have run out of water. A doctor is the right person for a worry, and a plan can always change. The Way will still be there.

## Emergencies

**112** is the European emergency number and it works in Spain. Say where you are as exactly as you can: the village, the day, the last landmark you passed.
