# Into Navarre: Ruesta to Puente la Reina

## The character of these days

Three days take you out of Aragon and across the hills of Navarre to the meeting of the roads. The first is a climb over a ridge above the reservoir and a descent to Sangüesa. The second crosses high farmland between two valleys, with few villages. The third is the longest, with a hard start. The Navarre tourist board describes the country as undulating, rich in grain, with windmills, wide pinewoods and wild gorges. It gives ~56 km from Sangüesa to Puente la Reina.

### Day 4: Ruesta to Sangüesa (~22 km)

Leaving Ruesta, the path crosses the Regal on a wooden walkway and passes a Romanesque hermitage of Santiago, abandoned, says the stage guide. Then it climbs for ~5 km on a forest track to a high point, with a view over the reservoir, the abandoned village of Tiermas and the ridge of Leyre. The guide warns of a sharp left turn, ~3 km from Ruesta, that is easy to miss.

Undués de Lerda, at ~11 km, is the last village in Aragon. Beyond it the path leaves the village and runs down a ridge. Aragon's brochure says a large marker stone shows the boundary between Aragon and Navarre, and the guide says that the signs change there: the wooden posts of Aragon give way to stone markers with a shell and a yellow arrow. From Undués to Sangüesa the brochure counts ~11.5 km, and the guide counts ~10.5 km, so the sources differ by a kilometre. The guide advises carrying enough water, above all in summer.

Navarre says the road enters its territory at Sangüesa. Its church of Santa María la Real, which Navarre calls an example of the passage from Romanesque to Gothic, has a doorway that the tourist board calls one of the high points of Spanish Romanesque art. Give it time. The old nucleus of Sangüesa is Rocaforte, across the river, says the guide, and King Sancho Ramírez gave it the charter of Jaca in the late eleventh century. The same law tied the two towns together.

### Day 5: Sangüesa to Monreal (~27 km)

There are two ways to Monreal, and Navarre's brochure describes both. The main way goes by Rocaforte and the Alto de Aibar. The brochure calls it harder but almost without tarmac, and the stage guide gives it ~27 km. It climbs, and the guide puts the highest point at ~770 m. The distances marked on the brochure's profile add up to ~25 km, so the figures vary by a couple of kilometres.

The climb out of Sangüesa to Rocaforte is steep, and the brochure says to carry water. The way crosses grain, vines and olives, passes under a wind farm, and reaches the Alto de Aibar. The brochure says that a dozen kilometres of valleys and pines lie between there and the next village, Izco, and the guide counts ~15 km with no village between Rocaforte and Izco and only a couple of springs. Carry food and water for the whole of it.

The second way goes by Liédena and the Foz de Lumbier, a gorge that a greenway follows along the old Irati railway. The guide calls it ~5 km longer, but gentler and through villages, and Navarre's brochure offers it to anyone who cannot face Rocaforte. Navarre's page describes the gorge as a natural reserve.

After Izco the path goes to Abínzano and Salinas de Ibargoiti, then through an oak wood to Monreal, at ~555 m. Navarre's brochure calls it a former hunting residence of the kings of Navarre, and says a Gothic bridge leads you in.

### Day 6: Monreal to Puente la Reina (~31 km)

Navarre's brochure says this day can be hard because of the ups and downs of the first kilometres, and the guide counts ~13 km of continual ups and downs on the slopes of the Alaiz range as far as Tiebas. There are fountains at Yárnoz, Otano and Guerendiáin. Tiebas has a ruined castle of the thirteenth century, once a home of the kings of Navarre.

After Tiebas the path follows the edge of the motorway and passes under it, through Muruarte de Reta and Olcoz, and past Enériz. Eunate comes at ~26 km. The octagonal Romanesque chapel stands alone in the fields, ringed by a portico of 33 arches. Navarre dates it to the second half of the twelfth century and says its Templar origins feed many legends. The guide says its purpose is still uncertain, and that scholars have suggested both a Templar work and a funeral chapel.

A kilometre or so after Eunate the path climbs to Obanos. There, in the Plaza de los Fueros, says the guide, pilgrims from Roncesvalles and pilgrims from Somport come together. Puente la Reina lies ~3 km further on. The chapter on arriving explains the question of where exactly the roads meet.
