# Body, weather and safety

## Feet

Look after your feet first. Take your shoes off at lunch and let your feet breathe. Change your socks. Treat a hot spot at once, before it is a blister. On a road with this much hard surface, cushioning matters, so spend money on good shoes and time on breaking them in.

## Heat

The summers can be hot. The record highs are ~38.6 °C at Porto airport and ~39.4 °C at Santiago airport, and the Confraternity of St James warns of heat with high humidity. Start early, carry more water than you think you need, wear a hat, and plan the hot hours for shade and rest. Day 3, ~34 km, is the day to think about it most. If you feel dizzy, sick or confused, stop in the shade, drink, and ask for help; if it does not pass, call 112.

## Rain

This is a wet road. Santiago gets ~1,500 to ~1,800 mm of rain a year, depending on the source, and the Confraternity says that in periods of low pressure the rain can be heavy and prolonged. A rain jacket and a cover for your pack are not optional, and a spare pair of dry socks in a plastic bag is the cheapest comfort on the road. Rain on a long day is also a reason to take a shorter one.

## Roads and paths

The Portuguese Way meets main roads. The Confraternity says it often joins them at the entrance and exit of towns and advises that, when using roads, you watch for traffic and use the indicated strip between the road and the drainage ditch. In Galicia the Xunta warns of the N-550 on Day 7 and Day 11 and says there are branches to the right that take you off the traffic. It describes the N-550 on Day 9 as a constant presence, with a new high-speed rail line that has altered the landscape, and asks you to watch the signs closely.

Where there is no path, walk facing the traffic, in single file, and never in the dark. Do not take a shortcut on a main road to save a kilometre.

Look at the arrows at every junction. Blue arrows, which the Confraternity says point back towards Porto, are not yours; yellow ones are. Around Barcelos and north of Tui you will also see the red and white marks of a long-distance footpath; they are a different route.

## Border and papers

Wikipedia says that Portugal and Spain are both signatories of the Schengen Treaty, so there are normally no formalities at the Minho. Carry your passport or identity card anyway, as you should in any country, and remember the clock changes.

## When to stop

Stop when something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, when you are dizzy, when the weather turns, or when the light is going. A short day costs you an afternoon; a bad decision can cost you the Camino. Tell someone your plan. The emergency number is 112, in Portugal and in Spain.

Before you go, check for diversions, closures and, in summer, heat and fire warnings. A route can change between this book's date and your walk.
