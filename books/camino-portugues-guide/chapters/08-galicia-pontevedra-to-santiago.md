# Galicia, from Pontevedra to Santiago: Days 9 to 11

## The character of these days

The last three days are the longest run of Galician walking: ~23 km, ~19 km and ~25 km. The road leaves Pontevedra over a medieval bridge, follows a thread of villages, stone crosses and small rivers to a spa town on the Umia, crosses the Ulla into the province of A Coruña, and climbs the last hills to the cathedral. The figures are the Xunta's, with the OpenStreetMap route as a cross-check, and they agree closely.

### Day 9: Pontevedra to Caldas de Reis (~23 km)

The Xunta gives ~23 km, rates the stage Medium and estimates ~5 h 45 min. The OpenStreetMap route is ~21 km and published guides say ~23 km, so plan for the longer figure.

You leave Pontevedra by the street called A Santiña, after crossing the Lérez on the Burgo Bridge. The way runs beside a large wetland, A Xunqueira de Alba, between the railway and the river Granda, and climbs to the church of Santa María de Alba. The Xunta says that Diego Gelmírez, the archbishop of Santiago, once stopped at Guxilde on his way from Braga to Compostela. The Pilgrim's Office credits him with the energetic promotion of the pilgrimage in the early twelfth century.

Then come woods, the leafy ones of Reirís and Lombo da Maceira, a small stone bridge into the municipality of Barro, and the village of Valbón among large stone houses. The Xunta singles out three stone crosses beside the way: one near a house, one with a carving of St James the Pilgrim on its shaft, looking north towards Compostela, and the solitary cross of Soutelo. It calls them a triad of sculptures made to mark crossroads.

After Valbón, the Xunta says, you need to watch the signs closely. The route meanders, meets other paths, and has the N-550 as a constant companion, and a new high-speed rail line has altered the landscape. Take care at junctions.

Caldas de Reis is a spa town. The Xunta says it is the Aquae Celenis of the Antonine Itinerary, a third-century record of Roman roads, and Wikipedia adds that the older name means "hot springs". The Umia and the Bermaña run through it. The road crosses a bridge over the Umia, which leads to the hot spring that has given the town its name since Roman times, and then a medieval bridge over the Bermaña. The chapel of San Roque marks the end of the stage.

### Day 10: Caldas de Reis to Padrón (~19 km)

The Xunta gives ~19 km, rates the stage Medium and estimates ~4 h 45 min. The OpenStreetMap route and published guides all fall within a kilometre of that, so on this day the figures agree.

The path runs through Casalderrique and Casal de Eirigo and enters the municipality of Valga. Mount Castelo follows, dense woods watered by the Valga river and dotted with old mills. The Xunta then lists Cimadevilla, the bridge over the Fontenlo and the villages of Cedelo and Condide, in Pontecesures. The Pino Manso viewpoint looks over the valley of the Ulla.

You cross the Ulla by a bridge that was originally Roman and is now much changed. The Xunta says this river is the boundary between the provinces of Pontevedra and A Coruña, so the end of the day is a provincial boundary as well as a river. On the far side is Padrón, which the Xunta calls the home of the Jacobean tradition. Its church of Santiago keeps the Pedrón, a Roman altar stone with a carved cross that, the Xunta says, is said to have been added by the disciples of St James.

The tradition has a story attached. Wikipedia says that, according to tradition, the disciples Theodore and Athanasius brought the body of St James from Jerusalem in a stone boat and moored it to a *pedrón*, a big stone, which gave the place its name. The Xunta tells the same story in its description of the sea and river route that follows the apostle's last journey: the body came by sea to the Ría de Arousa and up the Ulla to Padrón, the river harbour of the Roman city of Iria Flavia. It is a tradition, not a record. Whether or not you hold it, it explains why this small town matters.

Padrón is also known for its small green peppers, and Wikipedia quotes the Galician saying that some are hot and some are not. It says Padrón has been home to four important writers, among them the Romantic poet Rosalía de Castro, who lived from 1837 to 1885, and the Nobel laureate Camilo José Cela, who lived from 1916 to 2002.

### Day 11: Padrón to Santiago de Compostela (~25 km)

The last day is ~25 km by the Xunta, which rates it Low and estimates ~5 h. The OpenStreetMap route agrees at ~25 km. It is the longest day of the Galician stages, and it ends with a cathedral. Start early so that you arrive with time to spare and not with the light going.

You leave by Dolores Street and cross the Sar to Iria Flavia. The Xunta says it was once a Roman city and an episcopal see until the eleventh century. Beyond it is the foundation of the Nobel laureate Camilo José Cela, who is buried opposite. The way crosses the N-550, and again the Xunta says you can take the branch to the right to avoid the traffic.

The Sar valley follows, with its small villages, and then the sanctuary of A Escravitude, with views over the valleys of Padrón. The path goes on past O Faramello and over the hills to O Milladoiro. The Xunta says that the name is the *humilladoiro*, the place where pilgrims knelt, humbled, on first catching sight of the cathedral; and that here Compostela can already be breathed. Whether you see the towers from there depends on the weather and the day. Slow down anyway.

Then you drop to A Rocha Vella and cross the Sar by the Old Bridge. The route follows the river, and at a fork there are two clearly signed ways, which meet again at Rosalía de Castro Street. The old quarter is entered by the Porta Faxeira and O Franco Street. The Xunta says the traditional entrance to the cathedral for pilgrims on the Portuguese Way is through As Praterías Square, and Wikipedia says the cathedral's Praterías façade was built by Master Esteban in 1103.

Take the last hundred metres slowly.
