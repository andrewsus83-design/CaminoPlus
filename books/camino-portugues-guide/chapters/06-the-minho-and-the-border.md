# The Minho and the border: Days 4 and 5

## The character of these days

Two shorter days, with the border in the second. The first climbs over the Labruja hills and ends in the village of Rubiães; the second goes down to the Minho, passes a fortress town, crosses a river that is also a frontier, and ends in a Galician town with a cathedral. Between them you leave one country, one language and one clock behind.

The distances are measured along the OpenStreetMap route, as in the previous chapter, and compared with published guides.

### Day 4: Ponte de Lima to Rubiães (~18 km)

After the long day to Ponte de Lima, this is a short one, ~18 km along the OpenStreetMap route and in published guides alike. Do not be fooled by the figure. Wikipedia says that one of the most tiring parts of the inland Portuguese Way lies in the Labruja hills, in the municipality of Ponte de Lima, which are hard to cross. The route leaves the river valley by Arcozelo and Labruja and climbs.

Take it as a day of two halves. Start early, with water, and leave the afternoon free. A steady pace uphill is better than a quick start and a long rest. What goes up comes down, to the valley of the little river Coura.

Rubiães is a village, and your last night in Portugal on this plan. It has an old road to show you. Wikipedia describes the bridge of Rubiães as a Roman bridge over the Coura, built in the second century, on the Portuguese Way. The Xunta says that the Roman road Via XIX, which linked Braga with Astorga, ran through Ponte de Lima and Tui, and Rubiães lies between them. It is a good place to rest your feet.

### Day 5: Rubiães to Tui (~21 km)

This is the day you cross the border. The distance is ~21 km along the OpenStreetMap route, against ~19 km in one published plan that also ends in Tui. Published guides end the day at Valença, which is ~4 km before Tui, and begin the next from there: they put Rubiães to Valença at ~16 to ~17 km. The CaminoPlus stage list ends the day in Tui.

The way passes Cossourado, Fontoura and Arão, then comes to Valença. Wikipedia says that the town is walled, on the left bank of the Minho, and that its first walls were built in the thirteenth century and its present bastions in the seventeenth and eighteenth. Two polygons of walls stand on two small hills, and inside them, Wikipedia says, a Roman milestone marks the forty-second mile of the road from Braga to Tui. If you have time, walk in through the Porta do Sol, the Gate of the Sun, the main entrance. The view from the ramparts runs across the river to Galicia. Wikipedia says the town was populated by King Sancho I in the twelfth century and was called Contrasta, "village opposed to another", because Tui stood opposite.

Then the border. The Xunta says that the route enters Galicia by the International Bridge, which links Valença and Tui across the powerful Minho. Wikipedia says that the bridge carries a road and a railway and that both countries are in the Schengen area, so there are normally no formalities at the crossing. Stop in the middle: behind you is Portugal, and ahead of you is the Galician stretch of the road.

Remember the clock: Galicia is one hour ahead of Portugal.

Tui sits on a hill above the river. The Xunta calls it one of the seven capitals of the ancient Kingdom of Galicia, and says the town was declared an area of artistic and historical importance in 1967. The cathedral of Santa María, which Wikipedia dates from the eleventh to the thirteenth century, has Romanesque and Gothic parts; the Xunta says it presides over the medieval street plan. Peter González, the Dominican friar known as San Telmo and patron of sailors, died in Tui in 1246 and is buried in the cathedral, Wikipedia says. Near the entrance to the old town the Xunta notes a milestone showing ~115 km to Santiago; north of Tui the Confraternity says you will again see the red and white marks of a long-distance footpath beside the yellow arrows.

Tonight your credencial is about to carry the stamp of a second country.
