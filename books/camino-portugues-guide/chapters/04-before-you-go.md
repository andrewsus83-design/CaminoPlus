# Before you go

## The credencial

The *credencial* is your pilgrim's passport, and you need one before the first stamp. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says the official model is a cardboard card of sixteen concertina-folded pages, with boxes for the stamps. You can ask for it in person at the Office or at institutions the cathedral has authorised: parish churches, associations of friends of the Way, pilgrim hostels, confraternities. The Office lists where, by country, region and city, and it is worth looking before you leave home.

The Office gives the credencial two practical purposes: access to the hostels of the Way, and proof when you ask for the Compostela. It adds that the credencial "does not generate any rights". The stamps are normally collected where you sleep, and also in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. The Office describes a digital credential too, as a phone app. Ask the Office what it accepts, and carry the paper card in any case.

## Getting there

Porto has an international airport ~11 km northwest of the centre of the city, and Wikipedia says it has a station on line E of the Porto Metro. The Confraternity of St James adds that you can also fly into Lisbon, Vigo or Santiago and use public transport. Santiago airport is ~12 km east of the city.

The Confraternity notes a regular bus service between Santiago and Porto, of about four hours' journey, which makes it possible to fly into and out of the same city. Timetables change, so check the current ones when you plan your return.

## Choosing your start

Where you begin sets the length of your walk. On this book's figures:

- **From Porto:** eleven days, ~246 km, the whole road, beginning at the cathedral, the Sé.
- **From Ponte de Lima:** eight days, ~158 km, which keeps the border, the Spanish towns and the arrival, and skips the long Day 3.
- **From Tui:** six days, ~119 km, the Xunta's six Galician stages.

Add a rest day or two, and a day each to travel there and back, and the Porto start is about a fortnight. If you are choosing a start with the Compostela in mind, read the rule in the chapter on other ways to split the days first, and ask the Pilgrim's Office. No starting town in this book is a guarantee.

## Training

Nobody needs to be an athlete for this road. The best preparation is also the dullest: walk, in the shoes and with the pack you will carry, on a hard surface, for two or three days in a row, and build up to the length of your longest planned day. Eleven days of ~16 to ~34 km ask a lot of a body that has not walked far in months. A few weeks of regular walking will do more than any programme.

## Shoes and feet

Much of the road is minor roads and paved paths, with stretches of track and forest. Look for shoes with a cushioned sole and a little room in the toe box, because feet swell as the day goes on. Break them in before you leave. Two pairs of socks, changed at lunch, are a cheap defence against blisters. Treat a hot spot when you feel it, not when it has become a blister. If something hurts in a way that grows and does not fade, rest it, and see a doctor.

## Packing

Pack light, and then lighter. The essentials are plain: a rain jacket, because this is a green road; sun protection, a hat and a water bottle, because the summers can be hot; a small first-aid kit for feet; a head torch and earplugs for shared rooms; and something dry to sleep in. A phone with an offline map is a good tool and a poor single plan, so know your next town before the battery is low. Leave whatever you are unsure of at home.

## Money, language and the clock

Portugal and Spain both use the euro. Carry some coins and small notes for small purchases. Prices are not in this book; the people you meet will tell you what things cost.

Portuguese is spoken on the Portuguese side. In Galicia there are two official languages, Galician and Spanish, and you will meet Galician on signs and in place names such as O Porriño and A Escravitude. Pilgrims need little: *bom dia* ("good morning" in Portuguese), *obrigado* (a man says thank you) or *obrigada* (a woman does), and in Galicia *grazas* or *gracias*. Say them with a smile and you will be understood.

Remember the clock. Portugal keeps Western European Time, and Galicia Central European Time, one hour ahead. Your phone will change at the border, and your breakfast plan should change with it.

## Emergencies

The European emergency number is 112, in Portugal as in Spain. Keep it in your head, not just in your phone.
