# Porto and northern Portugal: Days 1 to 3

## The character of these days

The first three days take you out of a big city, through the countryside of northern Portugal, to one of the oldest towns in the country. The first day is the least romantic of the walk. The second has a Romanesque church and a legend about a rooster. The third is the longest day in the book.

A note on the figures. This book found no official stage table for the Portuguese side that it could read, so these distances are lengths measured along the OpenStreetMap route, as recorded in the CaminoPlus catalog notes, and compared with the figures of published guides. They agree to within ~2 km. Treat every one as a good guide, not as a measurement.

### Day 1: Porto to Vilarinho (~27 km)

You start at the Sé, the cathedral of Porto. Wikipedia says the present building was begun in the second half of the twelfth century and was worked on until the sixteenth. It has a Romanesque rose window above a Baroque porch, and it has an English footnote: Wikipedia records that King John I of Portugal married Philippa of Lancaster there in 1387. Ask before you leave the city where to get your first stamp.

Then the city lets go of you slowly. The Confraternity of St James says that the Central and Coastal routes alike spend their first day walking through the urban and industrial sprawl of Porto, and that is the honest summary. The way passes Moreira da Maia, which published guides use as the halfway point, then Vilar de Pinheiro and Aldeia Nova, and ends at Vilarinho, a hamlet in the municipality of Vila do Conde. Portugal has many places called Vilarinho; check that yours is the right one.

The distance is ~27 km along the OpenStreetMap route, and published guides give ~26 to ~27 km. It is a long first day on a hard surface, so go slowly and take your breaks early. If it is too much, know that one published plan, which counts twelve stages for the whole route, divides Porto to Barcelos into three days instead of two.

Watch the arrows. The Confraternity says yellow arrows are plentiful from the cathedral to the border, and that blue arrows, which sometimes run beside them, point back towards Porto: they mark the route from Santiago to Porto and on to Fátima. Follow the yellow. Somewhere in this stretch the Coastal Way parts from the Central route; Wikipedia puts the split in the countryside of Vila do Conde. That road is the subject of *The Coastal Way*, and here you stay with the arrows of the Central route.

### Day 2: Vilarinho to Barcelos (~27 km)

The way passes Arcos and then São Pedro de Rates, which Wikipedia calls a central place of the Portuguese Way and the place where pilgrims were most numerous. The Romanesque church of Rates has been a national monument since 1910. Wikipedia says its rebuilding began around 1100, when Henry, Count of Portugal, brought in Benedictine monks from France, and counts it among the oldest Romanesque churches in Portugal. According to legend the church honours Peter of Rates, a first bishop of Braga ordained by St James himself; the relics were moved to Braga cathedral in 1552. On the way to Rates, Wikipedia mentions the medieval Dom Zameiro bridge, rebuilt in 1185 on a Roman road.

From Rates the way goes on through Pedra Furada and Carvalhal. Barcelos comes at the end of the day, and the road enters it across the Gothic bridge over the Cávado, built, Wikipedia says, between 1325 and 1328. Barcelos has been a stop for pilgrims since the Middle Ages, and it is the home of a famous legend. Wikipedia tells it in several versions. In one, a Galician pilgrim on his way to Santiago was wrongly accused of a crime and condemned to hang. He asked to see the judge, who was at dinner, and said that the roasted rooster on the table would crow to prove his innocence. As he was hanged, the bird stood up and crowed; the knot had not tightened, and the pilgrim was freed. He later raised a stone cross there in thanks. The rooster of Barcelos is often seen as a symbol of Portugal. The town is also known for its pottery figurines of farmers, musicians and nativity scenes, and Wikipedia says it belongs to UNESCO's Creative Cities Network as a Crafts and Folk Art City.

Around Barcelos, the Confraternity says, you will also see the red and white marks of the long-distance footpath GR11-E9. Follow the yellow arrows. The day is ~27 km along the OpenStreetMap route, against ~26 to ~28 km in published guides.

### Day 3: Barcelos to Ponte de Lima (~34 km)

This is the longest day of the book, ~34 km along the OpenStreetMap route and ~34 to ~35 km in published guides. Decide before you start whether to walk it whole. A fit walker with an early start can. Published guides say it can be split in two, around the middle of the day. The way passes Lijó, Aborim and Balugães, and later Vitorino dos Piães, Facha and Correlhã, and goes over a ridge by the pass of Portela de Tamel, which a published guide names. The app and local advice will tell you which villages offer a bed for the night.

If you walk it whole, treat it as a long day and nothing more: breakfast at first light, water at every chance, lunch you have already bought, a hat and a plan for the heat. Heat is the main danger on a day like this: the record high at Porto airport is ~38.6 °C. If you are tired by the middle of the day, the middle of the day is the time to stop.

Ponte de Lima rewards the effort. Wikipedia calls it the oldest chartered town in Portugal and dates its first charter to 4 March 1125. It takes its name from its bridge over the Lima, which Wikipedia says may go back to the first century and was rebuilt in 1125. The Xunta places the Roman road Via XIX through Ponte de Lima, and Wikipedia connects the Roman bridge with it.

There is also an old story about this river. Wikipedia reports that in antiquity the Lima was said to cause loss of memory, and that Strabo compared it to Lethe, the river of forgetfulness; it suggests that Roman soldiers feared crossing it during the conquest of the region in 136 BC. It is a good place to remember why you started, and a safe one to forget your blisters.
