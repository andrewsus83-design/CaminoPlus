# The Way in brief

## Where it starts and ends

The Central route of the Portuguese Way starts at the Sé, the cathedral of Porto, and ends at the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela. It heads north through Barcelos and Ponte de Lima to Valença, crosses the Minho by the International Bridge into Tui, and then runs through O Porriño, Redondela, Pontevedra, Caldas de Reis and Padrón. The cathedral of Santiago is the last building you reach.

The CaminoPlus catalog gives the length as ~240 km. Other sources differ, because they start from different points and count different paths. The Confraternity of St James says ~252 km from Porto, and Wikipedia says ~260 km. The Xunta de Galicia, the regional government, gives ~119 km for the Galician part from Tui. The eleven days in this book, measured as the stage chapters explain, add up to ~246 km. Treat every figure as a good guide and not as a measurement.

The eleven days follow the CaminoPlus stage list:

- Day 1: Porto to Vilarinho, ~27 km
- Day 2: Vilarinho to Barcelos, ~27 km
- Day 3: Barcelos to Ponte de Lima, ~34 km
- Day 4: Ponte de Lima to Rubiães, ~18 km
- Day 5: Rubiães to Tui, ~21 km
- Day 6: Tui to O Porriño, ~18 km
- Day 7: O Porriño to Redondela, ~16 km
- Day 8: Redondela to Pontevedra, ~18 km
- Day 9: Pontevedra to Caldas de Reis, ~23 km
- Day 10: Caldas de Reis to Padrón, ~19 km
- Day 11: Padrón to Santiago de Compostela, ~25 km

Stage plans vary a great deal on this road. The CaminoPlus route list speaks of twelve stages, because published plans divide the first days of the walk in different ways, and some end a day in Valença instead of Tui. This book follows the eleven-day list. A later chapter shows how to cut it differently.

## What it feels like

It is a road of small rivers and stone bridges. In Portugal you cross the Cávado at Barcelos and the Lima at Ponte de Lima, and climb a ridge between them; then the Minho, the border, with a fortress on one bank and a cathedral on the other. In Galicia the road keeps within reach of the sea, with the head of the Ría de Vigo beside you on Day 8, before it turns inland to cross the Ulla at Padrón.

The Confraternity describes the route as following secondary and minor roads, with stretches of farm track and forest. It also warns that the Central route follows a main transport corridor, so it meets national roads, and the current route often joins them at the entrance and exit of towns. Expect road surfaces as well as woods, and a long first day through the suburbs of Porto.

The waymarking is generous. The Confraternity says yellow arrows are plentiful from Porto cathedral to the Portuguese border, thanks to the Associação dos Amigos do Caminho Português de Santiago, and that in Galicia granite pillars add the distance to Santiago to the arrows.

## Why it exists

The Xunta says this road grew in importance from the twelfth century, after Portugal became independent. It follows older roads, among them the Roman Via XIX, which the Xunta dates to the first century AD and which linked Braga with Astorga through Ponte de Lima, Tui, Pontevedra, Santiago and Lugo.

Kings and queens took it. The Xunta names Elizabeth of Portugal, the "Holy Queen", who made a pilgrimage in the fourteenth century and offered her crown at the altar of St James; the Confraternity gives her years as 1271 to 1336 and calls her Queen Isabel. It also names Manuel I, who walked from Lisbon to Santiago in 1502. Portugal's north to south road network, the Xunta adds, was organised around the pilgrimage. And in the nineteenth century, when pilgrim numbers were at their lowest, the Portuguese Way was the busiest of the routes: more than eighty per cent of the foreign pilgrims that century were Portuguese.

## Company on the road

Wikipedia, reporting the Pilgrim's Office statistics, says that in 2025 the Portuguese Way was the second most walked route to Santiago after the French Way, with 19 per cent of pilgrims. Of those who received the Compostela, 41 per cent started in Tui, 26 per cent in Porto and 18 per cent in Valença. Most finish between May and September.

Read together, those figures suggest that many people join at or near the border, so the road may feel quieter in Portugal than in Galicia. That is a reading of the statistics, not a promise.
