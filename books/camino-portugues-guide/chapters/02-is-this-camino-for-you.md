# Is this Camino for you?

## How hard is it?

Not very, by the standards of the long Caminos, and not nothing. The Xunta rates the Galician part of the route "Medium-Low" overall, and rates its six stages from Tui as Low or Medium. There are no mountains. Two places are singled out for difficulty. In Portugal, Wikipedia calls the Labruja hills, on Day 4, one of the most tiring parts of the inland route. In Galicia, the Xunta marks the three sections of Day 6, out of Tui, Medium-High, its highest rating on the route, though it rates the whole stage Low.

What makes the Portuguese Way demanding is less the height than the length of some days and the surface. Day 3 is ~34 km, the longest in this book. Much of the walking is on minor roads and paved paths, and the route meets national roads, as the Confraternity of St James warns, especially where it enters and leaves towns. Your feet will notice before your lungs do.

## Who it suits

It suits a first-time walker who has trained a little and wants a Camino with a clear shape: a start at a cathedral, a border in the middle, an arrival at another cathedral. The days run from ~16 to ~34 km, and eleven of them, with a rest day or two, fit inside a fortnight. The road passes through many towns between the stage towns, so a day can be cut short. The app and local advice will tell you where to sleep.

It suits people who like their history in stone. The road crosses medieval bridges, passes a Romanesque church at Rates, runs along sections of a Roman road, and goes through the old centres of Ponte de Lima, Tui and Pontevedra.

It suits people who want company. The Pilgrim's Office statistics, as Wikipedia reports them, put the Portuguese Way second among the routes to Santiago in 2025. Alone is possible, but you will have to choose your hours and your season.

## Who might choose another

If you want solitude and silence, think about the season. Most pilgrims finish between May and September, according to the same statistics, and many join at or near the border, so the Galician half is likely to be the busier.

If you want mountains, forests without roads, or a high pass, this is not that Camino. The French Way, ~773 km in the catalog, crosses the Pyrenees, the Meseta and the mountains of León.

If you want the sea, take the Coastal variant, which leaves the Central route near Porto and rejoins it at Redondela. It has a book of its own in this series, *The Coastal Way*, and this book does not describe it.

If you have only a week, the Galician half is the short version: the Xunta's six stages from Tui to Santiago, ~119 km. Whether a start in Tui, Valença or anywhere else counts for the Compostela is a question for the Pilgrim's Office, and the chapter on other ways to split the days gives the Office's rule.

## Questions worth asking yourself

- Can you walk ~20 km on a hard surface, two or three days in a row, with your pack?
- Is the long Day 3 acceptable, or will you split it? The next chapters show how.
- Do you want the whole road from Porto, or the border and Galicia?
- What do you want from the walk: sport, solitude, history, a rest, prayer, a promise kept? Every answer is a good one, and the road does not ask.

If most of your answers are yes, and the others are "we shall see", you are ready for the next chapter.
