# Arriving

## The cathedral

The Portuguese Way ends where every Way ends: at the cathedral of Santiago de Compostela, the reputed burial place of St James. Wikipedia dates the start of construction to 1075. It is a Romanesque building with later Gothic and Baroque additions. The Xunta says the traditional entrance for pilgrims on the Portuguese Way is through As Praterías Square, and Wikipedia says that the cathedral's Praterías façade was built in 1103. The great Baroque façade on the Praza do Obradoiro, the square in front, was completed in 1740. Inside, the Pórtico da Gloria, the carved Romanesque doorway by Master Mateo, carries the date 1188, according to Wikipedia. The old town was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1985.

The cathedral keeps a Holy Door, opened only in a Jacobean Holy Year, and the next Holy Year is 2027, then 2032. It also keeps the botafumeiro, the great censer, which Wikipedia says is swung on certain important religious holidays, and at every Pilgrims' Mass in a Holy Year.

Take a moment in the square before you do anything else. Sit on the stones if you like. The arrival may be quieter or noisier than you expected; there is no right feeling.

## The Pilgrim's Office and the Compostela

The *Compostela* is the certificate of your pilgrimage, written in Latin, and the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago issues it. The rule it follows is in the chapter on other ways to split the days, and it is the Office's to apply. The Latin text speaks of those who come *devotionis affectu, voti vel pietatis*, which the Office's own English version renders as coming "with an attitude of devotion or because of a vow or promise".

Bring your credencial, stamped along the way. You also take a ticket with a QR code, which lets you follow your place in the queue on your phone. The Office warns that in times of great influx it cannot guarantee that you will receive the Compostela on the same day, so do not book a flight home for the afternoon. The Office's website gives its address and its current hours, which are not in this book because they change.

If you walked for other reasons, or are not sure that yours count, the Office also offers a separate *certificate of distance*. It records where you began, how many kilometres you travelled, the dates and the route, whatever your starting point. It carries a fee, and the Office gives the amount. The Office says its reception service and the Compostela itself are free.

The Office meets a great many people in a day. Be patient, be warm, and if you have a doubt about your credencial, ask before you queue.

## The pilgrims' Mass

The cathedral holds a Pilgrims' Mass. Check the cathedral's own website for the time, which changes. You do not need to be a believer to sit at the back and listen. It is a cathedral full of people who have walked.

## The next day

Try not to leave on the morning after you arrive. A day in Santiago lets the walk settle. Wash your clothes, sleep late, and look at the old town. On the cathedral square stands a building that the Catholic Monarchs founded as a royal hospital for pilgrims; the Office says that those who showed their Compostela there once had the right to stay three days free, and that it has been a hotel since 1954.

If you still have legs and time, some pilgrims walk on to Finisterre and Muxía on the coast, ~90 km farther, a route you will find in the CaminoPlus app's list. If you do not, think about the way home: Santiago has an airport ~12 km from the city, and the Confraternity of St James notes a bus service of about four hours to Porto. Check the current times. And buy a postcard, and write to someone you walked with in your head.
