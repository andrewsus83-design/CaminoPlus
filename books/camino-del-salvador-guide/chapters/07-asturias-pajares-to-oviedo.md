# Asturias: Pajares to Oviedo

## The character of these days

Asturias puts it fairly. The hardest part, the climb to the pass, is behind you, but the climbs to Chanos, to El Padrún and to Picullanza and La Manxoya, it says, call for a good state of fitness. In return the road gives majestic landscapes, above all as you come down from Payares, and a singular route through the mining basin that follows the Lena and the Caudal. Green is the colour of Asturias, as the association notes, and the stone markers carry the scallop shell.

The three Asturian stages on the Turismo Asturias website, ~22.1, ~19.0 and ~18.4 km, begin at the pass and break at Campomanes and Mieres. The usual six-stage plan in this book breaks at Pola de Lena and Mieres instead, and the chapter on other ways to split the days sets them side by side.

### Day 4: Pajares to Pola de Lena (~24 km)

The sources draw this day in more than one way. Asturias takes the road to Floracebos, then a path by the chapel of the Virgen de las Nieves to La Romía and down to Naveo. It climbs the Alto de Torones, descends steeply to Fierros, climbs hard to Fresneo, and comes down through woods of chestnut and holly, by the village of Erías, to Campomanes. The association describes a variant by San Miguel del Río and Llanos de Somerón, and the Junta's leaflet marks a winter line and a summer line near Pajares. Follow the markers, and ask at your hostel the night before which line is good that day.

The association says the day's difficulty is in the descents, which test your knees, and that from Campomanes the walking is completely flat. It places the day in the Natural Park of Las Ubiñas and La Mesa.

At Campomanes, the Junta's guide says, the roads over the Pajares and La Cubilla passes meet, and by the river runs the Roman road from León to Gijón. Before the day ends comes the little church of Santa Cristina de Lena. Asturias calls it one of the most paradigmatic pre-Romanesque buildings in Asturias, built in the time of the Asturian monarchy, and singles out its superb iconostasis.

### Day 5: Pola de Lena to Mieres (~13 km)

The association rates this day easy and calls it a rest after the hard ramps behind you and before the ones ahead. It begins by a service road of an industrial estate. In Villallana, the association says, the church of San Martín stands beside the *Hospitalón*, a former pilgrims' hospital. In Ujo, Asturias says, the church of Santa Eulalia keeps a Romanesque doorway and apse.

There is one warning. The association says that the official markers from Pola de Lena to Ujo follow the old national road, and calls that completely inadvisable for its danger. It suggests following the yellow arrows of a recently recovered stretch that avoids the asphalt. Check the current line before you go, and walk the one that keeps you off the road.

Asturias advises the riverside path along the Caudal from Ujo to Mieres, crossing by the Ponte Verde, where the traditional crossing was. The town's name carries the road in it: Asturias says Mieres del Camín, still often called "del Camino", is one of the traces left by the Jacobean tradition in Asturian place names. You enter by the bridge of La Perra and leave by the Plaza del Requejo, one of the squares Asturias calls the incomparable cathedral of Asturian cider.

### Day 6: Mieres to Oviedo (~19 km)

The association calls it a clearly urban stage, with asphalt for much of it, some good stretches that avoid it, and a stretch on a Roman road. It rises and falls all the way, with El Padrún, La Manzaneda and La Manxoya as the short climbs, and it is short enough to be comfortable.

Asturias names the places. At La Rebollá there was once an inn, and the parish church keeps a few Romanesque corbels. On the way into Olloniego you cross a footbridge over the dual carriageway and the railway, and Asturias singles out the town's Romanesque bridge and its keep and castle. Then you climb by the base of the peak of Arnea and cross the valley of the Gafo to La Manxoya.

From La Manxoya, Asturias says, it is told that the old pilgrims cried out *¡Mi Dios!*, an exclamation of awe, when they saw the tower of the cathedral on the horizon. The walk ends by the district of San Lázaro and the cathedral of San Salvador with its Holy Chamber.
