# León and the Bernesga valley

## The character of these days

The first two days follow the Bernesga river north, out of a city and into a narrowing valley. The ground is mostly streets, paths and dirt tracks, with small ups and downs that never quite let you forget you are climbing. On the second day, after Buiza, the first real mountain arrives.

The lengths in this book follow the page of Oviedo's cathedral, which gives six usual stages that add up to ~120.3 km. The association of the León municipalities gives slightly different figures, and the chapter on other ways to split the days names every source. Treat each figure as a good guide, not a measurement.

### Day 1: León to La Robla (~27 km)

You start at the gates of the old pilgrims' hospital of San Marcos, where the Camino Francés and the Salvador part. The association says the first waymark stands on the pilgrims' avenue to the right of it, and that a plaque set in the ground points left for Santiago and right for the Saviour. It is a good place to stand for a minute.

The way follows the Bernesga on city streets. The association says that a signed riverside path saves you ~7 km of asphalt. Then come paths through woods of oak and holm oak to Carbajal de la Legua. The Junta de Castilla y León's guide gives it a long past: a twelfth-century foundation of Augustinian canons, replaced in 1147 by Benedictine nuns from San Pelayo in León, whom the guide says were affectionately known as *las Carbajalas*. It places the old Roman road from León to Oviedo near Cabanillas, the next village.

Near the end you pass the chapel of Celada, which the Junta says is Gothic in origin, from the fourteenth century, and altered in the seventeenth and eighteenth. Then the association's "door and hand of the pilgrim" welcome you to La Robla, which you enter under the main road and over the railway.

The association rates the day easy and warns that the ground is not flat: the constant small rises and falls will test your stamina. Carry food and water for the whole day, since the association says some villages on it offer nothing.

### Day 2: La Robla to Poladura de la Tercia (~23 km)

The association divides this day in two. The first half is easy and almost flat, as far as La Pola de Gordón. After it come ~5 km of asphalt that cannot be avoided, because the road is squeezed between mountains and river as far as Buiza. The Junta's guide calls Buiza an important crossing of Roman roads, pilgrim roads and royal roads, and says it is named in 1036, in the donation of the place by King Fernando I to the cathedral of Oviedo.

At Buiza the second half begins, and with it the mountain. The association says that in ~2.5 km you reach the top of the Forcadas de San Antón, at ~1,450 m, "as spectacular as hard". The Junta's leaflet map marks ~1,462 m, and the association gives ~1,459 m on another page, so sources differ by ~10 m on a hill this size. Then you descend through a gully called the Barrancón and follow a track to San Martín and Poladura de la Tercia. The association rates the whole day medium to high.

There is an option at the Barrancón. You can stay on the track and pass through the gorges of Rodiezmo to the Cruz de la Salve. From there, the association says, pilgrims once saw the shrine of Golpejar and the chapel of Barrio, and prayed a *Salve* for protection as they looked at the mountains they would cross the next day. This way is only ~200 m longer and the two meet again ~1 km further on. The association calls it a good choice if the path through the Barrancón is under snow. At the Cruz de la Salve the way turns sharply left onto a larger track. If you miss the turn you reach Rodiezmo and must walk the rest by road.

The association's longer plans cut this day in two at Buiza. Whichever you choose, arrive in Poladura with something left: the day that follows is short and hard.
