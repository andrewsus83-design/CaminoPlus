# Body, weather and safety

This chapter is plain on purpose. Nothing here is a reason to be afraid of this road. It is a list of the things worth taking seriously on a short one with a mountain in it.

## Feet and legs

Your feet decide how the walk goes. Stop at the first hot spot and dress it before it becomes a blister, keep your feet clean and dry, and eat and drink before you are hungry or thirsty. The association says the difficulty of the fourth day is the descents, which will test your knees, so take the steep ground slowly and use poles if you carry them. If a knee, a shin or the top of a foot gives you a sharp or growing pain, stop. A day lost to rest is better than a week lost to an injury. For anything that worries you, see a doctor or a pharmacist.

## The third day

This is the one day where a forecast should decide what you do.

- **Fog.** The Confraternity says the crossing may bring thick fog. Waymarking is generally very good, it adds, but in the mountains and in bad weather you must make sure you find the signs and follow them. If you cannot see the next marker, stop and wait, or go back to the last one.
- **Rain and cold.** The Confraternity lists heavy rain and severe cold, and the association says nights at altitude are cool even in summer. Carry a waterproof, a warm layer and a hat, and keep one dry set of clothes in a bag that cannot get wet.
- **Water and snow.** In the thaw, the association says, the streams on this day can carry a lot of water, so do not cross one you cannot see the bottom of. The snowfields can make the crossing very difficult, even impossible. Its rule is to avoid the mountain when it is snowed over, to listen to the hostel when it says conditions are bad, and to leave the stage for another time. The Way, it says, will always be there.
- **Time.** Start early, with daylight in hand. Anyone can twist an ankle, and in the mountains with few hours of light that is a serious problem, as the association puts it.

## The road is not an escape

The association is blunt: the road is never an alternative. In snow, it says, the ploughs clear the carriageway first and not the verge, and it calls the road one of the most dangerous in Spain. Its advice is a taxi, a train or a bus, and the mountain stages left for another day.

The Confraternity mentions an unofficial, unsigned winter route, kept in its own guidebook, as an escape from the mountains in bad weather. This book has no details of it, and no source read says how the two pieces of advice fit together. Ask at the hostel the evening before what is passable that week.

## Other roads, other days

- **The second day** has ~5 km of asphalt between the river and the mountainside, the association says, which cannot be avoided.
- **The fifth day.** The association says the official markers from Pola de Lena to Ujo follow the old national road, and calls that completely inadvisable for its danger. It points to yellow arrows on a recovered stretch that avoids the road. Check the current line before you go.
- **On any road,** face the oncoming traffic, keep to the edge and wear something bright. Never walk on a road in the dark.
- **In July and August,** the Confraternity says, León province is very hot. The first two days are in the valley, and a hot day there is a long one. Start early and carry more water than seems sensible.

## When to stop

Tell someone where you will sleep each night, and message them when you arrive. Keep your phone charged and your map saved on it, and do not rely on signal in the mountains. If you are tired, ill or frightened by the weather, stop. The road will be there tomorrow.

AEMET, the Spanish state weather agency, publishes warnings and town forecasts. Read them the night before and again at breakfast.

**In an emergency in Spain, call 112,** the European emergency number. Your Europe, the EU's information service, says it works in every EU country alongside the national numbers, that callers are answered in the language or languages of the country, and that English is offered in some countries only. Do not count on it. Know the Spanish name of the last village you passed, and say where you are as exactly as you can.
