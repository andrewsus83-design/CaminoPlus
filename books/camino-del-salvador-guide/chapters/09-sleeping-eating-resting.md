# Sleeping, eating, resting

## The pilgrims' hostels

Asturias says the stages of the Camino de Santiago begin and end in towns with hostels, public or private, and that the roads through Asturias have enough places to sleep for every pilgrim. The Confraternity of St James says there are albergues and other suitable accommodation on this road, and the association says there is enough infrastructure to do it in as many as nine days. This book does not say which village has what. That changes, and the app and the hostels' own pages have the current answer.

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: shared rooms, bunks, a *hospitalero* (the host) who checks your credencial and stamps it. Bring a sleeping bag, which the association's own list includes. Keep your pack off the bed and your boots by the door, and pack the night before so that you are not rustling at five in the morning. Everyone else walks tomorrow too.

Some hostels in the mountain villages are small, as the Junta's leaflet map shows. The Confraternity strongly recommends phoning ahead to book any accommodation that is not a pilgrims' hostel, and it says that in some places a meal must be arranged by phone beforehand so that the food can be bought in. Do that before the long days. The tourist offices and the hostels' pages have the numbers; this book gives none.

## Eating

Eat when you can, and carry food and water for the day. The Confraternity warns of a stretch of ~50 km without shops, and the association says some villages on the first day offer no services.

When you do sit down, the Junta's guide has a good deal to say about the table of the valleys of Gordón and La Tercia: cured meats and sausages (*chorizo*, ham, *botillos*, *longaniza*, *morcilla*), lentils, beans and cabbage, game and trout, and a dish called *entrecogido*, a platter built on the meats of the *matanza*, the traditional pig-killing. Do not count on all of it. Ask what the kitchen has.

## Resting

The association's advice about pace is as plain as its advice about snow: do not run, rest as much as you need, and you will find the stages comfortable. The third day is short in kilometres and long in effort, so plan an early night after it. Both ends of the road are cities worth a day: León before you start, and Oviedo, where the cathedral and its Holy Chamber are the reason you came.

A taxi is a tool, not a failure. The association notes that taxis are much used on this road, for tired legs, for a short cut to a shop or a bed, and for a day when the mountain says no. Use one without apology.
