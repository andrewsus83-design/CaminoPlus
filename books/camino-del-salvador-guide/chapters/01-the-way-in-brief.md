# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino del Salvador starts in León, "at the gates of the old pilgrims' hospital of San Marcos", in the words of Turismo Asturias, the tourism service of the Principado de Asturias. The Junta de Castilla y León's guide says San Marcos is where the Camino Francés and the Camino del Salvador part. The road ends at the cathedral of San Salvador in Oviedo.

The Confraternity of St James describes the shape of it. The first ~40 km, as far as Buiza, follow the valley of the Bernesga and are fairly flat, on paths and very minor roads. The next ~25 km cross the Cordillera to the village of Pajares, in Asturias, with two quite substantial climbs and descents. After that the road falls through the valleys of the Lena and the Caudal to Oviedo. Asturias calls this the mining basin. The Junta's guide names Arbás del Puerto as the last place in Castilla y León.

## How long

The CaminoPlus catalog gives ~120 km, and so does the Confraternity. The Junta's guide, updated in 2011, counts ~120.3 km, of which ~58.8 are in Castilla y León. Asturias gives no total. It publishes its part as three stages that add up to ~59.5 km (~22.1, ~19.0 and ~18.4), counted from the pass, where the Junta's guide counts ~61.5 km from Arbás.

Others differ. The cathedral of Oviedo says ~122 km, though its own six stage figures add up to ~120.3. The association of the municipalities on the León side gives ~121 km in one place and ~123 in another. The Junta's leaflet map runs to ~125 km, and Spanish Wikipedia says ~116. The gaps come from where a source starts counting and which line it follows. This book uses ~120 km.

The catalog calls the road the Camino del Salvador. Asturias writes Camino de El Salvador, and the cathedral and the Junta also use Camino de San Salvador. *Salvador* means Saviour, and the cathedral at the end is dedicated to him.

## The usual six days

Oviedo's cathedral and the association of the León municipalities both divide the road into six stages with the same stage towns, and this book follows them, with the cathedral's figures:

1. León to La Robla: ~27 km.
2. La Robla to Poladura de la Tercia: ~23 km.
3. Poladura de la Tercia to Pajares: ~14 km.
4. Pajares to Pola de Lena: ~24 km.
5. Pola de Lena to Mieres: ~13 km.
6. Mieres to Oviedo: ~19 km.

The third day is the short one, and the mountain day. Other counts exist, and the chapter on other ways to split the days sets them out.

## Why it exists

Oviedo's cathedral keeps the Cámara Santa, the Holy Chamber, and in it an ark of relics. The cathedral's page tells the older story as tradition: the ark left Jerusalem when the Persians took it in 614, travelled through North Africa and Spain, was hidden on a mountain near Oviedo, and was brought into the city under King Alfonso II. What is documented begins on 13 March 1075, when King Alfonso VI, passing through Oviedo on his own pilgrimage to Santiago, saw the ark opened before bishops and the court. The record of that day lists many relics. The one that stands out is the *sudarium*, the linen cloth that covered Christ's head after his death.

Asturias says the discovery made Oviedo a centre of pilgrimage in its own right. The Junta's guide says that by the end of the eleventh century the basilica had become the second pilgrimage centre of the medieval peninsula. The Confraternity says the purpose of the walk is to visit the Sudarium, and points to the Gospel of John, chapter 20, verses 6 and 7.

A saying carried the idea. Asturias gives it as a popular verse that people still recite: *Quien va a Santiago y no al Salvador, visita al criado y olvida al Señor.* Whoever goes to Santiago and not to the Saviour visits the servant and forgets the Lord. The Junta writes *deja*, "leaves", for the last verb, and the sources differ on the origin: Asturias calls it medieval and popular, the cathedral and the Confraternity call it French. This book does not choose.

## A detour, not a road to Santiago

Asturias says the road began as a detour from the Camino Francés, one that let those who took it join the Camino Primitivo if they wished, and also gave the walk a meaningful end in its own right. The cathedral says that for many pilgrims it was the end of the journey, and for many others the start of the road to Santiago. Spanish Wikipedia adds that from Oviedo they could go on by the coastal road or by the Primitivo. Asturias says that quite a few took the road home from Compostela, to kneel before the Lord they had passed on the way out.

## Why it faded, and why it is back

The Junta's guide gives three reasons for the decline. Legends told of the hardness and danger of the road. A new main road, today's N-630, was opened in the eighteenth century. And the confiscations of church property in the nineteenth century, with the loss of convents and hospitals, left the route almost forgotten. The same guide, in 2011, says it is increasingly used by pilgrims. Today the Salvador has its own credencial and its own certificate, and the chapter on arriving explains both.
