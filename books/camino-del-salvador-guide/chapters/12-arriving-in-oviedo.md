# Arriving in Oviedo

## The last streets

You come in by the district of San Lázaro, and the road ends at the cathedral of San Salvador, whose Holy Chamber, Asturias says, marks the definitive end of the journey. The Junta's guide dates the cathedral to the fourteenth to sixteenth centuries, and says it keeps the image of the Saviour, from the thirteenth century, which gave the road its name.

The Holy Chamber is older, and its story has changed. The cathedral says it was long believed to have been built under King Alfonso II (791 to 842), but that archaeology now points to the reign of Alfonso III (866 to 910). In October 1934 an explosion destroyed it, and it was rebuilt between 1939 and 1942.

## The Sudarium

The cathedral's page on visiting says that the Sudarium always stays in the Holy Chamber, and that, for reasons of conservation, the original can be seen only three weeks a year. The rest of the time you see an exact replica. Wikipedia names Good Friday and two days in September, while the cathedral's page gives no dates, so ask which weeks it means.

The cathedral publishes a calendar of the days it opens to visitors, and some days it is closed. Look at it before you fix your arrival day. It says a full visit with its audio guide takes about an hour. It also stamps the credencial of any pilgrim who asks, whatever Camino they walk, and says a neighbouring parish church holds a pilgrims' Mass and blessing, with the time on its own page. Look there, not here.

## The Salvadorana

The Salvadorana is the certificate of Oviedo's cathedral for those who have walked the road. The cathedral says it is signed by the Dean, and the association says so too. To ask for it you show a credencial stamped at the places you pass, and one stamp per stage is enough. The association adds that you can also get it at the pilgrims' hostel in Oviedo, while the cathedral's page names only the cathedral. Ask there how and when it is given.

The Confraternity of St James calls it the road's own "Compostela". That is a loose way to put it. The Compostela is a different certificate, given by a different body for a different walk, and the next section says what its rules are.

## The Compostela

The Compostela is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules. The Office owns the rules, and they can change. This book says what the Office says, and sends you to the Office in any doubt.

- The Compostela is for those who come to the tomb of the Apostle for religious and/or spiritual reasons and follow the routes of the Way of St James on foot, by bicycle or on horseback.
- It asks for at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred kilometres by bicycle, shown by the credencial, stamped along the route.
- On foot or on horseback, that means at least a hundred continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago that the Office recognises. The final stage must include at least the last one leading into the cathedral.
- You may walk the Way in stages, if they follow in chronological and geographical order. A pilgrim who walks only the minimum distance must get the credencial stamped at the start and at the end of each stage, with the date.
- A pilgrim who has already walked part of a recognised route outside Spain needs no less than seventy kilometres in Spain.
- The Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special and exceptional cases.

The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.

This walk ends at Oviedo, not at Santiago. The Office's pages read for this book, on the Compostela, the credencial and the Certificate of Distance, do not mention the Camino del Salvador or Oviedo. So this book says nothing about whether the Office counts the kilometres you walk to Oviedo, or what it would record for them.

From Oviedo ~320 km lie ahead on the Camino Primitivo, far more than the last hundred kilometres the Office asks for. If your plan is anything other than walking the Primitivo from Oviedo, or if you will walk it in stages, ask the Pilgrim's Office before you go.

## Onwards, if you want to

The cathedral says the Holy Chamber is the starting point of the Camino Primitivo. Opposite the statue of King Alfonso II, whom the cathedral calls the first pilgrim to Santiago, is the plaque from which the Primitivo sets out, and the branch that joins the Camino del Norte. *The Original Way*, in this series, walks the Primitivo. Your road and that one do not meet halfway: this one ends where that one begins.

## The next day

Do not leave on the morning after you arrive. The cathedral is the reason you came, and it is worth seeing without a pack on your back. Plan your way home with the timetables in front of you. Oviedo has a railway station, and Asturias Airport is ~48 km away.
