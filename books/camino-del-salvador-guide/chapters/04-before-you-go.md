# Before you go

## Your credencial

The credencial is your pilgrim's passport, stamped at the end of each day. It gets you into the pilgrims' hostels, Asturias and the association both say, and you show it in Oviedo to ask for the Salvadorana.

A credencial made for this road exists. The Confraternity of St James says it is available from the cathedral in León, and the association of the León municipalities names the tourist offices of La Robla and La Pola de Gordón, the seat in León of the pilgrim association, and hostels along the way. You do not need it. Oviedo's cathedral says that any other model is valid, and the association says the same of the official pilgrim credencial. Churches, hostels and tourist offices keep stamps, says Asturias, and Oviedo's cathedral will stamp the credencial of any pilgrim who asks, whatever Camino they walk.

For the Salvadorana the cathedral asks for a credencial stamped at the places you pass, and says one stamp per stage will do.

## Getting to León, and home from Oviedo

Wikipedia says León has a long-distance railway station, with high-speed services to Madrid in about two hours and direct trains to Galicia, Asturias and Valladolid. León's airport is ~6 km from the centre and flies mostly within Spain. Oviedo has a railway station with long-distance, regional and suburban services, and Asturias Airport lies ~48 km from the city, linked to it by motorway and a scheduled bus. Look up times on the operators' own pages.

Under the pass, high-speed trains now run in a tunnel of ~24.7 km, part of the line between León and Gijón, which Wikipedia says opened to passengers on 30 November 2023. You will cross the same range over the top, on foot.

## Training, boots and the pack

Asturias advises starting to walk a few months before you leave, in comfortable shoes that you have already used, and carrying sandals for the evenings. It says a rucksack should not pass ~10 kg, and that a torch and a walking stick are indispensable. Add the association's advice for this road: mountain boots in spring, autumn and above all winter.

The association's list for the pack includes a sleeping bag, a water bottle, poles, a torch, a change of clothes, good socks, a cap, plasters, a waterproof, sunscreen and sunglasses, and a first-aid kit, with your identity card, credencial, health card and a bank card or cash. It also says to forget the "just in case" items, because the road passes several important villages with the services you need. The Confraternity says that in the mountains pilgrim services are scant. They describe different stretches, so pack by the second warning and plan by both.

## Food, beds and a phone call

Carry food and water for the day. The association says some villages on the first stage have no services. The Confraternity strongly recommends phoning ahead to book any accommodation that is not a pilgrims' hostel, and says that in several places you must phone ahead to be sure a meal can be bought in for you. This book gives no phone numbers, because they change.

## Tell someone

Both the Confraternity and the association ask you to tell someone outside your group where and when you are walking, so that the alarm can be raised if you do not arrive when expected. Keep your phone charged, save your map on it before you leave the valley, and do not rely on signal in the mountains.

## Language and waymarking

The Confraternity says a little Spanish helps, especially if you need to ask for directions on the crossing of the Cordillera.

It calls the waymarking generally very good, with a warning: in the mountains, and in bad weather above all, make sure you find and follow the signs. The association describes markers, wooden posts and yellow arrows on the ground in León province, and the usual stone markers with the scallop shell in Asturias. Its words: as long as you watch for them, it is almost impossible to leave the road.
