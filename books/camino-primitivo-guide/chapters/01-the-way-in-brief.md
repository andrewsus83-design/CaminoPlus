# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino Primitivo runs from Oviedo, the capital of Asturias, to Santiago de Compostela. The first ~146 km are in Asturias: from the cathedral of Oviedo west through the councils of Las Regueras, Grado, Salas, Tineo, Allande and Grandas de Salime, up to the pass of Puerto del Acebo, which is the regional border. In Galicia the road crosses the province of Lugo, passes through the city of Lugo itself, and at Melide, in the province of A Coruña, joins the Camino Francés. The Xunta de Galicia says the Primitivo converges with the French Way there and continues to the cathedral, ~53 km away.

## How long is it?

The app's catalog gives ~320 km. The Confraternity of St James says about the same, and the Asturias tourism pages speak of ~321 km. They count differently. The Asturias itinerary measures ~146 km from Oviedo to the pass (and ~142 km by the high variant called Hospitales, described later). The Xunta measures ~167 km from the pass to Santiago on its route page, and says ~171 km on another page of its own. The Spanish Wikipedia gives ~313 km. The fourteen stages this book follows add up to ~312 km.

Plan on ~320 km and expect a few kilometres either way. Stage counts vary too. Asturias divides its part into seven stages, the Xunta divides the Galician part into eight, and the Spanish Wikipedia says the standard walk takes ten to fifteen days. This book follows fourteen walking days named by their stage towns, and the chapter on the high road shows where others cut them.

## Why it is called the original

*Primitivo* means first, original. The Xunta calls this the original and oldest pilgrimage route, and Asturias calls it the first Jacobean route recorded in history. The reason is a king.

## A short history

The story begins in the early years of the ninth century. Asturias tells it as a legend. Bishop Teodomiro of Iria Flavia, in the far west of the Kingdom of Asturias, came to the court at Oviedo with news: a hermit had seen strange lights over the forest of Libredón, pointing to a tomb where the apostle James was believed to lie. King Alfonso II wanted to see it for himself, and ordered a shrine built on the spot. The Xunta de Galicia says that this king, known as the Chaste, travelled to Santiago in the first third of the ninth century to confirm that the remains were the apostle's, and it calls him the first pilgrim king.

The Asturias pages are frank about what that means for your feet. They say the historical sources are not very reliable and that nothing fixes the exact route the king followed. They suggest, as the logical guess, the Roman road from Lucus Asturum, near Oviedo, to Lucus Augusti, which is Lugo. The Xunta says the Primitivo mainly follows the path of Roman roads. So "the road of Alfonso II" is a tradition and a sensible guess, not a surveyed line.

The Xunta adds that his successor, Alfonso III, took the route twice, and it calls him the architect of the consecration of the second basilica in Santiago in 899. Once León became the capital, the kings of the eleventh and twelfth centuries promoted the Camino Francés. The Primitivo stayed an alternative, for pilgrims who wished to venerate the relics at the cathedral of Oviedo and in Lugo.

The Confraternity of St James records a Spanish saying about Oviedo: *Quien va a Santiago y no al Salvador, visita al criado y deja al Señor*. Whoever goes to Santiago but not to the Saviour visits the servant and leaves the Lord. In its account, many pilgrims left the Francés at León and walked some ~120 km north to the cathedral of Oviedo, then took the Primitivo back to the Francés.

In 2015 UNESCO listed the route as a World Heritage Site, together with the Camino del Norte.

## Who walks it now

Wikipedia, reporting the Pilgrim's Office statistics, says about 24,400 pilgrims finished the Primitivo in 2024, making it the fifth most walked route. About 58 per cent of them started in Oviedo, and most finished in August.

## What it feels like

Asturias gives a reason why this road has kept its character. The mountains of the south-west are complicated country, and until recently they held back roads and development, so the lands the Way crosses have stayed tied to farming and livestock. You will see it in the slate roofs, the dry-stone walls, and the raised granaries called *hórreos*. Asturias says those who know the Jacobean routes well call the Primitivo the one that has best kept the essence of the old pilgrimage.

The walking follows the arc of the map: hills and farmland first, then high mountain days with few villages, then Galicia, with Roman Lugo, Romanesque churches, and from Melide the last stretch of the Francés. It is not an easy arc. The next chapter says so plainly.
