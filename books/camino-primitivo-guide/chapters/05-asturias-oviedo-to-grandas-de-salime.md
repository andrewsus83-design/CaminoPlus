# Asturias: Oviedo to Grandas de Salime

## The character of these days

The first six days belong to Asturias. You leave a capital, cross farmland and low hills, and then climb into the mountains of the south-west, where villages are small, roofs are slate, and the Way spends hours on dirt and gravel tracks. The first four days run ~26 km, ~22 km, ~19 km and ~28 km, and then come two days of real mountain walking (~17 km, ~20 km).

The distances in this chapter come from the official Asturias tourism pages, which divide this part of the road into seven stages. Their seventh stage, from Grandas de Salime to the pass, begins the next chapter. Other sources give slightly different figures, as "The Way in brief" says.

### Day 1: Oviedo to Grado (~26 km)

The Way begins at the gates of the cathedral. The official pages describe it as a Gothic church with its Holy Chamber, the pre-Romanesque palatine chapel of Alfonso II, and a Romanesque carving of the Saviour on the south side of the transept, in front of which, they say, many pilgrims consider their journey to have begun. The cathedral and the Holy Chamber are on the UNESCO list.

You leave by the nineteenth-century streets, pass the old Provincial Hospice, and then the city is behind you. The pages call the day a route without major difficulties: the foothills of Mount Naranco, a steep descent between Llampaxuga and Lloriana, a climb to Taraniellu, a descent to Premoñu, and then a flat run into Grado. Most of it is on tarmac roads, so watch the traffic. In the villages look for the *hórreos* and *paneras*, the raised granaries, which are particularly plentiful at Peñaflor, and for small chapels such as those at Llampaxuga and Lloriana. The pages also point to the Roman baths of Santa Eulalia de Valdunu.

The road was a pilgrim road long ago. The official pages mention a hospital of San Juan founded in Oviedo in 1096, a few metres from the cathedral, and a hostel at L'Escampleru from the fourteenth century. In Grado they single out the chapel of Los Dolores, plain outside and richly Baroque within, and the Valdecarzana palace.

### Day 2: Grado to Salas (~22 km)

The first job of the day is the climb to the Fresno pass: ~300 m in ~4.5 km, steeper in the last stretch before the top. Then you drop to the River Narcea at Cornellana, and climb again, much more gently, to Salas.

Cornellana is the day's great stop. The monastery of San Salvador mixes Romanesque and Baroque, was one of the most important monasteries in the north of the peninsula according to the official pages, and is one of the sites UNESCO added to the list in 2015. The pages also place the battle of the Casas del Puente nearby, where Ramiro I won the throne of Oviedo from Nepociano. In Salas, the Gothic collegiate church of Santa María la Mayor holds the mausoleum of Fernando de Valdés, the inquisitor who founded the University of Oviedo. Most of the day is on asphalt or concrete, with about a third on dirt and gravel.

### Day 3: Salas to Tineo (~19 km)

An easier day, and a good one to listen to your legs. The climb is gradual, steeper at the start between Salas and Porciles, and then the profile settles. The surfaces are evenly mixed: tarmac verges, dirt and gravel.

Look at the roofs. The official pages say that as you pass from Salas into Tineo, slate slabs gradually replace tiles. They also note a pilgrims' establishment at La Espina documented as early as 883, which they call one of the oldest in the Jacobean world. (Another of their pages gives the same year, 883, for a hospital at Bourres. Sources are not tidy about the first hospital.)

Tineo was important on the road. The pages say King Alfonso IX ordered pilgrims to pass through it in 1222, and that its pilgrims' hospital is documented from the thirteenth century and stood until the nineteenth. This is also where you decide about tomorrow. Read "The high road and other ways to split the days" before you reach Tineo, and decide with the forecast in your hand.

### Day 4: Tineo to Pola de Allande (~28 km)

This is the official itinerary, and it is the lower road. It is long, and the official pages say it runs mostly on dirt and gravel tracks. You climb ~200 m in ~5 km to the Piedratecha pass, drop ~300 m in ~3 km, and then make a last climb at the border of Tineo and Allande, to Lavadoira, before the descent into Pola de Allande.

In between is slate country: small farming villages such as Vil.lal.luz, Vega del Rey, Campiel.lu and Bourres, meadows, woods and dry-stone walls. A short detour from the route, ~400 m according to one official page, leads to the monastery of Santa María la Real de Obona. In 1222 Alfonso IX ordered that it be an obligatory stop for pilgrims. At the end, the Cienfuegos palace stands on its hill above Pola de Allande, an example of rural Baroque.

The alternative, the high Hospitales road, leaves this route in the hills after Bourres. It does not pass Pola de Allande at all. If you take it, Asturias advises sleeping at Campiello or Bourres the night before.

### Day 5: Pola de Allande to Berducedo (~17 km)

A short day that the official pages call intense. For ~8 km you climb ~600 m to the Alto del Palo, through a natural setting of dense vegetation, streams and dry-stone walls, some of which hold back boulders that look ready to tumble onto the Way. The pages call it one of the most beautiful routes of the Asturian Jacobean roads. Almost all of it is dirt and gravel, and until you pass the summit only a few isolated farmhouses mark the way. Carry food and water for the whole climb.

At the top are the remains of the Roman reservoir that gave the pass its name, and a tale that witches held the last documented coven in Asturias there. After a sharp first descent the going is gentler, through Montefurado, Lago and Berducedo. Montefurado owes its name, says Asturias, to the gold mines the Romans opened, and the churches of Lago and Berducedo are flanked by centuries-old yew trees. This is where the Hospitales road rejoins, ~8 km before Berducedo.

### Day 6: Berducedo to Grandas de Salime (~20 km)

This is the day of the reservoir. You pass A Mesa and Buspol, where, the pages say, one of the oldest bells in the region is kept, then enter the pine plantations above the Salime reservoir. The views are the day's reward. Then comes the descent of ~8 km, in places very steep, which the official pages say was famous in the Middle Ages for its danger. The old village and bridge of Salime lie under the water. You cross the dam, which hides a mural by Joaquín Vaquero Turcios.

After the water comes a climb of more than ~5 km to Grandas de Salime, most of it along the shoulder of the AS-14 road. Take care here: walk facing the traffic, keep well to the edge, and do not walk this stretch in poor light. The collegiate church of San Salvador stands at the end. The Spanish Wikipedia adds that in 1222 Alfonso IX obliged pilgrims to pass through Grandas as well. The stage hardly passes through any villages, so carry what you need.
