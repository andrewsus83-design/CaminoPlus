# Is this Camino for you?

## The honest answer: it is demanding

The Primitivo has a name for being hard, and the sources agree. The Confraternity of St James calls it a hard route through the mountains, with a great deal of climbing and descending, though mainly on well-made paths, and with a fair amount of road walking. It adds that you should really have some other hill-walking experience before going this way. The app's catalog rates the route 4 on its scale of 1 to 5. It rates the Camino Francés 3 and the Camino Inglés 2.

The hard part is the first half. The official Asturias pages list ~600 m of climbing in ~8 km on the way to the Alto del Palo, and ~600 m in ~13 km up to the pass of Puerto del Acebo. They also describe long days: ~26 km on the first, ~28 km on the fourth. Across the border, the Xunta de Galicia rates none of its Galician stages above medium; the two it rates highest are San Romao da Retorta to Melide and the last day into Santiago. No official source gives a total ascent for the whole route, so this book gives none.

There is one more thing to weigh. The weather in these hills is a risk, not a detail. The Confraternity says it can be harsh even in summer, damp and cold, and can change very quickly. The chapter on body, weather and safety says what to do about it.

## Who it suits

- **Walkers with some hill experience**, or a long training build-up behind them, who are comfortable with climbs of several hundred metres and a few days over ~25 km.
- **Pilgrims who want a quieter road.** Wikipedia calls the Primitivo a popular alternative to the much more heavily travelled Camino Francés. Remember that the last ~53 km, from Melide, are the Francés.
- **People who like mountains for their own sake**: slate-roofed villages, dry-stone walls, a reservoir far below you, and days when the road crosses empty country.
- **Walkers who can plan.** The official pages describe some days as passing hardly any villages, so you will carry food and water, and you will want to know where you are sleeping before you start the long ones.

## Who should think twice

- If you have never walked several long days in a row, train first, or choose a gentler Way. The Camino Inglés (~120 km) has its own book in this series, and so does the Camino Francés.
- If you have a knee, hip or heart condition, ask a doctor before you commit to the climbs and descents.
- If your dates fall in winter, read "When to go" carefully. Snow and mist on the high sections change what is sensible.
- If you hate wet feet, accept that Asturias and Galicia are green for a reason.

None of this is a reason to be afraid. It is a reason to prepare, and many people do: about 24,400 finished the Primitivo in 2024.

## If the mountains are too much

You do not have to choose between the whole road and nothing. The high road over the Hospitales is optional: the lower road is the official one, and the chapter on the two roads explains the choice. You can also split the stages into shorter days: the Asturias pages say their seven stages can be subdivided as often as you like. And you can start later along the Way. About a third of the pilgrims who finished in 2024 started in Lugo, Wikipedia reports. If you start anywhere other than Oviedo, read the Pilgrim's Office rules for the Compostela, set out in the chapter on arriving, and ask the Office.

The Primitivo asks a lot of the body. Go at a pace that still lets you look up.
