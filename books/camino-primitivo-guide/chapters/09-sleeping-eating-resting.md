# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office lists access to "hostels offered by the Christian hospitality of the Way" as the first practical use of the credencial. It also notes that hostels without subsidies are kept going by what pilgrims contribute. Leave a place cleaner than you found it, and give what you can.

The Asturias tourism pages say that along their part of the road there are public hostels, private ones, and a variety of other lodging such as hotels and guesthouses. How each one works, whether you can book it, and whether it is open this month, changes, so ask, and call ahead on the days that pass few villages.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and do not rustle bags at night or before dawn. Pack your rucksack in the hall.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed, and your boots where the house puts them. Wet boots take time to dry in these hills.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. Somebody will snore, and if it is you, apologise at breakfast.
- Keep your valuables close.

**Bedbugs** can be a risk in any shared bed anywhere. Check the seams of the mattress, keep your pack and clothes off the bed, and if you find bites, tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. It is not a reason to avoid hostels, only a reason to look before you lie down.

If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. It is not cheating. After a mountain day, a good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

Many places along the Way offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what is on it, and what it costs, before you sit. A set meal is a good way to eat well on a long day, and an easy way to try what the region cooks.

Asturias calls its cuisine one of the region's great attractions. The tourism pages name *fabada*, a white-bean stew, as an emblematic dish, and natural cider as an iconic drink. The cider festival, the *espicha*, is one of the most common festivities on the Asturian calendar. In Galicia look for *polbo á feira*, the fair-style octopus, which Wikipedia calls a traditional Galician dish and the totemic food of the festivities of Lugo's patron saint. Near Arzúa there is cheese: Arzúa-Ulloa has Protected Designation of Origin. At the end of the road is the *tarta de Santiago*, the almond cake, which Wikipedia says has its origin in the Middle Ages and the Camino.

You do not need to eat all of it. Pick one local thing a day.

## Food and water in the mountains

The official pages describe the day from Pola de Allande as having only a few isolated farmhouses before the Alto del Palo, and the day to Grandas de Salime as hardly passing through any villages. On the Hospitales road there are no villages at all. On any such day, fill your bottles at the last village and carry more than you think you need. Carry lunch and something for the evening as well, because a small village's shop or cafe may be closed when you arrive. Do not drink from a stream or trough unless you know it is meant for people, and if a fountain is marked as unsuitable, believe the sign.

## Resting

The Primitivo is not a road to rush. A day of rest is part of the walk, and the road offers good places for it.

- **Oviedo**, before you start. The Confraternity of St James lists the cathedral, the Holy Chamber, and three pre-Romanesque churches on the edge of the city: San Julián de los Prados, Santa María del Naranco and San Miguel de Lillo.
- **Lugo**, after the long day over the hills. The Roman walls can be walked in an afternoon.
- **Santiago**, at the end. It deserves more than a night, and the chapter on arriving says why.

On the road, listen to your body before you listen to your plan. Take short days as short days. If something hurts in a way that grows rather than fades, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
