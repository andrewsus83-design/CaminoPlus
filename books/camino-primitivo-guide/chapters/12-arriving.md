# Arriving

## The last streets

The last descent into Santiago, past San Lázaro and through the old streets, ends at the cathedral. Wikipedia says that construction of the present building began in 1075 and that it was consecrated in 1211. It is a Romanesque church with Gothic and Baroque additions, built over the reputed tomb of St James. The Baroque façade on the Obradoiro square was completed by Fernando de Casas Novoa in 1740, and behind it the Romanesque Pórtico da Gloria of Master Mateo, carved, says Wikipedia, by 1188. The crypt below the high altar is where the pilgrims of the ninth-century church ended their journey.

Pilgrims' Masses are held in the cathedral. Their times change, so ask on the day. The *botafumeiro*, the great swinging censer, is used on certain important religious holidays and, Wikipedia says, at all Pilgrims' Masses in a Holy Year. 2027 is a Holy Year. The Holy Door is opened only in Holy Years, and the Xunta says that in a Holy Year the alternative way into the cathedral is the Holy Door at A Quintana.

## The Compostela

The Compostela is the certificate the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules. The Office owns the rules, and they can change. This book says what the Office says, and sends you to the Office in any doubt.

- The Compostela is for those who come to the tomb of the Apostle for religious and/or spiritual reasons and follow the routes of the Way of St James on foot, by bicycle or on horseback.
- It asks for at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred kilometres by bicycle, shown by the credencial, stamped along the route.
- On foot or on horseback, that means at least a hundred continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago that the Office recognises. The final stage must include at least the last one leading into the cathedral.
- You may walk the Way in stages, if they follow in chronological and geographical order. A pilgrim who walks only the minimum distance must get the credencial stamped at the start and at the end of each stage, with the date.
- A pilgrim who has already walked part of a recognised route outside Spain needs no less than seventy kilometres in Spain.
- The Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special and exceptional cases.

The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.

From Oviedo you have ~320 km ahead of you, far more than the last hundred kilometres the Office asks for. If your plan is anything other than walking the Way from Oviedo, or if you will walk it in stages, ask the Pilgrim's Office before you go. This book does not say whether any other start counts.

## At the Pilgrim's Office

The Office issues the Compostela, a document in Latin. You will need your stamped credencial. Procedures and hours change, so look at the Office's own website before you arrive, and do not plan a tight connection for the same day.

Whatever the Compostela means to you, it is a record of a road you walked, not a measure of it.

## The next day

Do not leave on the morning after you arrive. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it rewards a second day.

- **Rest.** After ~312 km your legs have earned it.
- **See the cathedral without a queue behind you.** Sit in the Obradoiro square, or at A Quintana, and watch the others arrive.
- **Think about what comes next.** If you want to keep walking, the Camino to Finisterre and Muxía, ~90 km, goes on from Santiago, and has its own book in this series.
- **Plan your way home** with the timetables in front of you, not at the last minute.
