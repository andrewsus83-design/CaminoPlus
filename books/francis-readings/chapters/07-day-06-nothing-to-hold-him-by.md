# Day 6. Nothing to hold him by

*What can the world catch you by?*

## The passage

> The good Bishop of Assisi expressed a sort of horror at the hard life which the Little Brothers lived at the Portiuncula, without comforts, without possessions, eating anything they could get and sleeping anyhow on the ground. St. Francis answered him with that curious and almost stunning shrewdness which the unworldly can sometimes wield like a club of stone. He said, "If we had any possessions, we should need weapons and laws to defend them." That sentence is the clue to the whole policy that he pursued. … There was nothing that the world could hold them by; for the world catches us mostly by the fringes of our garments, the futile externals of our lives. … A man had to be thin to pass always through the bars and out of the cage; he had to travel light in order to ride so fast and so far. … You could not put his head in a halter without the risk of putting it in a halo.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter VII, The Three Orders (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

Watch how a very small thing can hold a whole afternoon. A phone at five per cent. A sandal strap that has begun to rub. The time on a clock that says you are slower than you meant to be. None of these is large. Together they decide your mood for hours, and the mood decides how you see everything else. The world, as Chesterton puts it in this passage, "catches us mostly by the fringes of our garments".

The passage comes from the moment when Francis's little band is no longer only a few friends. First there was Bernard, a wealthy townsman, and Peter, a churchman, who followed Francis when most people, Chesterton guesses, took him for a madman. Then a man named Egidio, apparently a poor workman, made it four. Chesterton calls them the Little Brothers, and he says that is closer to the name Francis used than "Friars Minor". They lived at a small church called the Portiuncula, with no comforts, no property, and anything they could get to eat. The bishop of Assisi, a kind man, was horrified. Francis answered with a sentence Chesterton thinks says it all: "If we had any possessions, we should need weapons and laws to defend them."

It is a plain argument, and a practical one. What you own, you must protect. What you must protect, you will sooner or later fight over. A man with nothing to defend is hard to threaten. You could not starve a man who was already fasting, Chesterton says, or ruin one who was already a beggar. The brothers were meant to be like "little fishes who could go freely in and out of that net", and they could do it because there was nothing to snag. "A man had to be thin to pass always through the bars and out of the cage; he had to travel light in order to ride so fast and so far." The last line of the passage is the best of them: you could not put his head in a halter without risking a halo.

In short, the passage makes poverty a freedom instead of a sorrow. The Little Brothers were not poor because they disliked the world. They were poor so that they could go anywhere, among any kind of people, and not be held. One of the later Franciscans, Chesterton says, put it in a line: "A monk should own nothing but his harp." Keep the song, and lose the luggage.

Now say where it is hard. First, it is a vocation and not a rule for everyone. Chesterton says so himself: "there is a sense in which the ordinary man cannot be free from the world; or rather ought not to be free from the world." A parent with a child cannot travel light. A person caring for someone at home cannot, either. Those are ties to be proud of. Francis did not want all of Italy on the road. Chesterton says he foreshadowed a Third Order for people who stayed in their homes, meant to help them "to be ordinary with an extraordinary exultation". The lightness was for a few, and the spirit of it for everyone else.

Second, there is a trap in admiring the whole thing from a walker's point of view. A pilgrim's lightness is borrowed. The pack comes off at the end of the walk and the house, the shelves and the diary come back. To feel light for a week or two is not the same as to live light for life, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. Francis chose it, and in Chesterton's telling never turned back. You are allowed to simply enjoy the lightness for what it is.

One more detail shows how much the rule mattered. Chesterton says Francis "was only once seen angry; and that was when there was talk of an exception to the rule".

What it asks of you, on an ordinary day, is not a vow. It asks only that you find your own fringes: the small thing the world is using to hold you by the sleeve. A battery, a pace, a comparison with the walker ahead, a hunger that is bigger in the mind than in the stomach. Once you can name it, it often loosens. You do not have to throw it away. You only have to see that it is a fringe, and not the garment.

## For the walk

- What is holding my mood by the sleeve right now? Look for the small, specific thing, not the large one.
- Check the route when you need to, and then put the phone away for an hour. Let your hands be empty for part of it, and swing your arms a little. Notice what you reach for, and what that tells you. If you find you cannot manage an hour, take twenty minutes, and be pleased with those.

## At the end of the day

The pack comes open on the bed, and the whole of your life for the week fits across a bunk. Spare shirt, socks, a little soap, the things for your feet, a charger, a book you have not opened, perhaps a keepsake. It always looks smaller laid out than it felt on the shoulders. It is a good picture to keep. Most of what you needed today was in it, and most of what held you up was not.

Go back through the day for the fringes. Where did the world catch you? Perhaps in the ache of one foot, which became the whole day. Perhaps in a glance at the time, or a message that did not come, or a brief unkind sentence you rehearsed about someone. It is worth being gentle about this. Everyone has handles. The point is not to be free of all of them. It is to know a few by name.

There is another half of Chesterton's story that suits the evening. The Little Brothers were light, but they were never alone. A little later in the same chapter, he tells of a young friar who had got it into his head that Francis despised him. Francis went straight up to the young man, and said: "Be not troubled in your thoughts for you are dear to me". Then he asked him to come in confidence whenever he liked. That is the other side of travelling light: you must lean on someone.

So think of the company of the day. A walker who shared water, a joke in a queue, a host who found you a bed, a friend who sent a word from home. Which of them made the road lighter? You may not have told them. A word tonight, or tomorrow, costs nothing and is the one thing that cannot be left in the pack.

Chesterton's monk was allowed one possession, a harp. If you could keep one thing that has no use at all, something for the song and not the luggage, what would it be? A tune you hum without noticing, a habit of looking up at birds, a person you think of when you are tired. It does not have to be grand. It only has to be yours, and light enough to carry.

And if you walked alone and felt lonely at the thought of lightness, that is honest, and understandable. Light and lonely are not the same, but they can feel alike at nine in the evening.

In the evening the mind tends to hold on tighter than it did in the morning. Tired people cling, to a message, to tomorrow's plan, to the exact rank of their aches. If you notice that, you need not argue with it. Put the pack where you can reach it in the dark, set one alarm, and let the rest go until the light comes.

Questions for the bunk:

- What was holding my mood by the sleeve today?
- What do I really need from what I carry, and what is only fringe?
- Who made today's road lighter, and have I told them?

## A short prayer

*Teach me to travel light. Let nothing small hold me by the sleeve tomorrow. Thank you for the people who made the road shorter. Keep me free and keep me kind. Give me a bed, and give me sleep.*
