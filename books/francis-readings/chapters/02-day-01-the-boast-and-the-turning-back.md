# Day 1. The boast and the turning back

*What if the road you planned is not the road you are given?*

## The passage

> High in the dark house of Assisi Francesco Bernardone slept and dreamed of arms. There came to him in the darkness a vision splendid with swords, patterned after the cross in the Crusading fashion, of spears and shields and helmets hung in a high armoury, all bearing the sacred sign. When he awoke he accepted the dream as a trumpet bidding him to the battlefield, and rushed out to take horse and arms. … As he rode out to war the great gate in the deep wall of Assisi resounded with his last boast, "I shall come back a great prince." A little way along his road his sickness rose again and threw him. It seems highly probable, in the light of his impetuous temper, that he had ridden away long before he was fit to move. And in the darkness of this second and far more desolating interruption, he seems to have had another dream in which a voice said to him, "You have mistaken the meaning of the vision. Return to your own town." And Francis trailed back in his sickness to Assisi, a very dismal and disappointed and perhaps even derided figure, with nothing to do but to wait for what should happen next. It was his first descent into a dark ravine that is called the valley of humiliation, which seemed to him very rocky and desolate, but in which he was afterwards to find many flowers.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter III, Francis the Fighter (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

The first morning of a long walk has a temperature of its own. There is a plan in your pocket: so many kilometres, somewhere to eat at midday, a bed by evening. And there is a body that has not yet agreed to any of it. Many walkers go too fast on the first day, because the road is new and the legs feel full of promise. By the third hour the day begins to say what it thinks. On a road beside the Atlantic, the wind will have an opinion too.

Chesterton starts his saint's story in the same place, with a young man and his plan. He begins with the young man because he thinks the story is lost without him. Francis is the son of a cloth merchant in Assisi, popular with everybody, a leader of French fashions, with what Chesterton calls "his guileless swagger as a Troubadour". He throws money about and never quite understands what it is. His mother says, "He is more like a prince than our son." Chesterton insists that at this stage there was no outward sign of anything mystical in him at all. What Francis wanted was to be famous, as a poet and, more often in his thoughts, as a soldier.

So when he dreams of weapons laid up in a high hall, all marked with a cross, he takes it for a summons. The sentence about the gate of Assisi is the one to hear: as he rides out, the great gate "resounded with his last boast". Not a prayer, a boast. Then, a little way down the road, his old sickness throws him. Another dream, or a voice, tells him he has misread the first. He is sent home, "a very dismal and disappointed and perhaps even derided figure".

Say plainly what the passage says. A young man sets out with a story about how it will end. The road corrects the story. And the correction is not even an explanation. He is not told what the dream did mean. He is told only that he was wrong, and to go back and wait for what comes next.

Here is the part worth slowing down for. The last sentence is told from a distance. Francis later found "many flowers" in that valley, Chesterton says, but the one trailing back to Assisi does not know it. To him it is just a bad week. That is how most turnings feel from the inside. And it would be too easy to turn the passage into a promise that every setback hides a gift. It does not say that, and nor will this book. Sometimes a blister is only a blister. Sometimes a closed café is only a closed café. Chesterton can speak of flowers because he knows how the story ends. Nobody on the road today does.

One more thing, calmly. The dream is a dream of its century: swords "patterned after the cross in the Crusading fashion". Francis will turn away from that world, though not at once. In a later chapter Chesterton says his glory would lie not in overthrowing men in battle but in building. We keep the passage because of the road in it, not because of the swords.

So what might it ask of a day of walking? Two small things. The first is to carry the plan lightly. A plan is a good servant on a long walk, and a poor master. If the wind is against you, if your feet say stop, if a village turns out to be shut for lunch and a stranger points you somewhere else, the day may be amending the plan on purpose. The second is to be kind to the self that trails back. A short stage, an early stop, a rest day taken without a lecture: none of these is a failure. Francis did not choose his own descent into the "valley of humiliation" either.

And watch for the boast. Everyone sets out with a small one. It may sound like "I will do thirty kilometres by lunch", or like a picture of yourself arriving at the cathedral. You need not give it up. You need only notice it, and notice what the day does with it.

## For the walk

- What have I promised myself about this road, and which part of that promise is a boast? You need not answer aloud. Only be curious about the shape of it.
- For the first hour, walk a little slower than feels necessary. Each time you notice yourself speeding up, ask what you are hurrying toward, and then ease off by a step or two. If the answer is simply the end of the day, remember that it will arrive without any help from you.

## At the end of the day

Somewhere around now, the day you planned and the day you had can be set side by side. They are rarely the same. A shorter stage than you meant, a wrong turn, a hot stretch in the middle of the afternoon, a blister that arrived early: the road has been editing your plan since breakfast. This is a good hour to look at the edits without marking them.

Which did you resist? Think of the moment you most wanted the day to be different. Perhaps it was a long stretch beside the sea with the wind in your face, when you wished yourself anywhere else. Perhaps it was a place you reached later than you had hoped. What did you do with that wish: push on, sulk, laugh, sit down on a wall? There is no right answer, only something worth knowing about yourself.

Then look for the opposite. Was any part of the day better because it did not go to plan? A conversation you would have skipped, a bench you would never have chosen, a small kindness that only turns up when the timetable slips. If nothing comes to mind, that is honest too. Francis in this story gets no consolation on the road home. The consolation, such as it is, is left to a later chapter.

Chesterton's young man also went home looking foolish, and that part is easy to forget. Did you feel foolish today? A wrong turn that a whole café watched you make, a map you read upside down, a pack that came undone at the worst moment. It stings for an hour and then, as a rule, nobody remembers it but you. Francis had a whole town to face. You have, at most, a few strangers who are already thinking about their own feet.

It may also be worth asking about the boast. Not the big one, the small one: the number you were quietly proud of, or the pace you set to impress nobody in particular. Did the road take it from you, or did you hold on? Neither is a fault. A walk of many days has room for a good many corrections, and the early ones are the cheapest.

And a word about tiredness. The first evening of a long walk is a strange one. The body is surprised, the mind is still up in the morning's excitement, and the two do not speak to each other. If you feel low tonight, it may be the plain arithmetic of a first day, not a verdict on the whole walk. Eat something, drink water, wash your feet if you can, and sleep before you decide anything. Tomorrow's road will be a different road with the same legs, and legs are better after a night.

Questions for the end of the day:

- Where did today's road differ from today's plan, and how did I meet the difference?
- Was there a moment I walked faster than I needed to, and what was I trying to prove?
- What small thing did the slower or unplanned part of the day give me?

## A short prayer

*Bless the plan I made this morning, and the day that changed it. Let me hold both loosely. Take the boast out of my pace. If I was turned round today, let me not mind. Give me rest, and a morning to begin again.*
