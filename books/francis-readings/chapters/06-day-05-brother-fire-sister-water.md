# Day 5. Brother Fire, Sister Water

*What would it be to meet one thing at a time, and not the whole view?*

## The passage

> The phrase about his brotherhood with the sun and moon, and with the water and the fire, occurs of course in his famous poem called the Canticle of the Creatures or the Canticle of the Sun. … It is a supremely characteristic work, and much of St. Francis could be reconstructed from that work alone. … It was not for nothing that he called fire his brother, fierce and gay and strong, and water his sister, pure and clear and inviolate. … A friend of mine said that somebody was the sort of man who apologises to the cat. St. Francis really would have apologised to the cat. When he was about to preach in a wood full of the chatter of birds, he said, with a gentle gesture "Little sisters, if you have now had your say, it is time that I also should be heard." And all the birds were silent; as I for one can very easily believe.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter VI, The Little Poor Man (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

On the coast it is easy to see the sea and nothing else. A strip of grey or blue, a line where it meets the sky, a sound. That is "the view". The gull is part of it, the wave is part of it, the dune is only a texture on the edge. You could walk beside it all day and meet almost none of it.

Chesterton has a sentence that will catch any reader off guard, and it comes in the pages just before the passage. "St. Francis was not a lover of nature." He means something particular. A lover of nature, as he hears the phrase, is someone who likes the world as a pleasant background, a stage set marked "Scene; a wood". For Francis, he says, "nothing was ever in the background". A bird went by him "like an arrow", with a story and a purpose of its own. A bush "could stop him like a brigand". He did not, in Chesterton's phrase, call nature his mother. He "called a particular donkey his brother or a particular sparrow his sister".

That is the point of the passage, and of the song it talks about. The sun and moon, the water and the fire: in Francis's famous poem, the Canticle of the Creatures or of the Sun, they are brothers and sisters, one by one. Chesterton admires the sureness of it. Fire is a brother, "fierce and gay and strong", and water a sister, "pure and clear and inviolate". These are not decorations. They are distinctions, drawn by someone who really looked.

A word about the song itself. This book does not print it. Chesterton gives only pieces, such as the line from his first chapter, "Praised be God for our Sister, Mother Earth, which brings forth varied fruits and grass and glowing flowers". (Earth is a sister and a mother there, so his remark about nature and mothers is about a way of looking, not a count of words.) What we have here is Chesterton's English for Francis's words, and a portrait of the man who sang them. The whole Canticle is easy to find in many translations, and it is short enough to read on a bench.

Say what the passage says, plainly. Francis treated every creature as someone, and treated someone with courtesy. Chesterton says he would have apologised to the cat. When he came to preach in a wood full of chattering birds, the story goes, he said, "Little sisters, if you have now had your say, it is time that I also should be heard." And the birds, Chesterton says, were silent, "as I for one can very easily believe".

Now the doubt. Plenty of readers will not believe the birds fell silent, and Chesterton does not need them to. A few lines later he calls much of Francis's way with animals "a sort of symbolic joke, a pious pantomime", a way of showing that he "not only loved but reverenced God in all his creatures". The legend is a bow, in other words, and you can take the bow without taking the legend as news. Chesterton's smile in that last clause is part of it.

There is a second difficulty, and it is a practical one on a coast. The elements in the song are not all gentle. Fire is "fierce". The sea is cold and large and does not care who you are. The route guide in the app names wind and sun as the main challenge on this road, and the sun, whom Francis called a brother, can burn. Courtesy to the elements does not mean carelessness with them. Carry water, cover your head and your neck, take shade when it is offered, and rest earlier than your pride suggests. It is a form of manners to arrive in one piece.

On the road it asks for something very small: to choose one thing, and meet it as itself. Not "the sea" but this wave, this one, coming in. Not "birds" but that gull standing on one leg on a post, who has been standing there since before you came up the path. It does not need a speech. It needs a minute of attention, and perhaps a quiet nod, as a stranger nods to another.

You will find that a thing looked at in this way changes. It stops being scenery and becomes an event. That is all Chesterton is claiming for Francis: not that he loved the world in general, but that he met it in particular, which is harder and, for a walker, a good deal more interesting.

## For the walk

- What is one thing on this road that I could look at today as if it were not scenery?
- Choose one living thing, a gull, a tree, a lizard on a wall, a dog on a doorstep that you watch from a respectful distance, and give it a full minute of attention. If you like, greet it by a name of your own making. It is a courtesy, not a belief, and no one is checking. Do not touch wild creatures, and keep away from animals you do not know.

## At the end of the day

By evening there is salt on your skin and sand in places you did not know had places. It is a good moment to ask what, out of the whole day's view, you actually met.

Perhaps nothing, and the day went by as a long stretch of "the view". That is the usual way, and there is no reproach in it. Or perhaps one thing stopped you: a bird, a gust, a trick of the light on wet sand, a dog that looked at you and decided you were fine. Go back to it for a moment. What was it like? What did it do?

Water and fire are good guides for the evening. Water: what did it do for you today? The first drink, a wash at the end, the sound of the sea, the cold of a tap. Fire: the heat of the sun on your neck, a stove, a candle, a warm cup in the hands. In the pages after the passage, Chesterton tells how, near the end of his life, when a red-hot iron was about to be brought close to Francis's failing eyes, he rose and spoke to the fire: "Brother Fire, God made you beautiful and strong and useful; I pray you be courteous with me." It is a startling moment, and Chesterton calls it a masterpiece of "the art of life". It is also a reminder that courtesy to the world does not require the world to be kind. It is something you offer, whatever comes.

The one element left out of the list is earth, the ground itself, which Francis's song calls both a sister and a mother. Your feet will have a good deal to say about it tonight. Sand that slides, boards that ring, cobbles that bruise, a hard shoulder of tarmac, a soft patch of grass that was a gift. Every kind of ground speaks differently to the sole of a foot. It held you up for hours and asked for nothing. That is worth a thought before the shoes come off, and perhaps a quiet word of thanks as you take them off.

If today's weather was harsh, wind, glare, or a long grey drizzle, you might think of it that way. Not as an enemy, and not as a friend, but as something with a character, which you can meet with manners. Your sunburn, if you have one, is not a moral failure. Take care of it, and be glad of the sun at the same time.

A word on tiredness. After a long day the mind goes flat, and a thing like a gull ceases to be interesting. Do not try to be moved. If you can manage one clear minute at the window, with whatever is outside it, that is plenty.

Questions for tonight:

- What one thing did I truly meet today, and not merely pass?
- Where did I speak to something, a bird, a dog, the wind, even as a joke, with some courtesy?
- What in today's weather was hard, and could I take it as a character and not an enemy?

## A short prayer

*For the sun and the wind and the water, for the gull on the post, and the sand in my shoes, thank you. Teach me to meet each thing as itself. Let tomorrow's wind be gentle with me, and let me be gentle with what I meet. Keep me from burns and blisters, and let the night be calm.*
