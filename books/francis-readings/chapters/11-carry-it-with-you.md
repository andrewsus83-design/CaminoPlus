# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the nine days, or the end of the road, or both. This book gives no certificate. It was meant as company for a coast road, and a road has no use for a book once the land runs out, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep.

Not everything. Nobody carries a whole book home. Choose three small things, one of each kind.

- **A direction.** Chesterton's Francis took "the queerest and most zigzag short cuts through the wood", and was "always going home". Yours may be a different word. Notice which way you lean when nothing is deciding for you, and give it a little room when you are back. One evening this week, go out for no errand, and walk until you find yourself turning for home.
- **A line.** One sentence from the nine days that stayed with you. It might be the plainest, "there is no way in which a man can earn a star or deserve a sunset", or the funniest, that a man could not be put in a halter without the risk of a halo. Write it on a card and put it where you will see it.
- **A practice.** One of the small things from the end of each morning: a quiet thank you before the second sip of water, an hour with empty hands, a full minute with a single gull, a turn round to look back along the road. Pick the one that cost you least and helped most. Do it again tomorrow.

If you can keep all three for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from a week away.

## How to go on

Go back whenever you like. The days are short, and they were written to be read more than once. A day that did nothing for you on the road may say a good deal on a grey morning in March. Read them in another order. Read only the evenings for a week. Read the prayers on a night you cannot sleep.

You might also read Chesterton himself. *St. Francis of Assisi* is about 44,000 words, it is in the public domain, and you will find it at Project Gutenberg, ebook number 63084. You will meet what we left out: his sweeping chapter on the old world, his quarrels with the sceptics of his day, his long defence of the miracles, and the sentences about other faiths that we warned you of at the start. Some of it you will love, and some of it may make you wince. Both are fair responses to a book written by a very quick and very certain man a hundred years ago.

And you might look for Francis's own song, the Canticle of the Creatures, which Chesterton quotes only in pieces. It is short, and it is better heard than described. Read it once aloud, outside if you can.

Be honest, too, about what did not work. Some days will have rubbed. A passage may have asked for more than you had, or sounded too sure, or too far from your life. That is allowed. A book like this is not a test. If a day did not speak to you, nothing has gone wrong. Leave it, and take what did.

## A last word

Chesterton ends his book by admitting how little he has to offer. Under the overwhelming arches of "such a temple of time and eternity", he says, he has nothing to set up "but this brief candle burnt out so quickly before his shrine". A book of nine days is a smaller candle still. If it gave you a little light on a stretch of road, or on a night in a bunk, it did what it was for.

If you are still beside the sea, look at it once more before you go. If you are at home, find the nearest open sky, and stand under it for a minute. Then walk again, next year if you can, or tomorrow, around the block, with your pockets empty.

*Buen Camino. Bom caminho.*
