# Day 3. Expecting nothing, enjoying everything

*Can a gift be enjoyed before it is earned?*

## The passage

> It is commonly in a somewhat cynical sense that men have said, "Blessed is he that expecteth nothing, for he shall not be disappointed." It was in a wholly happy and enthusiastic sense that St. Francis said, "Blessed is he who expecteth nothing, for he shall enjoy everything." It was by this deliberate idea of starting from zero, from the dark nothingness of his own deserts, that he did come to enjoy even earthly things as few people have enjoyed them; and they are in themselves the best working example of the idea. For there is no way in which a man can earn a star or deserve a sunset. … The great painter boasted that he mixed all his colours with brains, and the great saint may be said to mix all his thoughts with thanks. All goods look better when they look like gifts.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter V, Le Jongleur de Dieu (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

There is a particular water you drink on a long walk, after hours without it. It is not better water than the water at home. It is only that, for once, you have stopped assuming it. The first mouthful arrives like news. The shade of a single tree does the same, and so does a bench, and so does the moment a stretch of road finally turns toward the place where you will sleep.

Chesterton has a name for that feeling, and he finds it at the root of Francis's whole life. In the passage he sets two sayings side by side. The first is the cynic's: "Blessed is he that expecteth nothing, for he shall not be disappointed." It is a way of keeping your guard up. Expect nothing, and nothing can hurt you. Then the saint's version, which changes one clause: "Blessed is he who expecteth nothing, for he shall enjoy everything." Same starting point, opposite road. Where the cynic withdraws, Francis opens his hands.

Chesterton gives the saying to Francis. We have not traced the saying beyond his book, so take it as his report. What is clearer is what he does with it. He says it comes from starting at zero, from "the dark nothingness of his own deserts", and he puts it down in the plainest terms: "there is no way in which a man can earn a star or deserve a sunset". Nobody ever paid for the evening light on the water. The only way to have it is to be given it. And a few lines later he adds a thought which is almost a recipe: "the less a man thinks of himself, the more he thinks of his good luck". It is hard to be amazed while you are busy keeping accounts.

Look at his two examples, too: a star and a sunset. He does not choose a meal or a bed, which can at least be bought. He chooses the things nobody can pay for, or carry off, or put a fence round. The sky is the one gift on the road that is handed to everyone on the same terms, the hurried and the slow, the rich and the penniless.

Notice where this joy comes from in his telling. It does not come from a comfortable life. It comes from the opposite: from the winter woods of yesterday, from a man who has just been stripped of everything he had. Chesterton is sure of one fact, and says it flatly: Francis "certainly was ascetical and he certainly was not gloomy". A hard life and a glad one, in the same man, at the same time. That is the oddity this book is going to keep running into.

Say it plainly: the passage says that joy is easier to find from the bottom than from the middle. When you expect nothing, every small thing arrives as a gift. And all goods, Chesterton adds, look better when they look like gifts.

Now the hard part. "Expecting nothing" is not easy on a bad day. The albergue is full, the café has shut, a blister has opened, the wind has been in your face since morning, and a sentence about enjoying everything may sound like a joke at your expense. It is not a feeling you can order. It is closer to a habit of looking, and habits come slowly. Nor does it mean that you should expect nothing from other people or put up with whatever you are given. A bed that was booked is owed, and you may say so. Francis's phrase is about his own deserts, not anyone else's duties.

And there is a deeper catch for some readers. Chesterton believed that thanks needs somebody to thank. In a sentence we left out of the passage, just before the painter and his colours, Chesterton quotes Rossetti, who said that the worst moment for an atheist is when he is truly thankful and has nobody to thank. You may not share that. You may thank the day, or the sea, or no one at all. The practice does not seem to depend on the address.

In practice it asks something very small: to receive. Not to be grateful on command, not to list ten blessings with your jaw set, but to notice the moment when something arrives that you did not pay for, and to let it arrive. The sun on the water. A path laid by somebody, and kept by somebody else. A stranger's shorthand directions. The first cold mouthful of water.

On the coast there will be plenty of that to receive, and plenty of its opposite: wind, glare, sand in the boots. Both are part of the walk. Francis, in Chesterton's picture, did not enjoy the one by denying the other.

## For the walk

- What have I been treating as owed to me today? Start with small things, like a dry path or a free seat, and see how many there are.
- Each time you drink water on the road, say a quiet thank you before the second sip. To whom is up to you. See how many times the day gives you the chance, and whether the water tastes any different by evening. If you pass a person who is working to keep the road in order, a gardener, a sweeper, a driver slowing for you, include them.

## At the end of the day

Food tastes of the day that went before it. A plain bowl of soup after a long road is, as a rule, a better meal than anything ordered on an easy afternoon, and the reason is the same one Francis gives: you had stopped expecting it. Tonight is a good time to look at the day through that single window.

Start with what you did not earn. A cool breeze at the right hour. Someone who pointed the way without being asked. A bench where you did not expect one. The road itself, which somebody laid and somebody keeps. Make the list slowly, in your head, and notice how the list grows once you start. There is no exam at the end.

Then widen the list to people. Somebody made the bed you will sleep in tonight, and somebody will clean the shower you used. Someone grew, cooked or carried the food. A village is a home before it is a stop on a route, and its people give a great deal that no guidebook mentions. If you can, say a word of thanks before you sleep. A small one, said plainly, goes a long way.

If you passed any water today, a river, a harbour, the open sea, ask whether you really looked at it. Nobody charged admission, and nobody can send a bill for the light on it. A gift of that kind keeps no accounts, which makes it very easy to forget, and very easy to enjoy once you remember. A minute at the window before bed is enough.

Then look at the other side. Was there something you treated as owed today? A seat, a quiet morning, a clean bathroom, a pleasant word from a stranger? It is natural. Most of us walk with an invisible bill for the world. When it goes unpaid, we feel cheated. It is useful, only now and then, to see the bill, and to ask who exactly wrote it.

Gratitude has an awkward feature: it refuses to come on demand. Tiredness is the great enemy of it. When your feet hurt and your head is thick, a list of blessings can feel like a lecture. If that is tonight, put the list down. Do not manufacture a feeling. Sleep is a form of thanks as well, and perhaps the most honest one, and the list will still be there in the morning, looking fresher.

Chesterton says the saint "may be said to mix all his thoughts with thanks". Saintliness is not required for a spoonful. One thing, tonight, that was a gift. One word of thanks, aloud or under your breath, to whomever you like.

Questions for tonight:

- What did I treat as owed to me today, and what was in fact a gift?
- What small thing did I enjoy today that I had not expected?
- If I could thank one person or thing from today, who or what would it be?

## A short prayer

*For water, for shade, for the road beneath my feet, and for whoever made it, thank you. I did not earn the light on the sea, and I need not. Keep my hands open. Let me sleep thankful, or simply sleep.*
