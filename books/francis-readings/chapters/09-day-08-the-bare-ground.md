# Day 8. The bare ground

*What is it to lie down at the end?*

## The passage

> He who had become a vagabond for the sake of a vision, he who had denied himself all sense of place and possession, he whose whole gospel and glory it was to be homeless, received like a Parthian shot from nature, the sting of the sense of home. He also had his *maladie du clocher*, his sickness of the spire; though his spire was higher than ours. "Never," he cried with the sudden energy of strong spirits in death, "never give up this place. If you would go anywhere or make any pilgrimage, return always to your home; for this is the holy house of God." … After he had taken farewell of some of his nearest and especially some of his oldest friends, he was lifted at his own request off his own rude bed and laid on the bare ground; as some say clad only in a hair-shirt, as he had first gone forth into the wintry woods from the presence of his father. It was the final assertion of his great fixed idea; of praise and thanks springing to their most towering height out of nakedness and nothing. … But at least in the passages and porches of the Portiuncula there was a sudden stillness, where all the brown figures stood like bronze statues; for the stopping of the great heart that had not broken till it held the world.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter IX, Miracles and Death (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

There comes a point on most long afternoons when you would give a good deal to lie flat on the ground. Not to sleep. Only to be held up by something that asks nothing of you. The back lets go, the shoulders drop, the sky is very large. For a minute the whole business of getting somewhere is suspended, and the earth does the work.

Chesterton brings his story of Francis to rest in that posture. Today's passage is the last scene of a life, and it is told gently. Francis is dying. He is not yet fifty, Chesterton says, and worn out. He has been carried from place to place, and then, as the procession comes near Assisi, something lifts him. He sees "afar off on the Assisian hill" the pillars of the Portiuncula, the small church where his brotherhood began. And the man who made homelessness his glory, Chesterton says, receives "like a Parthian shot from nature, the sting of the sense of home". He calls it a sickness of the spire, a homesickness that fastens on a church tower, and adds that Francis's spire was higher than ours.

What Francis says next is the line a pilgrim reader will stop at: "If you would go anywhere or make any pilgrimage, return always to your home; for this is the holy house of God." Say it carefully. He is speaking of the Portiuncula, which Chesterton calls his home. He is not telling anybody to go back to their own front door. Chesterton finds the whole moment "profoundly pathetic, and full of great problems" (he means moving, not feeble): a man who spent his life leaving feels, at the very end, the pull of a place.

Then he is lifted from his bed and laid, at his own request, on the bare ground. Chesterton says it was "the final assertion of his great fixed idea; of praise and thanks springing to their most towering height out of nakedness and nothing". The idea is the one you met in the winter woods on the second day, and it comes round again. He ends as he began: with nothing, and with thanks.

The last stretch of the passage is quiet. The brothers stand round him in their brown habits, and in "a sudden stillness" they look like bronze statues. The great heart stops. It was a heart, Chesterton says in the sentence that closes the scene, "that had not broken till it held the world".

The song comes into this, though not in these lines. In the first chapter Chesterton remarks that the famous song of praise, the one that names the sun and the earth, ends almost with the words "Praised be God for our Sister, the death of the body." So the song's closing praise goes to death, and calls it a sister. It is one of the strangest and most beautiful things in the story, and it should not be tidied.

It also has to be said, honestly, that for many people death is not a sister. It is a loss, and some who walk the Camino carry it with them. Chesterton does not pretend otherwise on this page. He writes of the long day wearing on, and of how "that great darkness descended in which we all lost a friend". A reader who has lost someone is not asked to feel brotherly about it today. Nobody should be hurried into praise.

What this asks of a day is almost nothing grand. A day, too, ends, and a day can be ended well or badly. There is a plain wisdom in lying down at the end of it with something said: a thanks, a forgiveness, a task honestly left for tomorrow. Francis did it on the largest scale there is. You can do it on a bunk. Nothing in the passage requires you to be a saint. It asks only that you notice that the ground holds you, and that you are allowed to be held.

And the other line, the one about home. If you are near the end of your own walk, or reading this at home after one, "return always to your home" may sound comforting or may not. Homes are complicated. For some of us the word means a table with people at it, and for others a flat with a quiet hall. Francis's home was a small church and a group of friends. What is yours?

## For the walk

- What would I want to have said thank you for before this day is over?
- The next time you sit down, on a bench or a wall or the grass, put your hand flat on whatever is holding you. If the ground is dry and the place is safe, take your weight off your feet for three slow breaths. Feel what carries you, and let it.

## At the end of the day

Lying down is the oldest way of ending a day, and tonight you will do it without thinking. The body is flat, the eyes close, and for a few minutes you are held up by a mattress or a bunk or a floor, much as Francis was held up by the ground. It is worth noticing how completely the body trusts it.

This is also the hour to ask what has finished today. A day does not come back. The stage you walked is behind you, the people you met are scattered, the weather has moved on. Is there anything you would like to say before it closes? A thank you not yet spoken, a small sorry, a message that has been waiting since lunchtime. It is not a will, and nothing here is final. It is only the small custom of leaving a day tidy.

Francis, in Chesterton's picture, praised from nakedness and nothing. Tonight's version is far smaller, and also real. Look at what you actually have in the room: a bed, a blanket, a cup of water, shoes lined up by the wall, a pack with everything you need. It is not nothing, and it is not much. It is a fair place to say thank you from, and the thanks need not be long. "Enough" is a whole sentence.

Some people walk the Way in grief for someone who has died. If that is you, tonight may be heavy, and the passage may have been hard to read. You need not make anything of it. The prayer below can be said for them, with or without belief. If there is a name you would like to say aloud, the room is probably quiet enough.

And a thought about home, since the passage raised it. Who knows where you are tonight, and who is thinking of you? Not everyone has someone, and it is as well to say so. If you do, a short message might do both of you good. If you do not, you may want to be that person for somebody else.

In the evening the body asks for very little, and it is wise to listen: water, something to eat, feet up if there is a way. The heaviest thoughts are often only tired thoughts, and they look different in the morning. If one of them does not, it is a thought to share with someone, not to carry alone. And if sleep will not come, lie still anyway. Lying quietly with the eyes closed and the breathing slow is also a kind of rest, and it counts.

Questions for lying down:

- What would I like to have said thank you for before this day ends?
- Is there anyone I am carrying with me on this walk, living or gone, and what would I say to them tonight?
- What does "home" mean to me this evening: a place I am returning to, or one I am leaving?

## A short prayer

*For this day, now finished, thank you. For the ground that held me up, thank you. Let me lie down without fear and wake without hurry. For those I carry with me, living and gone, give peace. Let me be at home tonight, wherever I am.*
