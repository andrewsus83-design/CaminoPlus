# Day 2. Everything that can be called his

*What would you give back, to be free to go on?*

## The passage

> He stood up before them all and said, "Up to this time I have called Pietro Bernardone father, but now I am the servant of God. Not only the money but everything that can be called his I will restore to my father, even the very clothes he has given me." And he rent off all his garments except one; and they saw that that was a hair-shirt. He piled the garments in a heap on the floor and tossed the money on top of them. Then he turned to the bishop, and received his blessing, like one who turns his back on society; and, according to the account, went out as he was into the cold world. Apparently it was literally a cold world at the moment, and snow was on the ground. A curious detail, very deep in its significance, I fancy, is given in the same account of this great crisis in his life. He went out half-naked in his hair-shirt into the winter woods, walking the frozen ground between the frosty trees; a man without a father. He was penniless, he was parentless, he was to all appearance without a trade or a plan or a hope in the world; and as he went under the frosty trees, he burst suddenly into song.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter IV, Francis the Builder (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

Every morning starts with the same small judgement: the pack is lifted, and the shoulders give their verdict. You may find, by the second or third day, that a few things in it have not earned their weight. A third book. A second pair of shoes. A worry that was never packed on purpose and still got in. Walking is a slow audit, and it asks, kilometre by kilometre, what you actually need.

Francis's audit, in Chesterton's telling, was not slow. After the turning back of yesterday there are dark, aimless days, and he spends them praying in a neglected church in Assisi. There he hears a voice say: "Francis, seest thou not that my house is in ruins? Go and restore it for me." He does what he always does, which is to go at once. He sells his horse. Then he sells several bales of his father's cloth, making the sign of the cross over them to show where the money is going. Chesterton does not soften it: "In the coarse conventional language of the uncomprehending world, he stole."

The father is not a villain in this, only a man who finds his stock gone. He locks his son up. Francis hides, and Chesterton calls it "his blackest moment; the whole world had turned over; the whole world was on top of him". Then comes the hearing at the bishop's court, where the bishop, sensibly, tells him to give the money back. And in front of everyone, Francis does something more than that.

Say what the passage says, plainly. Francis does not argue. He gives the money back, and then he goes on, and gives back "everything that can be called his". That includes the clothes his father has given him, and he strips them off in public, down to a rough shirt of hair. He leaves in the snow with nothing: no father, no money, no trade, no plan. And then, Chesterton says, he bursts into song.

The passage is hard, and it is worth saying where. A son humiliates his father in front of the whole town, and the sentence "a man without a father" is cold. This is not a model for how to treat anyone's parents. Chesterton is not fair to the father in every line, but he grants, in another chapter, that "there may have been something to be said for Peter Bernardone". Someone reading this may have a father or a mother on their mind this week, perhaps with grief, perhaps with an old quarrel. The passage does not ask anybody to break with their family. It describes one man, one winter, and one very particular call.

What it can ask of the rest of us is smaller and more practical. Look at what you carry that was only handed to you. A title, a reputation, a way of being the sensible one, a set of expectations you never agreed to. On the road much of that may be weightless, because the people beside you do not know your job or your surname. For a few days you are a person with a pack. It is possible to notice, in that lightness, which of the burdens you carry at home were issued by other people, and which you chose.

Notice, too, what comes next in Chesterton's story. After the great renunciation there is no theory. There is work. "He went about by himself collecting stones." And the thought he comes to is a plain one: "The way to build a church is to build it." Letting go is not the end of the day's task. It makes room for the next small task, which is usually to put one foot in front of the other.

One last thing, and it is the odd one. Francis goes out into the cold and sings. The song is not a victory. It is more like the sound a body makes when a weight comes off it. You may know this on a small scale: the first minute after the pack is lowered at a rest stop, when your shoulders do not seem to belong to you. If there is a song in that minute, let it be a short one.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying that was handed to me and never chosen? Think of the pack, and then of the rest.
- At your next rest, take the pack off and lift it with one hand. Name one thing in it you carry from habit, and one you carry because somebody else expected it. You need not put either down. Only know which is which, and see whether that changes how the straps feel.

## At the end of the day

The straps come off, and for a few minutes you can feel exactly how heavy the pack was. There is a strange lightness in the shoulders, as if they had been lent to someone else all day and returned. It is the best moment to ask what else was carried today besides the pack.

Some of it may have been very small: a message you were putting off, a conversation you kept replaying, a judgement about how you were doing compared with the people you met. Some of it may have been heavier. A family that is far away, or near, or gone. A job you will go back to. Nobody here is asking you to drop any of it. The only question is which of it you were carrying out of habit, as a man might hold a coat he has stopped wearing.

Not everything that was handed to you is a burden, and the passage should not be read as if it were. A pair of boots that someone chose for you, a blessing at the door, a recipe, a name: gifts are also things that were given. Francis gave back even the clothes his father had provided. You do not have to do the same. Keep what was given in love, and keep it with thanks. The question is only which things you are holding by habit, and which by love.

Then there is the song. Francis, in this story, leaves in the snow and sings. Perhaps something in you lifted today for no clear reason: a downhill, a smell of bread, a stranger's laugh from across a square. Perhaps nothing did, and the day was only long. After a hard stretch relief does not always sound like music. Sometimes it is just quiet, and that is also welcome.

Taking off the day's clothes is a small stripping of its own, and it is worth a moment. The sweat-stiff shirt, the socks that have walked all afternoon, the hat that kept the sun off. A wash, a clean shirt, a pair of dry feet. It is an ordinary comfort, and an easy one to overlook. Francis went out into the cold with almost nothing, and the clean shirt tonight is a long way from that, which is a good reason to enjoy it.

A word on tiredness. Letting go costs energy, and the body does not always know the difference between putting down a worry and picking up a stone. If your head is full tonight, you have not failed at anything. Choose one small thing to be rid of until morning. A wet sock hung up to dry will do. It is surprising how much a small act of putting down can help the larger one.

Questions for tonight:

- What did I carry today that was handed to me and never chosen?
- What could I set down for this one night without losing anything I need?
- Where, if anywhere, did something in me lift today for no clear reason?

## A short prayer

*Let me set down tonight what is not mine to carry. Keep me kind to the people who gave it to me. When the cold comes, let a small song be found in me. If it does not come, let the quiet be enough. Let me sleep light on my shoulders.*
