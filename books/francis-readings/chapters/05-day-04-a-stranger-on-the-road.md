# Day 4. A stranger on the road

*Who is the stranger coming up the road, and who are you to the next one?*

## The passage

> I mean by the result the real man as he was after his first formative experiences; the man whom men met walking about on the Italian roads in his brown tunic tied with a rope. For that man, saving the grace of God, is the explanation of all that followed; men acted quite differently according to whether they had met him or not. … There is no answer except that Francis Bernardone had happened; and we must try in some sense to see what we should have seen if he had happened to us. In other words, after some groping suggestions about his life from the inside, we must again consider it from the outside; as if he were a stranger coming up the road towards us, along the hills of Umbria, between the olives or the vines.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter VI, The Little Poor Man (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

On a long straight stretch, a boardwalk or a beach or a field track, you can watch someone come toward you for several minutes. At first they are only a shape. Then a hat, a pack, a pace. You cannot tell if they are a pilgrim or a neighbour, and you cannot tell if they are in a good mood. A few seconds before you pass, you both do the same small thing: look up, nod, say *Buen Camino*, and the shape becomes a person for the length of a sentence.

Chesterton asks us to do that with Francis. He has just spent a chapter guessing at Francis from the inside, with the dreams and the cold and the song, and now he stops and says it is time to see the man from the outside. Not the saint in a painting, but a stranger coming up the road "along the hills of Umbria, between the olives or the vines". His reason is odd and bold. If we want to know why a movement swept across Europe, he says, there is no answer except that "Francis Bernardone had happened". And so we should "see what we should have seen if he had happened to us". The people who met him, he adds, "acted quite differently according to whether they had met him or not". The explanation, in other words, is a face under a hood.

What would the face have looked like? Chesterton sketches him elsewhere, and the sketch is not romantic. A small, quick man in a brown tunic tied with a rope. The rope, he says, was snatched up almost at random because it lay nearby, and the clothes were the oldest a peasant could spare. Francis begged for his bread, and chose the worst: "the beggar would eat the best he could get and the saint ate the worst he could get." A traveller from a more comfortable world might have crossed the road.

And yet, says Chesterton, nobody who looked into "those brown burning eyes" came away feeling like a crowd member. Each man felt that Francis was truly interested in him, in his own life from beginning to end. Chesterton says the only outward form that can carry such a feeling is courtesy. Francis moved among men like "one courtier, moving among a hundred kings". He honoured everyone he met: he loved them, and also respected them. That is a rarer thing than it sounds.

There is a second side to his courtesy, and it is the more surprising one. He asked for things as politely as he gave them. Chesterton says he could beg so confidently for food because he "really did regard every house as the house of a friend". Any pilgrim who has knocked at a door at the end of a day will see how much trust that takes, and how much it puts on the house. It is a compliment to the people who open the door, and also a request.

Say it plainly. The passage asks you to picture a poor man on the road and to ask what meeting him did. It claims that something began with an encounter, long before it was an argument or a rule or an order.

The hard part comes from the word "poor". Chesterton is a romantic about Francis, and a pilgrim is in danger of being romantic about poverty, too. A pack, a hostel bunk and a simple supper are a mild, chosen version of it. The people you pass are not all in that position. In another chapter Chesterton himself notes the difference, saying that the churchmen of Francis's day pitied men entering poverty "of their own free will", while the poor of his own day were "daily driven by cold coercion and moved on by the police". It is a fair thing to remember when you walk through a village. Its people are not scenery or lessons. Whatever your pack weighs, you are not living their life.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? To be a better stranger. Everyone on the road is somebody's stranger. You come up a path toward a farmer, a woman at a gate, a child on a wall, a tired walker sitting on a kerb, and for a moment you are the whole of what they know about pilgrims. Francis's way of doing it was attention, with nothing added. A look that says you are not a statistic. A greeting that treats the other person as somebody who matters.

You will not manage it every time. A hot afternoon is not a good one for courtesy. But it is possible to try once, with one person, and to give them your whole attention for the length of a hello.

## For the walk

- If a stranger met me for one minute today, what would they think they had met? Try not to flatter yourself, and not to scold yourself either.
- Greet the next few people you pass with your eyes as well as your voice, and give each of them your whole attention for the length of the greeting: no glance at the phone, no thinking of the next sentence. Notice whether anything changes, in them or in you. If a greeting is not returned, let it go without remark.

## At the end of the day

The people of the day arrive in the mind in no particular order. A face at a counter, a voice calling from a doorway, a walker who passed you twice in the afternoon, a hand lifted from a car window. Some you spoke to, and some you only saw. Tonight you can lay them out for a minute and look at them properly, which you could not do while you were moving.

Then turn it round. For each of those people, you were also somebody coming up the road. What did you look like? Perhaps tired, perhaps glad, perhaps bent over your phone, perhaps humming. You do not have to guess what they thought. It is enough to know that you were part of their day, as they were part of yours, and that a day is partly made of strangers.

If someone asked you for help today, money or a seat or a moment of your time, you will have had your own answer. This reading does not tell you what it should have been. It only invites you to look at it honestly: what you did, and what you felt as you did it. Chesterton's Francis ran after a beggar once, and swore never to refuse a poor man. Most of us cannot keep a vow like that, and it is better to know exactly what we can keep.

Think too about what you gave and what you were given. Directions, perhaps, or a word of warning about a steep stretch, or the loan of a pen. A hand with a gate. On the other side: a seat offered, a ripe fruit, a sentence in a language you half understand. Francis, in Chesterton's picture, was as ready to receive as to give. You may find the exchange on a road is more even than you think.

It may be worth asking where you were not attentive. A moment when somebody spoke and you were already looking past them. A place where you took a photograph of something that was also somebody's front door. Nobody is a saint on a hot afternoon. But Chesterton's point is that courtesy is a form of seeing, and seeing is a habit, and a habit can be started on a Tuesday evening in a quiet room.

And if you walked alone and met almost nobody, that is a day too. You may have been the only stranger for a very long way, and it is worth noticing what that was like. Perhaps you were glad of it. Perhaps you were lonelier than you expected. Either is possible, and neither means the day was wasted.

On tiredness, honestly: the end of a long day is not a time for good manners, and the bunk room does not always get your best. The effort is small. A word of thanks to whoever made the bed. A quiet voice after lights are low. A light turned out early. These are the small hospitalities that make a night shared by strangers easier to bear.

Questions for tonight:

- Who did I meet today, and did I really look at them?
- When did I treat someone as part of the scenery?
- What would a stranger have seen if they had met me for one minute today?

## A short prayer

*Make me a gentle stranger. For the faces I met, thank you. For the ones I passed without a look, forgive me. Let someone tomorrow find a welcome in my face, and let me find one in theirs. Bless every house that took a walker in today.*
