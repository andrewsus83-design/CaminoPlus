# Day 7. What may never be known

*How do you walk beside something you cannot explain?*

## The passage

> What it was exactly that happened there may never be known. The matter has been, I believe, a subject of dispute among the most devout students of the saintly life as well as between such students and others of the more secular sort. It may be that St. Francis never spoke to a soul on the subject; it would be highly characteristic, and it is certain in any case that he said very little; I think he is only alleged to have spoken of it to one man. … Finally after some fashion the apocalypse faded from the sky and the agony within subsided; and silence and the natural air filled the morning twilight and settled slowly in the purple chasms and cleft abysses of the Apennines. The head of the solitary sank, amid all that relaxation and quiet in which time can drift by with the sense of something ended and complete; and as he stared downwards, he saw the marks of nails in his own hands.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter VIII, The Mirror of Christ (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

Some mornings by the sea the fog rubs out the horizon. The road is where it was, and the next marker is there somewhere, but the world has shrunk to the few paces ahead. There is a particular quiet in that, and also a small unease. You are used to knowing what is coming. Today you walk beside a great deal that you cannot see, and you go on anyway.

Francis spent much of his later life in that sort of fog, and Chesterton, to his credit, does not try to clear it. This day's passage comes at the end of a long chapter, so a few words of background are needed first.

The chapter follows Francis's wish to be as like Christ as he could. In Chesterton's telling that wish took him across the sea. His first attempt, a sail for the East, has a comic edge. He saw a boat, threw himself into it without waiting for introductions or bargains, "becoming the Patron Saint of Stowaways", and lay "like lumber among the cargo". The voyage turned out "unfortunate and erratic" and ended with a return to Italy. A later voyage got further. He reached the camp of the Crusaders outside the city of Damietta, went on alone to speak with the Sultan, and came back, Chesterton says, "as freely as he came". The aim, as Chesterton puts it, was to end the Crusades "by conversion and not by conquest". The whole effort broke off short. "Men liked him too much for himself to let him die for his faith," Chesterton writes, and he calls the whole venture the "beginnings of a great bridge that might have united East and West".

Here the book has to say something plainly. Chesterton writes about Islam and about "the East" in the language of 1923, and with a confidence we do not share. Some of his sentences in this chapter read badly now. We have left them out of the passage and have not tried to defend them. One thing in his account holds up without them: a man set out to meet people he was told to fear, and did so alone, in good faith.

Then Francis turned toward the mountains. A gentleman with lands in Tuscany gave him a mountain, "a thing somewhat unique among the gifts of the world", and he used it as a place for prayer. The mountain is Alverno. And here is the sentence this day is built on: "What it was exactly that happened there may never be known."

In the stretch we have left out, Chesterton describes what Francis is said to have seen: a winged figure filling the sky, like a seraph spread out in the shape of a cross, in pain. At the end of the passage the vision is gone, the morning is quiet, and Francis looks down and sees "the marks of nails in his own hands". These are what Christians call the stigmata, wounds like those of the crucified Christ. Chesterton takes the account as true. He says the one thin report that has come down "reads very like the report of something real".

You may take the story as a miracle, or as a mystery, or as a tale you cannot judge. This book does not argue for or against it. What it can say is what the passage does: Chesterton does not pretend to know more than he does. He gives the dispute, the silence, and the quiet air over the Apennines, and he stops.

Now say where it is hard. In this chapter Chesterton tells us that a refrain ran through Francis's days: "I have not suffered enough; I have not sacrificed enough." He "wandered about the valleys of the world looking for the hill that has the outline of a skull." Nothing here asks you to want pain. A blister is not a sacrifice to be hoarded, and an aching knee is not a gift to be sought. If you are walking with pain today, your own or someone else's, this reading asks only that you be gentle.

What can it ask of an ordinary day, then? Something much smaller than a vision: to let one thing stay unexplained. Almost every walker meets a thing like that. A feeling that arrives on a hill and does not fit anywhere. A grief that is not for any reason you can name. A joy with no cause. The habit of the mind is to explain it at once, to file it, to turn it into a sentence for a post. Chesterton's habit, here, is to wait. "May never be known" is a perfectly good place to stand.

## For the walk

- What is there in my life that I cannot explain, and could I carry it for one day without explaining it?
- At some point in the morning, stop for one minute. Let your hands hang. Listen to the farthest sound you can find, and the nearest. Do not name anything. When the minute is over, walk on without telling anyone what it was.

## At the end of the day

The best conversations of the day may have been the ones without an answer. A sentence at a table that nobody finished. A question a stranger asked about why you were walking, which you answered one way at lunch and another way at supper. Evening is a good hour to notice how many things go unexplained, and how well you got on without it.

Chesterton's own honesty is worth borrowing. He says that what happened on the mountain "may never be known", and a few sentences later that, "to me personally", the one report that has come down "reads very like the report of something real". It is possible to hold both. A hunch and a doubt can share a bench. You do not have to resolve the day's strangest moment before you sleep. Perhaps it will keep.

It is worth remembering that the same chapter has a joke in it. A saint who sets out for the other end of the sea by lying like lumber in somebody else's cargo is allowed to make you smile, and Chesterton clearly meant him to. Awe and humour keep company more often than people expect. If something today made you laugh at yourself, a missed turn, a pack dropped in front of an audience, you may have been closer to Francis than you thought.

If there was pain in the day, a foot, a back, a grief that came up in a quiet hour, you carried it well enough to get here. It does not have to be turned into a lesson. A hot shower, if there is one, and a plain meal are a respectable way of answering it. If the pain is worse than usual, or does not ease, or worries you, see a doctor, or ask your host where to find one. The standard European emergency number is 112. There is no prize for walking on a problem.

And then the small thing about silence. The passage ends in it: "silence and the natural air filled the morning twilight". You may find the evening has a silence of its own, after the stove and the voices and the last tap running. If you can, give it one minute before you reach for the phone. There is nothing in it to fix.

It may help to notice, too, what you left unsaid today. A word held back at a table, a name you did not look up, a feeling you let pass without a comment. Not all silence is a failure of nerve. Some of it is courtesy, and some of it is simply the right size for the thing.

A word on tiredness. A tired mind wants every question answered so that it can stop. Tonight, let some of them stay on the table. They will still be there in the morning, and a rested mind is a better judge of them.

Questions for tonight:

- What did I try to explain today that might have been better left alone?
- What can I carry for a while without understanding it?
- Where did I find quiet today, even for a minute?

## A short prayer

*For what I cannot explain, give me patience. Let me hold it gently and not rush it into words. Where there is pain in me or near me tonight, ease it. Give me a quiet that is not empty, and let that be enough.*
