# About the book and its author

## The saint

Francis of Assisi lived from about 1181 to 1226. The edition we use gives no dates, only that he was not fifty when he died, so these are the well-known ones, and the first is approximate. He was born in Assisi, a walled town on the hills of Umbria, in central Italy, the son of a cloth merchant. As a young man he gave up the merchant's life, took to the roads in a rough brown tunic tied with a rope, and gathered a band of followers that came to be called the Friars Minor, or, as Chesterton prefers, the Little Brothers. He is a saint of the Catholic Church. He left some writings of his own, among them his song in praise of the creatures. This book is not one of them.

## The author

Gilbert Keith Chesterton lived from 1874 to 1936. He was an English journalist, novelist, poet and Christian apologist, which means a writer who defends his faith in argument. He is best known for the Father Brown stories, about a priest who solves crimes, and for a great many essays, written quickly and with a love of paradox. He became a Catholic in 1922, and *St. Francis of Assisi* came out the following year. These dates are general knowledge, and the edition we used does not state them.

## The book

The book is a short life of Francis, about 44,000 words by our count, in ten chapters. Their titles run from "The Problem of St. Francis" and "Francis the Fighter" through *Le Jongleur de Dieu* (the minstrel and jester of God) and "The Little Poor Man" to "Miracles and Death" and "The Testament of St. Francis".

Chesterton tells you at once what kind of book it is. A life of Francis, he says, can be written as secular history, as a devout work, or by an outsider who admires the man and cannot quite follow him. He chooses the third, and writes, he says, for "the ordinary modern man, sympathetic but sceptical". His key is a simple one: that "to this great mystic his religion was not a thing like a theory but a thing like a love-affair". He calls his own work "this thin and scratchy sketch".

It is also a book by someone in love with his subject: the figure in the brown habit, he tells us, stands above the hearth in the room where he writes. That affection is the best thing in it, and it is something to be aware of.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

Because the saint is a walker. Chesterton's Francis is, in his phrase, "a man always going somewhere", on the roads of Umbria with a rope for a belt, begging his bread and singing. The book is about leaving, travelling light, courtesy to strangers, gratitude, and the odd joy of having little. It is short, quick and often funny, and it can be read a page at a time. It is also opinionated: Chesterton argues in almost every sentence, and these readings do not try to make him less so.

## What the book is, and what it is not

**It is a portrait, not Francis's own words.** Everything in the passages is Chesterton's English. The sayings of Francis in it are Chesterton's report of them, and the song of the sun appears only in pieces. This book does not print it whole. Chesterton draws on the early biographers (he names Bonaventura, Celano and the Three Companions) and does not always say which tale comes from where.

**It belongs to 1923.** Chesterton writes about other faiths, about the Protestant tradition, and about the Middle Ages and the age before them, which he calls the Dark Ages, with the confidence of a polemicist and the assumptions of his time. His words for Muslims and for the East ("Moslem", "Mahomedan", "oriental") are of that time, and some of his sentences about them read badly now. His second chapter, a sweeping account of the ancient world and the centuries after it, is the most dated part of the book, and we do not use it. We chose passages that do not rest on these assumptions. Where a day comes near one, it says so briefly and moves on.

**It retells medieval stories.** Chesterton gathers the miracles into one chapter and says that some of the tales have "the character of fairy stories". This book neither argues for the miracles nor against them. If you want the life as historians now tell it, read a modern life alongside this portrait. They may not always agree with Chesterton.

## The edition, the translator and the licence

This is an English original, so there is no translator. We used the text published by Project Gutenberg as ebook number 63084, prepared from scans of an old printing by Hodder and Stoughton, of London and Toronto, in a series called the People's Library. It states no year, so 1923 is the well-known date and not one the file gives. Chesterton died in 1936, and the book is in the public domain. Every passage in these pages was checked against that text, word for word.

The volunteers who prepared the text corrected obvious printer errors, regularised the hyphens and marked italics with underscores. We show those italics as italics, and otherwise changed nothing in the passages, not Chesterton's spelling, his punctuation, or even a *Cæsar*. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the nine passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original. The passages come from Chapters III to X of the book, with Chapter VI used twice. Under each passage we give the chapter and its title, as they stand in the book.

Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
