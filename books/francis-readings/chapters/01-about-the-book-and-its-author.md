# About the book and its author

## The saint

Francis of Assisi lived from about 1181 to 1226. The edition we use gives no dates, apart from saying that he was not fifty when he died, so these are the well-known ones, and the first is approximate. He was born in Assisi, a walled town on the hills of Umbria, in central Italy, the son of a cloth merchant. As a young man he gave up the merchant's life, took to the roads in a rough brown tunic tied with a rope, and gathered a band of followers that came to be called the Friars Minor, or, as Chesterton prefers, the Little Brothers. He is a saint of the Catholic Church. Francis left some writings of his own, among them his song in praise of the creatures. This book is not one of them.

## The author

Gilbert Keith Chesterton lived from 1874 to 1936. He was an English journalist, novelist, poet and Christian apologist, which means a writer who defends his faith in argument. He is best known for the Father Brown stories, a priest who solves crimes, and for a great many essays, written quickly and with a love of paradox. He became a Catholic in 1922. His *St. Francis of Assisi* came out the following year. These dates are general knowledge, and not stated in the edition we used.

## The book

The book is a short life of Francis, about 44,000 words by our count, in ten chapters: the problem of St. Francis; the world he found; Francis the fighter; Francis the builder; *Le Jongleur de Dieu*, the minstrel and jester of God; the little poor man; the three orders; the mirror of Christ; miracles and death; and his testament.

Chesterton tells you in the first pages what kind of book it is. A life of Francis, he says, can be written as secular history, as a devout work, or from the standpoint of an outsider who admires the man and cannot quite follow him. He chooses the third. He writes, he says, for "the ordinary modern man, sympathetic but sceptical". His key is a simple one: that "to this great mystic his religion was not a thing like a theory but a thing like a love-affair". He calls his own work "this thin and scratchy sketch".

It is also a book written by someone in love with his subject. Chesterton tells us that the figure in the brown habit stands above the hearth in the room where he writes. That affection is the best thing in it, and it is also something to be aware of.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

Because the saint is a walker. Chesterton's Francis is, in one of his phrases, "a man always going somewhere", on the roads of Umbria with a rope for a belt, begging his bread and singing. The book is about leaving, travelling light, courtesy to strangers, gratitude, and the odd joy of having little. It is short, funny and quick, and it can be read a page at a time.

It is also opinionated. Chesterton argues in almost every sentence and does not try to be even-handed. These readings do not try to make him less so.

## What the book is, and what it is not

Three things are worth knowing before you start.

**It is a portrait, not Francis's own words.** Everything in the passages is Chesterton's English. The sayings of Francis in it are Chesterton's report of them, and his song of the sun appears only in pieces. This book does not print it. Chesterton draws on the early biographers (he names Bonaventura, Celano and the Three Companions) and does not always say which tale comes from where.

**It belongs to 1923.** Chesterton writes about other faiths, about the Protestant tradition, and about the Middle Ages and the so-called Dark Ages with the confidence of a polemicist and the assumptions of his time. His words for Muslims and for the East ("Moslem", "Mahomedan", "oriental") are of that time, and some of his sentences about them read badly now. His second chapter, a sweeping account of the ancient world and the centuries after it, is the most dated part of the book, and we do not draw on it at all. We chose passages that do not rest on these assumptions. Where a day comes near one, it says so briefly and moves on.

**It retells medieval stories.** Chesterton gathers the miracles into one chapter and says that some of the tales have "the character of fairy stories". This book neither argues for the miracles nor against them. If you want the facts of Francis's life as historians now hold them, read a modern life alongside this portrait. They do not always agree with Chesterton, on dates or on the order of events.

## The edition, the translator and the licence

This is an English original, so there is no translator. We used the text published by Project Gutenberg as ebook number 63084, which was prepared from scans of an old printing by Hodder and Stoughton, of London and Toronto, in a series called the People's Library. The edition states no year. The date of 1923 is general knowledge. Chesterton died in 1936, and the book is in the public domain. Every passage in these pages was checked against that text, word for word.

The volunteers who prepared the text corrected obvious printer errors, regularised the hyphens and marked italics with underscores. We show those italics as italics, and otherwise changed nothing in the passages: Chesterton's spelling, his punctuation, his capital letters, even a *Cæsar*. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the nine passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours, and they are original. The passages come from Chapters III to X of the book, with Chapter VI used twice. Under each passage we give the chapter and its title, as they stand in the book.

Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
