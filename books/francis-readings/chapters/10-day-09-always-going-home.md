# Day 9. Always going home

*How do you go on, when the road has taught you what it can?*

## The passage

> It is perhaps the chief suggestion of this book that St. Francis walked the world like the Pardon of God. I mean that his appearance marked the moment when men could be reconciled not only to God but to nature and, most difficult of all, to themselves. … He was not a mere eccentric because he was always turning towards the centre and heart of the maze; he took the queerest and most zigzag short cuts through the wood, but he was always going home. … For that is the full and final spirit in which we should turn to St. Francis; in the spirit of thanks for what he has done. He was above all things a great giver; and he cared chiefly for the best kind of giving which is called thanksgiving.
>
> — G. K. Chesterton, *St. Francis of Assisi*, Chapter X, The Testament of St. Francis (English original, 1923)

## Reflection

A coast road rarely goes straight. It follows the edge of the land, and the edge of the land is a long argument between rock and water: a boardwalk that ends at an estuary, a detour round a headland, a stretch inland because the shore has given up. Anyone who has walked such a road knows the odd sense of progress it gives. You are not going the shortest way. You are going the way the ground allows, and you are, all the same, getting there.

Chesterton's last chapter is about that same kind of progress. He steps back from the story and asks what Francis left. The first half of the chapter is an argument about the quarrels that followed his death, and about whether the Popes were right to restrain the stricter Franciscans. Chesterton is a partisan in it, and sure of himself, and we have left it out. The passage comes from the later pages, where he tries to say what the whole story was for.

It begins with a bold phrase: "St. Francis walked the world like the Pardon of God." By "pardon" he means something like a fresh start. A little later he says it was Francis's "whole function to tell men to start afresh". His appearance, he says, marked the moment when men could be reconciled to God, to nature and, "most difficult of all, to themselves". A reader who does not share his faith may hear the word "pardon" as a plain one: a door left open, a new page.

Look at the last item on that list. Reconciled to themselves. It is the hardest of the three, and there is a reason it comes last. A long walk shows you a good deal. How fast you go and how soon you tire. What you do with an empty stomach. How you talk to strangers, and to yourself, when nobody is watching. Some of that you may like, and some of it you may not. To be reconciled with it is to stop waiting for a different person to do the walking. You are the one with the pack, and you are doing it.

The second stretch is the one to carry. Francis seemed eccentric, Chesterton says, but he was not a mere eccentric "because he was always turning towards the centre and heart of the maze". He "took the queerest and most zigzag short cuts through the wood, but he was always going home". Think of everything in his story: the boast, the stripping in the bishop's court, the stowaway voyage, the mountain. Any of those, taken singly, looks like a swerve. Chesterton's claim is that they were all one direction, which he calls going home.

You might look at your own road that way. The detours of the walk, the wrong turn, the extra kilometres, the day you rested when you meant to go on. And behind them, the longer detours of a life, which may look from here like nothing but swerves. It is not a promise that they were all leading somewhere. It is an invitation to wonder whether some of them were.

Chesterton ends in thanks. "For that is the full and final spirit in which we should turn to St. Francis; in the spirit of thanks for what he has done." He describes Francis as "above all things a great giver", and as one who cared chiefly for "the best kind of giving which is called thanksgiving". A few lines on, he calls the saint's way a "grammar of gratitude". It is a good phrase for a last day on the road: not a feeling, but a way of speaking, a grammar that you practise until it begins to sound natural.

Now the honest part. Last days are odd. An arrival can be an anticlimax. The place you walked towards may be crowded, your legs may be heavy, and the thing you expected to feel may not turn up. If you reach the end of the road and feel nothing in particular, that is allowed. Gratitude can come a week later, at your own table, in the middle of an ordinary Thursday. And if you are reading this at home with no walk behind you, the same holds: go on, with what you have.

The passage asks two plain things of an ordinary day: to keep a direction when the path zigzags, and to keep saying thanks when there is not much to say it about. Neither is dramatic. Both are what a long road is made of, and what a life is, once the road is over. You will not need a vow for either. You will need, now and then, to remember them.

## For the walk

- Which way is "home" for me today, and what has been the shape of my zigzag so far? A direction can be a person, a place, or only a kind of peace.
- At some point today, stop and turn round. Look back along the way you have come, as far as you can see. Say thank you for one thing on it, quietly, and let your eyes rest on it for a breath. Then turn forward and walk on, and see how the road looks when you have thanked it. Do it only where the path is clear and safe.

## At the end of the day

If this is your last evening on the road, the room may be louder than usual. Farewells, a few addresses exchanged, somebody cooking for too many. It can all feel a little unreal, as if the day had been cut loose from the days before it. If this is an ordinary evening at home, it is only the ninth, and it counts as much.

Give a few minutes to going back over the nine days. A boast that the road turned round. A pack, and the things given back. Water that tasted like news. A stranger on a path. A gull on a post. The small fringe that kept catching your sleeve. A fog with something in it you could not name. A day that ended on the bare ground. They were Francis's days, in Chesterton's telling, and in their small way they may have been yours as well.

There is a small temptation on a last evening, which is to start composing the story you will tell. A walk of many days turns, in the telling, into three anecdotes and a view. That is fine. It is how stories work. Only remember that the walk was longer and odder than any version of it, and that you do not owe anybody the whole thing. Some of it can stay yours.

Then look for the zigzag. Where did the road bend most? What looked like a wrong turn at the time and now looks like part of the way? Nobody needs certainty here, and not every bend needs a meaning. A few may remain only bends. But it is good to notice the ones that seem to point somewhere.

And think about thanks, which is where this book ends. Not a grand statement, only a few small, true ones: for a bed, a road, a pair of feet that did their job, the people who said hello, the water, the weather you could manage, the days you managed. If there is anyone to thank aloud, tonight is a good night to do it.

Your feet have earned a plain thanks of their own. Wash them, dry them well, and put them up for a while, with the shoes out of reach. They carried the rest of you the whole way, and they will not ask for more than that.

A word on tiredness, for the last time. The end of a road is tiring in a different way, partly the body's and partly the mind's. It is the tiredness of something finished. You may sleep very badly, or very well. Either way, you are not obliged to feel anything particular in the morning. You are only invited to go on.

Questions for a last night:

- What am I most thankful for from these nine days?
- Which zigzag of my life now looks as if it was heading somewhere?
- What is the first small thing I will do, tomorrow or when I get home, to keep one part of this walk with me?

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road, and for everyone on it. Thank you for water, for weather, and for feet that carried me. Help me to be at home in myself. Let me go on, by short cuts or long ones, and let me always be going home. Let the night be gentle.*
