# Day 9. If I only knew

*What would you do today if you knew how the walk ends?*

## The passage

> A certain man being in anxiety of mind, continually tossed about between hope and fear, and being on a certain day overwhelmed with grief, cast himself down in prayer before the altar in a church, and meditated within himself, saying, “Oh! if I but knew that I should still persevere,” and presently heard within him a voice from God, “And if thou didst know it, what wouldst thou do? Do now what thou wouldst do then, and thou shalt be very secure.” And straightway being comforted and strengthened, he committed himself to the will of God and the perturbation of spirit ceased, neither had he a mind any more to search curiously to know what should befall him hereafter, but studied rather to inquire what was the good and acceptable will of God, for the beginning and perfecting of every good work.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 1, chapter 25 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Somewhere in the second week the question arrives, and it rarely arrives at a good moment. It comes on a long uphill, or in the last hour before a stop, or at two in the morning in a strange bed. Will I finish? Will the knee hold? What if the blister turns into something? What if I get there and nothing has changed? What if I stop and everyone knows I stopped? The questions are not stupid. They are only unanswerable, and they are very good at keeping you awake.

Thomas à Kempis tells a short story about a man in this position. It is a story he tells without naming anyone. The man is anxious, tossed between hope and fear, overwhelmed with grief, and one day he falls to his knees in a church and says, in effect, what many of us have said in our own way: *If I only knew that I should still persevere.* If I only knew I would make it.

The answer he receives is short, and it is a question. *And if thou didst know it, what wouldst thou do?* The man does not have to think very long. He would keep going. He would do whatever he is already trying to do, with a little less fear. And so the voice says: *Do now what thou wouldst do then, and thou shalt be very secure.*

It is worth turning that over slowly, because it is cleverer than it first appears. The man wants a guarantee before he acts. But a guarantee would not change what he did today. It would only change how he felt while he did it. So the thing he is waiting for is not what he needs. What he needs is already in his hands: the next step, the next act, the next honest effort. If you would walk on knowing you would arrive, then walk on, and let the knowing go. Today's task is the same either way.

Then the story ends with a small, beautiful sentence. The man's disturbance of spirit ceased, and he no longer wanted to search into what would happen to him in the future. He wanted, instead, to ask what the good thing to do was, now. The question changes from *what will happen* to *what is the right thing to do next*. That is a much smaller question, and a much easier one to answer.

For a walker it translates cleanly. You cannot know whether your knee will last, or whether you will reach the cathedral. You can know whether to drink water now, to stop at the next village, to take the shorter path, to ask a pharmacist about that tendon. The big question is out of reach. The small ones are all within your hands, and they are the ones that decide the big one.

Where is it hard? The passage works best on one kind of doubt, the kind that is about getting through. It is much less helpful on a different kind, the doubt about why you are walking at all. A man who asks "Is this worth doing?" is not answered by "Keep going". For that question there may be no tidy reply. You may walk for days with it unresolved, and the passage does not pretend to settle it. What it may do is suggest that the question can be carried while you walk, and need not be answered before the next step.

There is another difficulty. The man in the story receives an answer, a voice, in a church. Many people never do. They kneel, or sit, or walk, and hear only the wind. Thomas does not say this is a failure. He tells the story because it happened to someone, and offers it as a possibility, not a promise. Whether or not you expect a voice, the question it asked is a good one to carry by yourself.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Each time an "if only" comes up, finish the sentence honestly: *and so today I will...* The knee, the distance, the cathedral and the verdict of other people can wait. Today's walk is enough to be getting on with.

## For the walk

- If I knew for certain how this walk will end, what would I do differently today? Answer honestly. Nobody will hear.
- Each time a "what if" rises in your mind, answer it with "and so, now, I will...", and name one small thing within reach: a sip of water, the next waymark, a greeting. Do that thing, and let the "what if" stand where it is. If the worry is large, make the small thing smaller: only the next ten steps.

## At the end of the day

If you are lying awake with the questions, you are in good company. Doubt keeps late hours, and it is at its most persuasive between eleven and three. It is worth remembering that nothing you think in the dark has a vote in the morning. Let the thoughts come, and let them be heard, and then, if you can, put them down.

Look back over the day with the story in mind. Where did "if only" visit you? Perhaps it was on a climb, or at the point where the path divided. Perhaps it came while you were waiting for something: a bed, an answer, a message. Perhaps it lived somewhere in the background all day like a radio in another room. Name the biggest "if" of the day. Say it plainly, once, in a whisper or in a single line on paper. A worry that has been named has edges, and a worry that is only carried has none.

Then ask a second question: what did you do anyway? There is nearly always something. You walked the hill with the "if" in your head. You asked about the path. You drank water, ate bread, and stepped over the next stone. That, the passage says, is the whole business: you did, now, what you would have done then. It is more than it sounds.

And if the big doubt, the "why am I doing this at all", was also with you today, it can stay unsettled tonight. Nobody has to resolve it in a bunk. Some people carry it for the whole walk and understand only afterwards what it was for. Some people never do, and walk well all the same.

There is one more thing worth seeing. Many of the people in this room have the same questions in their heads tonight, and are as quiet about them as you are. A walker's reserve can hide a great deal of the same fear. Tomorrow, an honest word to someone might lighten the load for both of you.

Three questions for tonight:

- What was the biggest "if" of today, and what did I do in spite of it?
- Is there a smaller, answerable question hiding inside the large, unanswerable one?
- What is one thing I can do tomorrow without knowing how it ends?

An honest word about tiredness. Doubt and exhaustion look very alike from the inside, and they often travel together. A worry that sounded like a verdict at midnight may sound much more like a worry after breakfast. If it is still there in the morning, with food inside you, take it seriously then. Tonight, the most useful thing is to lie still and let sleep be the answer for now.

## A short prayer

*I do not know how this ends, and tonight I will not try to find out. Let me do what is in front of me, and do it without fear. Quiet the questions that have no answers yet. Let me rest in the small certainty that I walked today, and that tomorrow there will be a next step.*
