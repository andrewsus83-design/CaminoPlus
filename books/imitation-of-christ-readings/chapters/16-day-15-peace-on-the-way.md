# Day 15. Peace on the way

*Can you have peace without everything going right?*

## The passage

> My peace is with the humble and lowly in heart. Thy peace shall be in much patience. … Yet never to feel any disquiet, nor to suffer any pain of heart or body, this belongeth not to the present life, but is the state of eternal rest. Therefore count not thyself to have found true peace, if thou hast felt no grief; nor that then all is well if thou hast no adversary; nor that this is perfect if all things fall out according to thy desire.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 25 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

There is a kind of evening that every walker remembers. The boots are off, the feet are up, a plastic cup of something cold is in your hand, and the light across the fields has gone the colour of honey. Nothing is wrong. You are clean, fed, a little sunburnt, and quite certain that this is the best place in the world to be. It is peace of a sort, and it is easy to take it for the real thing.

Then the light goes, someone starts a conversation that grates, you realise your sock has a hole, and the peace goes with it. It was peace of the kind that depends on conditions. It lasted exactly as long as everything was right.

Thomas à Kempis is talking about another kind. The chapter this passage comes from begins with the voice of Christ saying that his peace is not given the way the world gives it. Then it makes a plain, rather bracing claim. Peace, it says, is with the humble and the lowly in heart, and it grows in much patience.

The second part of the passage is the surprising one. To feel no disquiet at all, to suffer no pain of heart or body, is not something this life provides. That, he says, is the state of eternal rest. So you are not to think that you have found true peace because you have felt no grief. Nor that all is well because you have no adversary. Nor that things are perfect because everything has gone according to your wishes.

Read that again. It does not say that grief means peace has failed. It says that the absence of grief is not the same thing as peace. They are different things. A day with no trouble can be a day with no peace in it. And, by the same logic, a day with a lot of trouble can have a quiet place in the middle of it. Peace, in this reading, is not the absence of trouble. It is something that can sit alongside it, and does not need the trouble to leave before it arrives.

Walkers know something about this. The best moments are often not the most comfortable ones. A bench on a hard day, a slow stretch with a companion, the first light after a bad night. The peace is there with the sore feet. It does not wait for them to be cured.

Later in the same chapter Thomas has a phrase for the reader: *a wayfaring man*. It is an old word for someone on the road. Such a person, he says, may have abundance of peace, as much as is possible for someone who is only a wayfarer. That is both realistic and gentle. It does not promise the peace of the destination. It promises the peace of the road, which is real, and partial, and enough to go on with.

The passage pairs peace with humility, and that pairing can sound like a test you might fail. If you are anxious, are you proud? Plainly not. Anxiety and pride are different things, and many humble people lie awake. The sentence is better read as a description than as a verdict: it tells you where peace tends to be found, and it does not tell you that you have been refused. Nor does it mean that you should stop caring about justice or change. It says only that peace and perfect conditions are two separate things.

Another hard place is the idea that perfect peace is not available here at all. Some readers will find that sobering and some, a relief. If the state of complete rest belongs to another life, what is the point of looking for any now? The answer in the passage is that partial peace is real, and the road is where it lives.

Once today, in the middle of something imperfect, look for the small peace that is already there. The sock with the hole. The bed that is a little too hard. A friend who is difficult. Under the minor trouble is often a stillness that was waiting for you to stop waiting for conditions.

## For the walk

- Where, today, is there some peace in the middle of something imperfect? It may be very small.
- At the next moment of mild discomfort, tired legs, a slight chill, a delay, pause and look for the small peace that is present alongside it. You do not have to make it larger. Say, quietly, "This too," and stay with it for three breaths before you walk on.

## At the end of the day

The room is quiet, or quiet enough. Another day has gone, and this one was not perfect, and you are somehow at the start of the third week. Sit for a minute, and do nothing about it.

Go back over the day for its moments of peace, and be careful how you look. The easy ones are obvious: the view, the shower, the meal. Those are real, and worth naming. But look for the harder kind too: a moment of quiet that came in the middle of something uncomfortable. A calm that arrived while your feet hurt. A moment of ease in a crowded room. A conversation in which you were tired and, to your own surprise, did not mind. Those are the ones the passage is talking about, and they are often easier to miss.

Now ask what you were waiting for. Many of us hold a private list of conditions for peace: when the feet stop hurting, when the next stage is booked, when the weather turns, when that conversation is behind me. It is worth looking at the list. Some of the items may not be needed. Some may be keeping you from a peace that was on offer an hour ago.

And what disquiet did the day hold? Say it plainly. It need not be solved. It is enough to see that it can sit in the same room as a small peace without either of them having to leave.

If you have begun to count down to the end of your walk, notice that the countdown is also a condition: I will rest when I arrive. It is a very natural one, and a very common one, and it can make the last days a long wait. The passage suggests that the peace on offer is not at the end of the road. It is on it.

A small practice for the night: put a hand on your chest or your stomach, feel the breath going in and out, and say to yourself, "This is enough for tonight." If that feels silly, try it anyway. Silly is allowed.

Three questions for tonight:

- Where did I wait for conditions to be perfect before allowing myself to rest?
- What small peace was present today alongside something uncomfortable?
- What am I trying to get away from tonight, and could it simply be allowed to be here?

An honest word about tiredness. The end of a day is not a test of how peaceful you are. If you are tired and irritable, you are tired and irritable, and that has little to do with the state of your soul. Peace that is real can look quite ordinary in the evening: a lowered shoulder, a long breath, the plain fact of being horizontal. Do not ask for more tonight than that.

## A short prayer

*Let there be peace tonight, even if it is only a small one. Not because everything is right, but because I am here, and the day is over, and the bed is soft enough. Let the trouble sit quietly beside me, and not climb in. Let me be a wayfarer, and not ask the road to be a home. Let the peace I could not find in the day find me in the dark.*
