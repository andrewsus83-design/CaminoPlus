# Day 16. My planning can profit but little

*What would it be like to plan a little less and trust a little more?*

## The passage

> “My Son, always commit thy cause to Me; I will dispose it aright in due time. Wait for My arrangement of it, and then thou shalt find it for thy profit.” … O Lord, right freely I commit all things to Thee; for my planning can profit but little. Oh that I did not dwell so much on future events, but could offer myself altogether to Thy pleasures without delay. … My Son, a man often striveth vehemently after somewhat which he desireth; but when he hath obtained it he beginneth to be of another mind, because his affections towards it are not lasting, but rather rush on from one thing to another.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 39 (tr. William Benham)

## Reflection

Every pilgrim has a plan, and every plan has a short life. It begins at home, with a spreadsheet or a pencilled table of stages, distances, beds and cafés. It survives the first few days. Then comes a storm, or a blister, or a friend who wants to go on, or a bed that turns out to be full, and the plan is revised. After the third revision it is hardly a plan any more, just a direction. Walkers have a saying for the process, that the Way provides. It is half a joke and half a confession: we learned that our arrangements were not the only ones being made.

Thomas à Kempis has a short passage on this, and it has two voices in it. The first is the voice that speaks to the disciple in his third book: *always commit thy cause to Me; I will dispose it aright in due time. Wait for My arrangement of it.* The second is the disciple's reply, which is honest and a little rueful. He agrees freely. His planning, he admits, can profit but little. If only he did not dwell so much on future events.

It is an easy thing to misread. Thomas is not saying that you should stop thinking ahead. A person who walks into August without a bed is not being trusting, only hopeful. His point is about what planning can do. Planning prepares. It does not control. It can get you to the starting line, but it cannot arrange the weather, or the knee, or the person you will meet. The mistake is to put all your weight on it, and then be shaken when it gives way.

The wise path, in practice, is to plan and then loosen your grip. Make the arrangements you can. Look at tomorrow's stage, see where the water is, know the next town. Then let the day have its say. That small letting-go is nearly all of what Thomas means by waiting. It is the half-second at the fork in the path when you allow that the road may know something you do not.

The third part of the passage turns, quite unexpectedly, to desire. A person often strives hard for something, he says, and when he has it, he begins to be of another mind. His affection for the thing is not lasting. It runs on, from one object to the next. Anyone who has wanted a shower for six hours and then barely noticed it knows this. And anyone who has been dreaming of the end of the walk, the cathedral, the certificate, the long sleep, may be quietly preparing for a surprise. The thing we strive for does not always turn out to be what we wanted. The passage does not say this to spoil it. It says it so you will not be caught unawares.

The word "wait" is a difficult one for people with practical problems. Someone with a sick parent, a bill, a legal matter, a decision with a deadline, cannot simply wait for it to be arranged. Nor should they. Thomas's advice is not an excuse for neglecting what is in your own hands. It is better read as a counsel for the part that is not: do everything within reach, and then do not carry the remainder as if it were all on your own back.

There is also a doubt that deserves to be said out loud. "I will dispose it aright" is a promise, and not every reader will feel that it is kept. People commit their cause and it goes badly. Thomas knows this as well as anyone. The passage does not say that all will be well. It says that the burden of arranging the world is not yours alone, and that is a modest claim, and a lighter one than it sounds.

Try this: plan once, in the morning, and then stop. Allow the day one change. At the first fork, wait a few seconds before choosing. Leave the stage app in your pocket for an hour. See what the road suggests when you are not busy telling it what to do.

## For the walk

- What am I trying to control today that I could only prepare for? Name it, and then name the one thing about it that is actually yours to do.
- Make the day's plan once, this morning, and then put it away. Allow the day one change, and when you come to the next fork or the next decision, wait three breaths before you choose. Notice what the day offers that was not on the plan.

## At the end of the day

Tonight's bed may be the one you planned, or one you arrived at by an accident, or one you were very glad of at the end of a day that did not follow the schedule. Whichever it is, you are in it, and that is a small proof of what the passage says: it was arranged somehow.

Look back at the day and set your plan beside what happened. The two are rarely the same. Where did they part? Perhaps the morning stage was longer than expected, or shorter. Perhaps a person turned up that you had not meant to meet, or a place you meant to see was closed. Perhaps you changed your mind at a crossing and went another way. Some of those changes will have been annoying. Some, when you look at them now, may have been gifts. It is worth noticing, without insisting on a tidy moral, which was which.

Notice, too, how your body felt about the changes. Some people find a change of plan a relief, and some find it a small crisis. Neither is wrong, and knowing which you are is useful for the days ahead. A plan held loosely has one pleasant side effect: when it works, it feels like a gift, and when it fails, it feels like weather.

Now think about tomorrow, and be careful. The mind loves to begin planning at exactly the hour when it is least able to plan well. It will draw up a perfect itinerary at ten at night, and shred it at seven the next morning. Try giving tomorrow a single line: where you hope to be, roughly, by evening. That is plenty. The rest can be arranged by the day.

And the third part of the passage: is there something you wanted a great deal that you now have, and find strangely ordinary? Perhaps it was a bed, a meal, a rest, a view. Perhaps it was something larger. Hold it up for a moment and notice what happened to the wanting.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I plan today that did not happen, and what happened instead?
- What am I planning tonight that I could leave until morning?
- What did I want so much that, once I had it, I began wanting something else?

About tiredness: a tired mind plans badly and worries well. If you find yourself running through tomorrow's difficulties in detail, you are probably not solving anything. You are only rehearsing. Put the plan down. It will be exactly as useful in the morning, and you will be better equipped to make it.

## A short prayer

*Let me do what is mine to do, and put down the rest. Teach me to plan lightly and to wait without fretting. Let tomorrow be arranged by hands wiser than mine, and let me be glad of the surprise. Let me not want so hard that I cannot enjoy what I have. Tonight, let me simply lie down in the arrangement of the day.*
