# Day 3. The soft whisper

*What can you hear when the road goes quiet?*

## The passage

> Blessed is the soul which heareth the Lord speaking within it, and receiveth the word of consolation from His mouth. Blessed are the ears which receive the echoes of the soft whisper of God, and turn not aside to the whisperings of this world. Blessed truly are the ears which listen not to the voice that soundeth without, but to that which teacheth truth inwardly.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 1 (tr. William Benham)

## Reflection

Some time in the first hour, if you let it, the road goes quiet. The village is behind you. The last dog has given up. What is left is small: the scuff of your boots, the creak of the pack, a cockerel somewhere that has not been told it is no longer dawn, and perhaps, far off, the hum of a main road that you can never quite place. Then, after a few minutes, you notice there is another kind of noise. It is inside. The conversation you should have had last week. The list for the weekend. The tune that has been playing since breakfast.

Thomas à Kempis opens his third book with this problem. Up to here his chapters give advice. In the third book he changes the form: it becomes a conversation, a voice speaking to the reader, and the reader answering. These words are the beginning of it, and they are the reader speaking, hoping to be spoken to. Blessed are the ears, he says, that take in the echoes of a soft whisper and do not turn aside to the whisperings of this world.

Two kinds of listening are being set side by side. One is the voice that sounds outside: news, opinion, gossip, advertising, the commentary of other people. The other is the voice that teaches from within, and Thomas says it is soft. It does not shout. It has to be listened for, and it cannot be heard over the other.

Everyone who has walked a few hours alone understands the practical sense in that. A morning spent with earphones in is a morning with the world's voice playing on top of your own. There is nothing wicked in it. But the whisper, whatever you take it to be, a conscience, a memory, a calm you had forgotten or God, is not going to compete.

Here is where the passage is harder than it looks. For some people quiet is not gentle. It is the place where the old arguments are kept. If the voice inside you is mostly a critic, then "listen inwards" is not an invitation, and nobody should force it. Thomas has something quieter in mind, the voice that consoles. But it may take days of walking before the critic runs out of breath, and until it does, the quiet can feel crowded. That is not a failure of the practice. It is how the practice begins.

Notice also what he does not say. He does not say that company is bad, or that talk is a sin. A road full of voices, a table shared with strangers, a laugh from the next bunk: these are among the best things on the Way. Thomas is warning against being carried off by whatever speaks loudest. The line he draws is between what pulls at you and what steadies you, and you can hear both from the same bench.

The quiet is not empty, either. A path through fields has more in it than a street does: water in a ditch, wind in a hedge, the tick of a gate, your own steps. You only hear them once you stop talking over them.

In practice, that means an hour with no input. Not as a discipline or a boast, only as an experiment. Walk without earphones. Let a thought arrive and watch it go by, like a car on a distant road, without climbing in. Listen for the quietest sound you can find, a leaf, a water pipe, your own breath, and stay with it a minute. It may help to choose a landmark ahead, a tree or a bend in the path, and decide that nothing will be added to the air until you reach it.

And a word of doubt, because this book would rather be honest than soothing. Some mornings nothing comes. The quiet is merely quiet, and the mind is merely busy, and the whisper, if there is one, has nothing to say. Thomas knows this day well. His whole book is full of such days, and it keeps going. Perhaps "blessed" does not mean those who hear clearly. Perhaps it means those who keep their ears turned the right way, and stay on the road, and wait.

## For the walk

- What is the quietest thing I can hear right now, and what is the loudest thing in my head? Notice them both. You do not have to change either.
- For the first twenty minutes of walking, take no music, no podcast and no conversation. When a thought pulls at you, name it in one word, "plan" or "worry" or "tune", and let it go by. Go back to your steps. If twenty minutes is too many, take ten. The aim is to leave a gap, not to win it.

## At the end of the day

Even in an albergue the evening has its own sounds: water running, a spoon on a plate, three languages in the kitchen, someone laughing at the far end of the table. Then, later, the sounds of sleep, and a few of the less welcome sounds of sleep. This is a good moment to ask what you heard today, outside and in.

Outside, perhaps, there was a lot. Voices on the road, a bell, traffic, birds, the cheerful bustle of a village. Some of it you chose and some of it simply arrived. Some of it, if you are honest, you used to avoid hearing yourself think. That is no sin. Most of us carry a little noise with us in the way we carry a snack.

Inside, what was loudest? Today it might have been planning: tomorrow's distance, tonight's bed, the next meal. It might have been an old conversation, rerun with a better ending. It might have been the voice that says you are slower than everyone, or that you should have stayed at home. Notice it without arguing. A voice that has been named has less power than a voice that has only been obeyed.

And was there ever a quieter one? Often there is, and it is easy to miss: a moment when your breathing matched your steps, when something in the landscape stopped you, when a sentence you had been looking for turned up without being called. If you noticed such a moment today, you were already doing what the passage describes. If you did not, you are not behind.

It is also worth noticing the voices you went looking for. A phone in the pocket is a very small door onto the whole world, and in the evening it is hard not to open it. There is no rule against that. But you might ask whether the last voice you hear tonight should be the world's, or only the room's. Evening bells, if there are any in the town, are a good last thing to listen to. They are only a regular sound, a way of cutting the day into pieces small enough to carry. If you hear one, let it end the day.

Three questions for the end of a day:

- What was the quietest moment of today, and where was I?
- Which voice was loudest in my head, and did I believe it?
- Is there anything gentle I noticed today and then walked past?

Tiredness, honestly: evening is a poor time for listening. The body wants food and a flat surface, and the mind plays back the day too fast to follow. Do not force a silence you are too tired to hold. Sometimes the kindest kind of listening is to lie down and let the room be as noisy as it is. If something quiet has to say itself to you, it can wait until the morning, when the road is empty again. If you can manage it, put the phone face down tonight and listen to the room for one minute: the breathing, the pipes, a door, the town beyond the window. That is a very small version of the morning's practice, and it is enough.

## A short prayer

*In this noisy room, let me find one quiet place. Let what is gentle be heard over what is loud. If there is a word for me tonight, let it be small enough to hear. If there is none, let the silence be enough, and let me sleep inside it. Let the noise of the day settle like dust in a sunbeam, and be still.*
