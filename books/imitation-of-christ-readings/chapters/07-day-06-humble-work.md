# Day 6. Humble work

*What do you do when the high road is closed?*

## The passage

> So long as thou wearest a mortal body, thou shalt feel weariness and heaviness of heart. … At such a time it is expedient for thee to flee to humble and external works, and to renew thyself with good actions; to wait for My coming and heavenly visitation with sure confidence; to bear thy exile and drought of mind with patience, until thou be visited by Me again, and be freed from all anxieties. For I will cause thee to forget thy labours, and altogether to enjoy eternal peace.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 51 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Sooner or later, usually in the first week, there comes an afternoon on which nothing is wrong and everything is heavy. The road is straight. The sun is where it was an hour ago. You are not in pain, exactly, but you are used up, and the thoughts that carried you along on the first morning, the sense of purpose, the feeling that this was a good thing to be doing, have gone somewhere you cannot follow. You keep walking because stopping is not an option. It is not a crisis. It is just flat.

Thomas à Kempis knew this afternoon. In this chapter the voice that speaks to the reader is, in his telling, God's, and it begins with something every walker will recognise: you will not always be able to stay in the high clear air. A person cannot live permanently on the mountain. Weariness and heaviness of heart come with having a body, he says, and for as long as you have one, you will feel them.

What he suggests is almost comically modest. When the high road is closed, go down to the low one. Turn to humble and outward works, to small good actions, and renew yourself with those. Do not wait for the great feeling to return. Do something useful with your hands.

Anyone who has walked for a week has met this cure without knowing its name. Wash your socks. Hang them on the line. Sweep the floor where you slept. Peel the potatoes in the shared kitchen, or wash the pan someone else cooked in. Refill the bottle of the person whose hands are full. Mend the strap you have been meaning to mend. After half an hour of that, the heaviness has often lifted a little, not because the work was important, but because it was real, and small enough to finish. A mind that is dry does not need to be fed with big thoughts. It needs a task it can actually complete. Thomas spent much of his own life copying manuscripts by hand, and he knew how careful, repetitive work can steady a restless mind.

There is something else in the passage worth noticing: "good actions" are not only for the tidy. Kindness is one of them. A small, practical act for somebody else is a very good remedy for the flat afternoon, because it moves the attention off the weary self and onto something that needs doing now. This is not a trick. It is how people so often get through long days: by being useful to each other.

Then Thomas says the thing that gives the passage its patience. Wait, with confidence, until the lightness returns. Bear this dryness of mind, this "drought", he calls it, until you are visited again. That is a picture of the weather of a long walk. There are days when the legs seem not to belong to you, and then, quite without warning, a morning when they come back. It does not always happen on the day you want. But it happens often enough that it becomes a kind of trust.

Where is it hard? In two places. First, the passage promises that you will forget your labours and enjoy a perfect peace. That is a promise about the end, not the afternoon, and Thomas does not pretend otherwise. It may console you or it may sound far away. Both are fair. Second, and more important: ordinary tiredness and something deeper can look alike. Chores will not cure a real depression, and Thomas is not suggesting that they will. If the heaviness does not lift after a few days, or if it frightens you, say so to someone, a friend, a doctor, a person you trust. That is not weakness. It is exactly the kind of humble, practical action the passage is about.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? That when you are low, you reach for the nearest small honest task, and do it well, and do not demand more of yourself than that. An afternoon spent washing a shirt properly is not a wasted afternoon. It may be the thing that gets you to tomorrow.

## For the walk

- What is the smallest useful thing I can do right now, for myself or for someone near me? A small act for someone else counts as well: a held gate, a shared orange, a plaster for a stranger's heel.
- When you next feel flat, do one task slowly and completely: wipe down your boots, fill your water bottle and someone else's, tidy the corner where your things are. Let your attention rest on your hands, and when you have finished, notice how you feel.

## At the end of the day

Your hands smell of soap, or of boot polish, or of the orange you peeled at four o'clock. There is a line of washing somewhere with a few small, ridiculous items on it, drying in whatever evening light is left. It is a humble sight, and a faintly cheering one.

Think back over the day, and ask a plain question: when was it heavy? Perhaps at some point in the afternoon the way you felt turned grey, and you could not say why. Perhaps it was a sudden dip after a good morning. Perhaps it was more like a low hum than a mood, and you only noticed it when it stopped. Do not try to explain it. Just see where it sat in the day.

And did it lift, at any point, even a little? If so, what had you been doing? Sometimes it is something very small: a conversation that had nothing to do with anything, a job with a beginning and an end, a shower. You may not have thought of it as a cure. It may well have been one.

If you did a small kindness today, however trivial, hold it for a moment. It need not have been noticed. If you received one, hold that too. The small economy of favours on this road is one of its great consolations, and it runs on very little.

Take a moment to notice what the small tasks did for you. Work with the hands has a way of making the mind better company. It is worth remembering this, because the next flat afternoon will not announce itself either.

And if there was a long grey patch today that nothing lifted, give that a plain note too. Not that you failed, only that some afternoons are stubborn. Say it to yourself in one line, so that tomorrow you can tell whether it was a day or the start of a pattern. And notice, before you sleep, how your body feels after the work: the tiredness that follows small honest tasks is different from the heaviness of drift. Many people sleep better after having done something finished.

Three questions for tonight:

- When did the heaviness lift today, even slightly, and what was I doing?
- What small, finishable thing could I do tomorrow when it comes back?
- If it does not lift, who is the person I would tell?

An honest word about tiredness. A tired body makes everything seem worse than it is, and at night the worst is the loudest. Nothing you decide at ten in the evening is final. If the day felt flat, that is allowed. A flat day is still a day walked, and flatness is something that passes, usually faster than expected, and often during sleep. You do not have to be cheerful about it. You only have to let the night do its part.

## A short prayer

*For the work that was small and honest today, thank you. For the weariness, let it ease in the dark. Let the lightness come back when it will, and let me not be afraid in the meantime. Let me be glad of small things. If someone needs a hand tomorrow, let me see them, and if I need one, let me ask.*
