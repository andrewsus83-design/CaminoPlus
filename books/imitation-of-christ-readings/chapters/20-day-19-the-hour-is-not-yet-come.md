# Day 19. The hour is not yet come

*What do you do with the day after the destination?*

## The passage

> Already thou longest to be in the glorious liberty of the children of God; already the eternal home delighteth thee, and the heavenly country full of joy; but the hour is not yet come; there remaineth still another season, even a season of warfare, a season of labour and probation. Thou desirest to be filled with the Chief Good, but thou canst not attain it immediately. I AM that Good; wait for Me, until the Kingdom of God shall come. … Thou must still be tried upon earth, and be exercised in many things. Consolation shall from time to time be given thee, but abundant satisfying shall not be granted.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 49 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

The morning after you arrive is one of the quietest of the whole walk. You wake without the alarm that has run your life for weeks. The pack is by the bed, but it has nowhere to go. No yellow arrow is waiting outside, and no stage on the page. You lie there a minute, and then you notice a small and entirely unexpected feeling. It is something like vertigo. The road, which has been telling you where to be, has stopped, and nothing has taken its place.

People who have walked far often say that this morning is stranger than the arrival itself. The great thing has happened. You are tired and sunburnt and quietly proud. And yet there is a hollow in it. You had expected, without quite saying so, that the end of the road would answer something, and it has not. There is a café, a street, a queue for the bathroom. The day seems to be asking what you want to do with it.

Thomas à Kempis writes this moment, though he is not thinking of a road. In this chapter the voice that speaks to the reader is, in his telling, God's, and it speaks to a person who longs to be done with the labour and be at home, in what he calls the glorious liberty of the children of God. Already, it says, you long for the eternal home. But the hour is not yet come. There is still another season, a season of warfare, labour and testing. You want to be filled with the good you are seeking, and you cannot have it all at once.

It is not a comforting sentence at first. It sounds almost like a refusal. But read it as the morning after a long walk, and it begins to sound like a plain and accurate description. You have arrived somewhere, and it is not the place you were really aiming for. The place you were really aiming for was not a place.

The second half of the passage is blunter still. You must still be tried on earth, it says, and exercised in many things. Consolation will be given from time to time, but abundant satisfying will not be granted. That is a hard sentence to meet the day after a triumph. It might feel like a hand taken off your shoulder.

And yet there is something freeing in it. It tells you that your flatness is not a failure. You are not meant to be perpetually filled. The day after the great thing is an ordinary day, and the ordinary day is part of the road. A person who expects constant fulfilment will be disappointed, over and over. A person who expects consolation from time to time will be pleasantly surprised when it comes.

It is hard, quite openly, in two places. First, the phrases "warfare" and "probation" are heavy words, and belong to the language of a fifteenth-century cloister. You can read them more lightly as "more of life, with its work in it". Second, the passage says that what you long for is God, and that the answer is to wait. For some readers that is the beating heart of the matter. For others it is not what they were longing for at all. The longing may have been for rest, or forgiveness, or company, or a self they had misplaced, and it may have nothing to do with a name. Take the part that fits, and let the rest be.

There is also a plain, small honesty. Some of the hollow you may feel is only tiredness and the end of a routine. Do not read too much into it. A good breakfast does a great deal. And there is a gentler reading of "not yet": it means that the story is longer than the walk.

Let the day after be slow. Do not rush to fill the quiet. Wash your clothes, eat properly, walk a little without a destination, and see what the hollow does when it is not hurried. It may be the first space in weeks that is entirely yours.

## For the walk

- What was I hoping the end of the road would give me that it has not, and can I hold that without solving it?
- Walk a short way today with no destination at all. Go out for twenty minutes and come back. Stop when something interests you, and go on when you wish. Notice how it feels to move without being on your way anywhere. If you are already at home, a walk round the block will do.

## At the end of the day

The day after is nearly over, and you are probably more tired than you thought you would be. It is a different tiredness from the walking days: slower, more spread through the body, with less to show for it. You may have wandered, or sat, or slept. You may have done a lot of nothing, and felt oddly guilty about it.

Look back over the day and ask what you expected from it. Perhaps you thought you would feel finished, or free, or changed. Perhaps you expected a clear sense of what to do next. What did you actually feel? It may have been relief, or a faint sadness, or restlessness, or nothing very much. Name it, without trying to make it larger or smaller than it was.

Now ask what longing is still with you. There is almost always one. The walk may have taken the edge off it, or made it clearer, or only changed its shape. It need not be solved tonight. It is enough to know that it is there, and that it has not been dismissed.

And what was good about the day? A slow breakfast. Not having to be anywhere. A conversation with someone who is also at a loose end. A stretch of street that you saw properly for once. These are small, and they belong to the season the passage describes: the ordinary one, in which consolation is given from time to time.

It is worth remembering that you are not the first person to wake the morning after a long walk and not know what to do with your feet. The feeling is common enough that walkers talk about it as a kind of blues, and it passes more often than it stays. If you would like something small to look forward to, make a list of three ordinary pleasures for the coming week that have nothing to do with goals or stages.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I expect to feel today, and what did I actually feel?
- What longing is still with me, and can I let it stay unsolved for now?
- What is one ordinary thing I am looking forward to?

A word about weariness. The tiredness after an arrival is partly physical and partly emotional, and it is sometimes mistaken for sadness. Be gentle with it. Sleep, eat, drink water. If the flatness lasts for many days after you get home, and begins to feel heavy, say so to someone you trust, and ask for help. It is not a failure of the walk. It is a normal and common thing, and it can be eased.

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road I have walked, and for the quiet of this day after it. I have arrived, and I am not finished. Let me be patient with the part of me that wants more. Give me small consolations when I need them, and the sense to receive them. Let tomorrow be ordinary and kind. Let me sleep.*
