# About the book and its author

## The man

*The Imitation of Christ* is traditionally, and now generally, attributed to Thomas à Kempis. He was born about 1380 in Kempen, a small town near the Rhine in what is now Germany, which is where the *à Kempis* comes from: "of Kempen". He grew up in the Low Countries, in a reforming movement of the time called the Modern Devotion, which cared less for learned argument than for a plain, steady inner life. He joined a community of canons regular at Mount Saint Agnes, near Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands. Canons regular are priests who live together under a rule. He spent most of his long life there, copying manuscripts, reading and writing, and he died in 1471.

We know little else with certainty, and we will not pretend to. Scholars have debated the authorship of the book for centuries. The introductory note in the English edition we use mentions, among other names once put forward, Bernard of Clairvaux, the English writer Walter Hilton and the Parisian chancellor Jean Gerson, and calls Thomas the most probable author. That is still the common view. A reader who prefers to say "the author" is not wrong.

## The book

The Imitation was written in Latin, in the 1420s. It was copied by hand in great numbers, translated into many languages, and printed from the later fifteenth century on. The edition we use opens with a note that says that, apart from the Bible, no Christian writing has had so wide a following. The title comes from the heading of its first chapter, "Of the imitation of Christ".

It comes in four short books, each with its own heading in this edition:

- The first book, *Admonitions profitable for the spiritual life*: plain counsel on humility, silence, patience and bearing with other people.
- The second book, *Admonitions concerning the inner life*: how to live from within, and what to do when comfort goes away.
- The third book, *On inward consolation*: the most loved of the four. It is written as a conversation. A voice, which the book means as Christ's, speaks to the reader as "My Son", and the disciple answers. When a reading is taken from such a passage, the reflection says who is speaking.
- The fourth book, *Of the Sacrament of the Altar*: on Holy Communion. Two readings use lines from it that speak of patience and of shrines. We have not tried to explain or discuss the Sacrament. It belongs to those who share the author's faith.

## Why it suits a pilgrim

The book calls its reader a stranger and a pilgrim in more than one place. It is not about a destination. It is about how to walk: how to begin, what to do when you are tired, how to be among people, how to bear a bad day and a good one without being thrown by either. It is made of short chapters and can be read a little at a time. That is why it travelled so well in its own century, and why it can still sit in a pocket.

It is also a hard book, and it is a fifteenth-century book. In places Thomas is more severe with himself than most of us would care to be, and his pages on judgement and punishment, on despising the world and on obedience in a cloister are not here. They are all still in the original for anyone who wants them. We chose the passages that speak most plainly to someone on a road. Thomas writes "man" and "he" for everyone. We have left his words alone, and these readings are for anyone.

## The translation, the edition and the licence

The English in the passages is by the Reverend William Benham, published in 1886. Benham and Thomas are both long out of copyright, so the translation is in the public domain. It is available from Project Gutenberg as ebook number 1653, and every quotation in this book was checked against that text, word for word.

What we changed in the passages is nothing. We kept the old spellings and endings, and even a spot or two where the text looks like a printer's slip. We left out the small footnote numbers. Where we skip a stretch inside a passage, a mark like this shows it: …. The choice of the thirty passages, their order, the reflections, the questions and the prayers are ours and they are original. Of the thirty, eighteen come from the third book, five from the second, five from the first and two from the fourth.

Nothing of the Project Gutenberg licence text or trademark is copied here.
