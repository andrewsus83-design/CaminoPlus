# Day 18. Shrines and memorials

*What will you carry home from the place you walked to?*

## The passage

> Many run to diverse places to visit the memorials of departed Saints, and rejoice to hear of their deeds and to look upon the beautiful buildings of their shrines. … Often in looking at those memorials men are moved by curiosity and novelty, and very little fruit of amendment is borne away, especially when there is so much careless trifling and so little true contrition.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 4, chapter 1 (tr. William Benham)

## Reflection

The end of a pilgrimage has a particular noise. There is the square, wide and stone and full of people, and a great many of them are photographing each other. Some have walked for weeks, with the sunburn and the lean look to show for it, and some stepped off a coach twenty minutes ago. There are rucksacks stacked against the walls, and hugging, and the odd person lying flat on their back with their arms spread. It is joyful and tiring and slightly bewildering. Somebody is playing music. Everybody has, at once, arrived.

Whichever day you reach your own end, a cathedral square or a kitchen door, you will probably meet a version of it, and Thomas à Kempis has a sharp word for the moment. Many people run to various places, he says, to visit the memorials of departed saints, to hear of their deeds and look at the handsome buildings of their shrines. And then, in a second sentence: often, looking at those memorials, people are moved by curiosity and novelty, and very little fruit of amendment is borne away.

Amendment is the old word for change, for a life a little better afterwards. Thomas is asking a plain question. What goes home with you? Not what you saw. What you carry.

Note first where this sentence sits. It comes from the fourth book, in the chapter on receiving communion. In the sentence that we have left out, Thomas turns from shrines to the Sacrament, which is the subject of the fourth book, and sets it above any shrine. That sentence belongs to readers who share his faith, and this reading will not try to explain it or argue with it. What remains is the question about fruit, and that stands on its own.

It is a hard thing to be asked at the end of a long walk. You are tired, and perhaps moved, and a little giddy, and here is a man from the fifteenth century wondering whether any of this will make a difference. It sounds almost rude. But it is a good question, and it does not accuse you of anything. Pilgrimage has long had its sightseers and its seekers, and they are often the same people, on different days of the same road. Curiosity is human. Nobody is wrong to admire an old building, or to be curious about the person it remembers. The question is only what, in the end, you take away.

The hard word in the passage is "careless trifling". It would be easy to use it as a stick against the crowd in the square, and to feel a quiet superiority over the people who arrived by coach. That would be a mistake of the kind Thomas is always warning about. We are all a mixture of trifling and seeking, in the same hour, in the same body. A person with a camera may be quietly overwhelmed. A person with a rucksack may be thinking about lunch.

The passage also talks of "true contrition", of real sorrow for what has been wrong. That is a heavy phrase for a first-time reader, and not everyone will recognise it as their own. Take it more simply, if you like. A walk of this length tends to show a person things about themselves. The question is what you do with what you saw.

Fruit, in this sense, is quite small. It is not a transformation. It is something like: I will walk more slowly. I will ring my sister. I will stop saying yes to everything. I will not carry that grudge into the new year. A small, definite thing, chosen before you leave, and taken home like a stone in a pocket.

None of this is a demand. A pilgrimage that ends in a quiet decision to ring someone is not a smaller pilgrimage than one that ends in a vow. Small, definite things tend to last.

Whichever day you reach the place you were walking to, do not let the noise decide for you. Before you leave, find a quiet corner, a pew, a step, a wall, and sit for a few minutes. Ask what you would like to carry home, and let it be one thing.

## For the walk

- If I could take one small change home from this walk, what would it be?
- Before you leave the place you were walking to, find a quiet corner and sit for five minutes. Do not decide yet what the change is. Only ask the question, and see what rises. If you like, write one line on a scrap of paper and put it in your pocket. Choose a change small enough to survive an ordinary Monday, and keep it to yourself for now.

## At the end of the day

The evening after an arrival has its own odd flavour. You are probably tired in a way that is hard to describe: not the tiredness of the legs, which are full of pleasant complaint, but a more general exhaustion, as if you had been feeling things for several days without a break. You may also feel a little flat. People do. The great thing has happened, and the world has gone on, and there is a plate of ordinary food in front of you.

Look back over the day for what was actually there, and not for what was supposed to be. There may have been a moment of real feeling, in the square, in the church, in the face of someone you walked with. There may have been a stretch of noise and photographs and a sense of unreality. There may have been nothing special at all, and then, an hour later, in the middle of something quite ordinary, a lump in the throat. All of that is the day. None of it is wrong.

Think about what you came for. It may be that you set out for one reason and found another. It may be that you cannot say, even now. Ask whether it is still the same. There is no correct answer. The passage only wants you to ask, and to take the answer seriously.

And think of who is at home, and what you would like them to know. Not the facts of the trip, which can be told in an hour, but the thing you would like them to see in you. It may be easier to tell a friend than to tell yourself.

If you have your credencial with you, take it out and turn its pages slowly. Each stamp, each *sello*, from a hostel, a church or a café, is a small receipt for a day. Some will bring back faces. Some will look like nothing at all. Let them be what they are.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I come for, and is it the same as what I found?
- What one small change do I want to carry home?
- Who do I want to tell about this walk, and what do I want to say?

An honest word about tiredness. After an arrival there is a very large tiredness, and a very large amount of feeling. Do not try to settle the meaning of the walk tonight. It will take weeks, perhaps years, to see what it was. For now it is enough to eat, to sleep, and to know that you got here. The fruit, as the passage says, is borne away. It does not have to be picked tonight.

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the road and for the place it led to. Let me not mistake the noise for the fruit. Show me, when I am rested, one small thing to carry home, and give me the courage to keep it. Let those who arrived with me, and those who did not, be held in the same gentle light. Let me sleep, and let the walk sink in.*
