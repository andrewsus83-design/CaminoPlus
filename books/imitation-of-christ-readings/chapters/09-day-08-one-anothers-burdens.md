# Day 8. One another's burdens

*Who is carrying something for you, and what are you carrying for them?*

## The passage

> Endeavour to be patient in bearing with other men’s faults and infirmities whatsoever they be, for thou thyself also hast many things which have need to be borne with by others. … But now hath God thus ordained, that we may learn to bear one another’s burdens, because none is without defect, none without a burden, none sufficient of himself, none wise enough of himself; but it behoveth us to bear with one another, to comfort one another, to help, instruct, admonish one another. How much strength each man hath is best proved by occasions of adversity: for such occasions do not make a man frail, but show of what temper he is.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 1, chapter 16 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Half past five in a dormitory is a good place to learn about people. Somewhere in the dark a plastic bag is being unpacked, one rustle at a time, by someone who has clearly decided that if they go slowly enough it will not count. Across the room there is a head torch, which sweeps your face as it passes. Beneath you, or above you, there is a man who has been snoring since ten in a steady, patient, thoroughly committed way. You lie there composing remarks.

Then, if you are lucky, you remember the sentence that Thomas à Kempis put at the front of this passage: *thou thyself also hast many things which have need to be borne with by others.* Somebody in this room, some night, has lain awake because of you. It may be the cough you do not notice, the zip, the late phone call, the boots. We rarely see ourselves as the irritant. Everyone else is the difficult one. Thomas, with his usual unflattering clarity, turns the sentence round.

The second half of the passage goes deeper. We are meant, he says, to learn to carry one another's burdens, because nobody is without a fault, nobody without a weight, nobody sufficient or wise enough alone. He lists what we owe each other: to bear with, to comfort, to help, to instruct, to correct. It is a small catalogue of what a community does, and a road is a kind of temporary community. The pilgrims who share a table tonight may never meet again, and yet for an evening they act like a family. Plasters pass from hand to hand. A charger is lent. Somebody mentions that the next stretch is longer than the sign suggests, and somebody else is glad to know. Water is shared. It happens so often that it is easy to forget it is a form of the thing Thomas is describing.

Then comes a line that deserves to be read slowly. Adversity, he says, does not make a person frail but shows what sort of person they are. Anyone who has watched other walkers in the rain will know how true that is. Tired, hungry, wet and late, people stop performing. Some turn out to be extraordinarily kind. Some discover a short temper they did not know they had. And you, in the same weather, will find out the same about yourself, sometimes with a small surprise.

There are two places where this passage is hard. The first is the idea of bearing with other people, which has a shadow side. Patience is a virtue when it takes a snorer's night on the chin. It is not a virtue when it puts up with something that is actually harmful: a person who makes you unsafe, who frightens you, who will not stop. Thomas is speaking of ordinary faults and infirmities, the everyday friction of people living close together. For anything beyond that, say so, and say it to the host or to someone you trust. Bearing with does not mean staying silent about harm.

The second hard place is the phrase "none sufficient of himself". Pride of self-sufficiency is a very common thing on the road. Some walkers insist on carrying every burden alone, refusing a lift, refusing a plaster, refusing a word of advice. It looks like strength. It is often a kind of loneliness. To receive help is a good deal harder than to give it, and the passage quietly asks you to do both.

And there is a plain, honest doubt. Some days you do not like people. You do not want to bear with them, comfort them or admonish them. You want to be left alone with your bread and your map. The passage does not demand that you like anybody. It only suggests that you will need to be borne with yourself, soon, and that a small courtesy given today will be useful credit tomorrow.

Two small things would be enough. Let one irritation go by without remark. And, once, give or ask for a bit of help: a spare minute, a bit of tape, a seat made room for, a hand with a strap.

## For the walk

- Whose faults am I carrying today, and who is carrying mine? If you can, think of one person by name, and let your thoughts about them soften a little.
- Do one small kindness before the next stop, and ask for one small kindness in return: directions, a moment's company, a spare hand. Take it with thanks, and notice how the day feels when you have both given and received. If that feels too much, simply greet someone you would normally walk past.

## At the end of the day

The long table in the shared kitchen is a place where the day is gathered up. Someone has made too much pasta and is offering it round. Someone is peeling an orange with great concentration. In three languages, nobody quite understands what anybody else is saying, and the conversation goes on perfectly well.

Think back over the people you met today. Some you will not remember by next week. Some will stay with you a long time, for no reason you could easily explain. Was there someone who was hard to bear? It may be the person from the early morning, with the bag, or someone in the afternoon who talked too much, or too little. Is there another way to see them? Perhaps they were frightened, or tired, or lonely, or hurting in a way that you could not see. You do not need to forgive anything tonight, only to loosen your grip a little.

And was there anyone who bore with you? It is worth noticing, because it often happens without a word. A person who gave up the lower bunk. Someone who let you pass on a narrow path, or waited while you found your map. A host who said nothing about the time. Our own faults are carried by other people far more often than we know.

If you gave help, however small, hold on to it for a moment, and let it be a small gladness. If you received help and found it hard to take, notice that too.

The last word on Thomas's list, to correct, is the delicate one. On a road where nobody has any standing, it usually means the gentlest sort of honesty: telling a friend that their pace is hurting them, or that their silence is worrying you. If there is someone like that, you might think tonight about whether a word is owed, and how kindly it could be said.

Three questions for tonight:

- Whose patience did I use today without noticing?
- Who did I find hard to bear, and what might they be carrying that I cannot see?
- What small help could I offer, or ask for, tomorrow?

Tiredness, honestly. Patience is the first thing to go when you are tired. The person who snapped at five o'clock was not the person you would like to be, but they were tired, and so was everyone else in the room. Do not judge the whole of yourself by the six o'clock version. You can begin again with the people in the morning, when everyone is a little kinder, a little more rested, and a little more ready to be borne with. Many a night's grievance has dissolved over the first coffee.

## A short prayer

*For the people who carried me today without saying so, thank you. For the ones I found hard, let me see them more gently. Forgive my own noise and my own faults, and let them be lightly borne. Let me be glad to help, and not ashamed to ask. Let those who snore be forgiven, and those who lie awake be patient. Let this room sleep in peace.*
