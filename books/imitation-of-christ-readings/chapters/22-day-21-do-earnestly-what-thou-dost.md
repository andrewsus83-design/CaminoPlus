# Day 21. Do earnestly what thou dost

*What will you do tomorrow, and can you do it as if it mattered?*

## The passage

> My Son, let not the labours which thou hast undertaken for Me break thee down, nor let tribulations cast thee down in any wise, but let my promise strengthen and comfort thee in every event. … Do earnestly what thou dost; labour faithfully in My vineyard; I will be thy reward. Write, read, sing, weep, be silent, pray, endure adversities manfully; eternal life is worthy of all these conflicts, yea, and of greater.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 47 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Somewhere at the back of the cupboard, or by the door, are the boots. They are drying, or not. They are the colour of the road. A week from now they will have been put away, and the shell, if you have one, will be on a hook. And on some perfectly ordinary morning a few days after that, you will do something quite small, wash a cup, answer a message, catch a bus, and realise that you are in the middle of the next stage. It has no waymark. It does not look like a pilgrimage. It is the rest of your life.

That is where this book ends, and so does Thomas à Kempis, more or less, in the passage that closes the day. In this chapter the voice that speaks to the reader is, in his telling, God's, and it begins with encouragement: do not let the labours you have undertaken break you down, and do not let troubles cast you down. Let the promise strengthen and comfort you in whatever happens. Then comes a very practical sentence: *Do earnestly what thou dost.*

Do, in other words, whatever you are doing, and do it with your whole self. Not half on the task and half on the next one, not with one eye on the phone. The washing-up. The spreadsheet. The conversation with a person you find tiresome. Labour faithfully, he says, and the reward will not be an outcome but a presence. The old word is "earnestly", and it means something close to wholeheartedly.

Then there is the list, which is one of the loveliest in the book. *Write, read, sing, weep, be silent, pray, endure adversities manfully.* It is an ordinary person's list. It is more or less everything a human being does on a given day, set down without ranking. Notice that "weep" is on it, between singing and silence, as something to be done, and done earnestly, like the rest. Nobody is asked to be cheerful. They are asked to be present for whatever the day brings, whether that is a song, or tears, or a long quiet.

For a pilgrim there is a gentle echo here of the road itself. A walker's day is a very simple thing: get up, walk, eat, rest, talk, sleep. And yet it had a depth that ordinary days lack. It was not that the tasks were different. It was that they were done with attention, because there was nothing else to do. The passage suggests that this attention is not a gift of the Camino. It is a way of doing things, and it can be done at a sink.

Now the hard places, because this is the last reading and honesty is owed. "Endure adversities manfully" is a phrase of its century. You can hear it as courage, which is its best sense, and set aside the rest. The same goes for the promise of "eternal life" worth all these conflicts. Some readers will find that strengthening, and some will find that it asks too much of them. Take whatever of it is yours. It would also be wrong to hear the opening advice, not to let the labours break you down, as a simple instruction. Some labours do break people. There are times when what is needed is not more endurance but rest, or help, or a change. "Labour faithfully" does not mean "labour without limit".

Notice too how little the passage says about results. It does not promise that the labour will succeed, or that anyone will be impressed. It asks only that the work be done wholeheartedly, and leaves the rest. That is a relief to anyone who has ever been worn out by the outcome of things.

Then the ordinary doubt. Will any of this survive a Monday? Perhaps not all. The practice that was clear on the road may fade, and the passages may sit unread on a shelf. But a person who has once done something earnestly, with their whole attention, knows what it is like, and that is hard to forget entirely. You can find your way back. That is what the end of the road is for.

Pick the next ordinary thing you will do. Do it as if it mattered, because it does. Then the next. That is all it is.

## For the walk

- What is the next ordinary thing I am going to do, and can I do it with my whole attention?
- Choose one small task today, washing a cup, making a bed, writing a message, and do it slowly and completely, as if there were nothing else to do. When you have finished, notice what that was like. Then take the question with you into the next task, and the one after that.

## At the end of the day

This is the last evening of the three weeks. You may find that nothing particular marks it. There is no ceremony, no bell. There is a day, and it has ended, like the others, in a room with a light in it. That is quite right. The point of the passage is that most of what matters is not marked.

Look back over the day, as you have learned to, and ask what you did earnestly. Something, almost certainly. A task done properly. A conversation in which you were really listening. A meal eaten without a screen. A stretch of walking in which you were fully there. Give it its due. These are the moments that hold the rest together, and they are hard to notice, because they feel like nothing.

Then go through Thomas's list as if it were a checklist of the day. Did you write anything? Read? Sing, or hum? Was there a moment of tears, or near-tears? Was there silence? Was there prayer, or something like it? Not every day contains all of these, and it does not need to. But if there is one on the list that you have left out for a long time, tonight is a fair time to notice it. A person who never weeps, or never sings, or never sits in silence, is missing a part of the day.

A quiet thought, if you want one: you are probably not the person who began this book, and not the person who began the walk. You may not be able to say how. That is how real change usually looks from inside. It looks like an ordinary day.

And think about tomorrow, quite lightly. Not as a plan, but as a beginning, the way you learned to on the fourth day. What is the first small thing you will do? Name it, and leave the rest.

If you have kept the habit of the evening questions, you may notice that they have become quicker. That is how a practice settles. It gets shorter, not longer. And the book has no more days, which is not a loss. You can go back to any of them. The fourth day will always be there, with its good beginning.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I do today with my whole attention?
- Which item on Thomas's list, write, read, sing, weep, be silent, pray, have I been leaving out?
- What will I begin tomorrow, and how small can I make it?

An honest word about tiredness. You may feel very tired tonight, or not particularly tired at all, and either is fine. If there is a feeling of sadness at the end of the book, that is natural, too. Things end. But this is not an ending in any sense that matters. You have a way of walking, and a set of small practices, and a few words that were worth keeping. All of that goes on with you.

## A short prayer

*Thank you for these three weeks, and for every step of them. Let me do whatever comes next with an honest, whole heart. Let me write and read, sing and weep, be silent and pray, as each day asks. Where I forget, let me begin again. Where I am tired, let me rest. Walk with me, and let me walk well. Let tomorrow be a good beginning, and the day after it.*
