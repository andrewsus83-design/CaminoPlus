# Day 7. A place to rest

*Can you stop, and let stopping be enough?*

## The passage

> Grant unto me, above all things that I can desire, to rest in Thee, and that in Thee my heart may be at peace. Thou art the true peace of the heart, Thou alone its rest; apart from Thee all things are hard and unquiet. In Thee alone, the supreme and eternal God, *I will lay me down in peace and take my rest*.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 15 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

The first rest day is stranger than it sounds. After a week of walking the body asks for it, and you give it a day, and the day arrives, empty and slow. You wake without an alarm. You do not need to be anywhere. The town goes on with its ordinary day as if you were not there, and you sit with a coffee and discover a sensation you had forgotten: you do not know what to do with your hands.

Many people find resting harder than walking. Walking has a purpose built into it. Rest has none, and into the gap come all the usual visitors: guilt, restlessness, the arithmetic of kilometres lost, the quiet suspicion that you ought to be improving yourself. It is common to fill a rest day with chores and errands and to reach the evening more tired than a walking day would have left you.

The passage is a prayer, the disciple's answer to the voice that has been speaking to him in his book. It asks for one thing above everything he can desire: to rest. And it says where. Not in a place, and not in an achievement, but in God, whom he calls the true peace of the heart, its only rest. Apart from that, he says, all things are hard and unquiet. The last words are borrowed from a psalm, and in the psalm they are about lying down to sleep.

A reader who does not pray as Thomas did may still hear what is going on underneath. He is saying that rest is not only a matter of the body. You can lie in the best bed in Spain and not rest. You can sit in a plain church for ten minutes and be quieter inside than you have been all month. The body can be helped by sleep and soup, and they are good things, but the part of us that keeps running after the body has stopped needs a different kind of place.

There is a hard side to that. "Apart from Thee all things are hard and unquiet" is a large claim, and not every reader will accept it as stated. Some find peace in a landscape, a friend, a quiet routine, work well done. Thomas does not forbid those. He is talking about a deeper layer, and he is sure it needs God. We can leave that as it is. Each reader will know whether it fits.

Often the mind, given nothing to do, begins to review the whole week at speed: every conversation, every small mistake. Let it, if it will, but ask it to be kind. A rest day is a poor time for a trial.

There is also a more ordinary difficulty. Rest is a skill, and it can be out of practice. Nobody mentions this on the first day of a long walk, when everyone is thinking about distance and pace. By the seventh day you learn that stopping takes some learning too. You have to stop doing the things that feel like progress: counting, planning, checking the next stage. You have to let a slow hour be slow.

It also happens that the first rest is not chosen. An injury, a storm, a closed bridge, a body that simply says no. If that is how the day has come to you, the passage has something kind to say. A place to rest is not a reward for walking. It is part of the walk, and it was always going to be needed.

Many traditions set aside one day in seven for stopping, and it was never meant as an indulgence. It was meant as a way of staying human. A pilgrim who cannot stop is only a very determined traveller.

What does it ask of an ordinary day, then? That for a few hours you do less than you planned. That you let a meal take an hour. That you sit on a bench with nothing in your hands and see what the restlessness does when it is not fed. It rises, it peaks, and, if you wait, it falls. Somewhere beyond it there is a quiet that was waiting to be noticed.

## For the walk

- When did I last stop without something to do next, and what did it feel like?
- Sit for five minutes today with nothing in your hands: no phone, no map, no book. Watch the urge to do something rise and fall like a wave. When it has passed, stay one minute longer. If sitting is hard, count ten slow breaths and begin again.

## At the end of the day

One week. Seven mornings of boots and straps, and tonight there are no boots on the floor, because for once they have not been used. You may find that the evening of a rest day has a different quality from the others. It is softer, and a little slower. You are tired in a new way: not from distance, but from stopping.

Look back over the day, as you have been learning to. How did it go? Be honest: was it rest, or was it restlessness in a different place? Perhaps you spent the morning at the washing line, and the afternoon looking up the next stages on your phone. Perhaps you slept more than you thought you could. Perhaps there was a moment, in a church or on a bench or in a bath, when everything simply went quiet. If there was, hold it. If there was not, this is also normal. It sometimes takes the second rest day to learn how to have the first.

And since it is the seventh evening, you might glance back at the whole week as well. Not to judge it. Only to see it, the way you might look back along a road from a rise: how far you came, which bends you do not remember, what was harder than you thought, and what was easier. Notice, too, who you have met, and which faces you would be glad to see again.

After a rest day it is tempting to plan: tomorrow's stage, how to make up for the lost time. Leave that for the morning. A day of rest has not cost you a day.

If you spent part of today in a church or some other quiet corner, notice what you did there. Not what you should have done. Some people sit and say nothing at all. Some light a candle. Some simply look at the ceiling for a long while. All of that is rest, and all of it counts.

Three small questions for tonight:

- What did I let go of today that I would usually have clung to?
- When, if at all, did I feel properly rested, and where was I?
- What do I want to carry from this week into the next one, and what can stay behind?

An honest word about tiredness. A rest day can bring a tiredness of its own, the kind that arrives when the body relaxes and tells you the real total. If you are more tired tonight than you were last night, that is not a sign that the day failed. It means the body has finally been given room to say what it has been holding. Let it say it, and sleep. If sleep does not come at once, that is common after a rest day: the body has been quiet all afternoon and has less to ask of the night. Lie still, unclench your hands and your jaw, and let the thoughts go by like traffic. You are resting even when you are awake.

## A short prayer

*I have walked, and now I have stopped, and both were good. Let this bed be enough. Let the town sleep, and let me sleep with it. Whatever I have been running after, let it wait until morning. Give my body its rest and my heart its quiet, and let me wake ready for the road. Let the week behind me be a gift and not a debt.*
