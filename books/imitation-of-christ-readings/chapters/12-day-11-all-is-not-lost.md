# Day 11. All is not lost

*What if the bad day is not the whole story?*

## The passage

> What doth care about future events bring thee, save sorrow upon sorrow? Sufficient for the day is the evil thereof. … It is vain and useless to be disturbed or lifted up about future things which perhaps will never come. … When thou thinkest thyself far removed from Me, I am often the nearer. When thou reckonest that almost all is lost, then often is greater opportunity of gain at hand. All is not lost when something goeth contrary to thy wishes.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 30 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Some days go wrong in a row. A wrong turn, then a long road in the rain, then a village with its one café shut, then the news that the bed you were counting on is not there. By four o'clock the mind has stopped dealing with the day and begun to deal with the future. If this is how it is today, how will it be tomorrow? If the plan has failed once, why will it work later? You start rehearsing disasters that have not happened, with great skill and in considerable detail.

Thomas à Kempis knew the habit, and this passage is aimed at it. In this chapter the voice that speaks to the reader is, in his telling, God's, answering a person in trouble. And what it says is practical. What does worry about the future bring you, it asks, except sorrow upon sorrow? Then it quotes a line from the Gospel of Matthew, in the old translation: *Sufficient for the day is the evil thereof.* Each day has enough of its own. Do not borrow tomorrow's.

Next it says that it is vain and useless to be disturbed about future things that may never come. The word "perhaps" is doing important work. Most of the disasters we rehearse on a bad afternoon never turn up. The rain stops. The next village has a bed. The blister is tended, and heals. The mind, after all, is very bad at forecasting.

Then comes the line that gives the passage its warmth. When you think you are far from help, it says, help is often nearer. When you reckon that almost everything is lost, there is often a better chance of gain at hand. And then, quietly: nothing is lost just because something has gone against what you wished.

There is a great deal of ordinary road experience in this. Walkers tell stories, and tell them often, of the day that went wrong and turned out to be the one they remember. The wrong turn that led through a village they would never have seen. The closed door that led to a parish hall, a kind stranger, a pot of soup. Thomas is not promising that this always happens. He says "often", and it is a careful word. But the shape is familiar, and it is worth remembering at four o'clock.

Where is it hard? The passage is easily misread as "everything will work out", and that is not a promise anyone can honestly make. Some losses are real. A death, an injury that ends the walk, a job gone, a friendship broken: none of these is answered by "all is not lost". The sentence here is about something smaller: a day, a plan, a wish that went contrary. It does not say that nothing has been lost. It says that not everything has. In the case of a great loss this book has less to offer, and it says so plainly. A person carrying that should have other people close, and good help.

There is a second difficulty. The passage speaks of feeling far from help, and it hints that feeling is not the same as fact. That is true, and it is also hard to hear. When you are cold and hungry and discouraged, the feeling of being alone is as convincing as any fact. All Thomas suggests is that you hold it a little more loosely, and do not treat today's mood as tomorrow's forecast.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? That at the worst moment you stop, and look around, and count what is still intact. Legs, a little water, some bread, the road ahead, a day that will end. That you leave tomorrow to tomorrow. And that, when the plan collapses, you wait a minute before you decide what the collapse means. The first reading of a bad moment is rarely the right one. The mind's first account of a bad day is usually its worst. Walkers who have had such a day sometimes find, a week on, that they can hardly remember the details. The feeling stays, but the facts have faded.

You do not need to feel consoled. You only need to let the door stay slightly open for something unexpected. It sometimes comes in the shape of a person, and sometimes in the shape of a bench. Sometimes it is just the next kilometre turning out to be easier than the last.

## For the walk

- What have I decided is lost today that may only be delayed or changed? Be as specific as you can.
- At the next moment when something goes wrong, stop for two minutes before you react. Look around and name three things that are still all right: your legs, a bit of water, the light. Then decide what to do next. If you cannot find three, find one, and look at it properly.

## At the end of the day

You are in the place you ended up, which may or may not be the place you meant to be. Whatever the plan was this morning, it has been revised, cancelled, replaced, or has quietly turned out to have been fine all along. The bed is a bed. The ceiling is a ceiling. You are indoors, and you are fed, or soon will be.

Think back over what went against you today. It might have been large or small: a wrong turn, a closed door, rain, a conversation that went badly, a shoe that rubbed. Take them one at a time, and ask what each one turned out to be. Did the wrong turn cost you an hour or give you something? Was the closed café the end of a plan or the start of another? Some of them will have been simply bad, with nothing hidden in them. Let them be that, without making a lesson of it. Not every setback is secretly a gift.

But look, too, for anything that came at the edge of the trouble. A person who turned up. A decision made for you that you were glad of afterwards. A moment of unexpected kindness at the lowest point of the afternoon. If there was one, it is worth saying thank you for it, silently or aloud.

Then the future, which has probably been knocking at the door all evening. Tomorrow's weather. Tomorrow's distance. Tomorrow's bed. Leave it outside. It will still be there in the morning, and it will be easier to deal with when you are fed and rested. Tonight's work is only to put the day down.

A small ritual for the end of a day like this: say, or write, three things that went right, however trivial. A hot shower. A dry pair of socks. A kind word. It is not that the bad things did not happen. It is that the mind tends to keep only one side of the ledger, and tonight you can put in the other.

If someone near you has had a worse day than yours, a word costs very little.

Three questions for tonight:

- What went against my wishes today, and what did it actually turn out to be?
- What am I afraid of tomorrow that may never come?
- What was still intact today that I forgot to count?

An honest word about tiredness. The mind that is tired is a catastrophist. It reads every small thing as a sign and every sign as a warning. If tonight everything seems to be going wrong, remember that you are judging the whole of the road from the lowest point of a long day. Eat, wash, lie down. The sentence about "all is not lost" can wait for you until you are rested enough to believe it.

## A short prayer

*Let me put down the day's worries here. Whatever went wrong, let it be over. Whatever I fear for tomorrow, let it wait until morning. Be near me where I thought I was alone. Let me close this day gently. Let me sleep, knowing that not everything is lost, and that something good may already be on its way.*
