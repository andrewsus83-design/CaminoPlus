# Carry it with you

You have come to the end of the three weeks, or you have decided to stop early, or to keep going slowly. All three are fine. This book does not give out certificates. It was meant to be company for a road, and a road has no use for a book once the road is over, except to be remembered kindly.

So the only question left is what to keep.

Not everything. Nobody carries a whole book, and it would be a poor sign if you did. Choose three small things, one of each kind.

- **A line.** Something Thomas said, or something we said about it, that stayed in your head. It might be *Do earnestly what thou dost*. It might be *All is not lost*, or the plain question from the ninth day, *And if thou didst know it, what wouldst thou do?* Write it on a card and put it where you will see it.
- **A practice.** One of the small things from the end of each morning: three breaths before the first step, an hour without music, a thank-you said slowly, a stone taken out of a boot. Pick the one that cost you least and helped most. Do it again tomorrow.
- **A question.** Some of the evening questions will have gone round in your mind for days. Keep the one that did. A good question is better company than an answer, and it travels light.

If you can keep all three for a month, you will have kept more than most people keep from anything.

## How to go on

Go back whenever you like. The days are short, and they were written to be read more than once. A day that did nothing for you in the second week may say a great deal in the sixth. Read them in another order. Read just the evening part for a week. Read a single passage aloud on a bad morning and nothing else.

You might also read Thomas himself. His book is old and uneven, and long stretches of it are not in these pages, some of them left out on purpose. But it is in the public domain, in the same translation we used, and you will find it at Project Gutenberg, ebook number 1653. Some people read a page at a time, and pause when something catches. That is how it was written to be read.

And be honest about what did not work. Some days will have rubbed. Some passages will have asked for more than you could give, or less than you hoped for. That is allowed. A book of this kind is not a test. If a day did not speak to you, nothing has gone wrong. Leave it, and take what did.

## A last word

Thomas à Kempis spent most of a long life in a small community in the Low Countries, copying and writing and living alongside its other members, trying to live a plain and honest life. He would have been astonished to find himself a companion on a road in Spain six centuries later. But he would not have been surprised by what the road does. It takes away the things that were never needed, and leaves a person with their own breath, their own feet and a few honest questions.

In one of his chapters he calls the reader a wayfaring man. It is an old and a good phrase. A wayfarer is someone who is going on. You do not have to know where. You do not have to be strong, or certain, or finished. You need only a next step, and a little patience with yourself, and now and then a stranger's kindness at the right moment.

Walk again. Next year, if you can. Or tomorrow, round the block, with your phone left behind and your hands open.

*Buen Camino.*
