# Day 10. He doth much who loveth much

*What are you counting, and who are you counting it for?*

## The passage

> Without charity no work profiteth, but whatsoever is done in charity, however small and of no reputation it be, bringeth forth good fruit; for God verily considereth what a man is able to do, more than the greatness of what he doth. … He doth much who loveth much. He doth much who doth well. … He envieth none, because he longeth for no selfish joy; nor doth he desire to rejoice in himself, but longeth to be blessed in God as the highest good.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 1, chapter 15 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

"How far did you walk today?" It is the standard question on a long road, and everyone asks it, usually before asking a name. The answer is a number, and the number does a surprising amount of work. It places you. It tells the other person whether you are ahead or behind, strong or cautious, keen or sensible. Most of us have felt it, that small flicker of pleasure at a large figure, and that small flinch at a modest one. Phones help. They keep count of steps, hours, climbs, and offer a tidy summary at the end of the day.

There is nothing wrong with numbers. You need some of them to plan a stage, to book a bed, to work out the water. The trouble starts when they turn into a ranking. Then they stop being information and become a verdict, and everyone on the road is quietly marked out of ten.

Thomas à Kempis, who never carried a phone, had a different yardstick, and it is worth reading carefully. He says that any work done in love, however small and unnoticed, bears good fruit, and that God, as he puts it, considers what a person is able to do, more than the greatness of what they do. The measure is not the size of the deed. It is the size of the deed against what you had to give.

That changes the arithmetic. A tired person who walks twelve kilometres on a day when twelve is all there is has done more, in this reckoning, than a strong person who walks thirty on a day when thirty was easy. The one who gives up a seat, or waits for a slower friend, or says a kind thing at the end of a bad day, may have done a great deal, even if it appears nowhere on any screen.

Then come two short sentences that seem almost too simple. *He doth much who loveth much. He doth much who doth well.* The first puts love ahead of quantity. The second puts care ahead of speed. One way to read them together is as a practical test: not how much, but how lovingly and how well. Anyone who has watched a person bandage a blister with real attention, or fill a stranger's bottle without being asked, will know what is being measured.

In the passage's last sentence the effect appears. A person who loves in this way, he says, envies nobody, because they long for no selfish joy. Envy is the shadow of ranking. As soon as you stop trying to win, there is no one left to envy. The strong walker ahead of you is not your competitor. They are someone with a different body and a different road.

Here the passage is hard in two places. First, "of no reputation" is a demanding phrase. Almost everyone wants to be seen doing well, and it is odd, and a little painful, to be asked to do something good that no one will ever notice. The old advice to do small things well in secret runs against the grain of a world of photographs and totals. Second, "doth well" has a perfectionist's shadow. It does not mean that you must do everything flawlessly or not at all. A blister dressed clumsily and kindly is still a kindness.

There is also a plain doubt. Numbers are comforting. They give a day a shape. To walk without them, even for an hour, can feel like losing a handrail. That is a fair feeling, and it is no failure of character. The passage does not ask you to burn your pedometer. It asks only that you notice what is being measured, and by whom, and whether the measure is yours.

"Charity", in Thomas's old English, means love, not donations. It is the thing that gives a small act its weight. If you want a one-line version of the whole passage, it might be this: your effort is measured against your own capacity, and that is the only fair scale.

For one stretch of today, walk without counting, and ask yourself only whether you are walking well. And do one small good thing without telling anyone.

## For the walk

- What am I counting today, and who am I counting it for? Ask it without judging the answer.
- For one hour, do not look at the distance, the time or the steps. Ask only, every so often, "Am I walking well?" And do one small good thing quietly, so that nobody notices, and leave it unmentioned. If you catch yourself composing tonight's number in your head, smile at it and let it go.

## At the end of the day

The evening begins, as it always does, with a question that is also a number. "How far today?" You may have heard it two or three times already, over the washing up, across the table, in the queue for the shower. Think about how it felt to answer. Did you hear yourself adjusting the figure, rounding it up, saying it a little too lightly? Did you feel better or worse once you had heard someone else's?

Look back over the day with the passage's yardstick in your hand, which is capacity and not size. What did you do today that was large for you? It may have been nothing that would sound impressive: a hill taken slowly, a hard stretch finished, a conversation you were afraid to have. Give it its proper weight. Be fair to yourself on the scale that counts.

What did you do that no one saw? If you did something small and kind today, and it passed without a word, hold it for a moment. It is the kind of thing the passage means. If you cannot think of anything, you may have done several without noticing. Most of what is done in love goes unrecorded, including by the person who did it.

And was there anyone you envied? It is a very human thing, and it appears with surprising force on long walks. It may have been someone faster, or lighter, or quieter, or simply someone who seemed to be enjoying it more. Name what you wanted from them. It is often something other than what you first thought, and it may be something you can find for yourself.

If you did walk far today, you do not need to apologise for it. If you walked little, you do not need to explain. The passage cuts both ways. And there is a gentler number to end on, if you want one: the number of people who were kind to you today. It is often larger than the distance feels. If you spent part of the day with someone who ranks everything, be gentle with them too. The habit is a tired one, and a lot of it comes from fear of being left behind.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I count today, and did counting help me or hurt me?
- What did I do today that nobody saw, and does it matter to me that nobody did?
- Whom did I envy, and what did they have that I actually wanted?

A word about being tired. Comparing is worst when you are tired. At the end of a hard day, the walker who looks fresh and tells you cheerfully how far they went can seem like a rebuke. They are not. They are another person with another body and another history. The stage you walked is the stage you walked, and it was enough. Let the numbers go quiet for the night, and let the day be only what it was.

## A short prayer

*For the small things done today that nobody counted, thank you. Let me be satisfied with what I was able to give. Take the measuring tape out of my hands tonight. Where I envied, let me wish well. Where I was proud, let me smile at it. Let love be my measure, and let it be enough. Let me sleep without being ranked.*
