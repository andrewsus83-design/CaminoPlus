# Day 14. My silence speaketh

*What can you say when the words run out?*

## The passage

> O Jesus, Light of Eternal Glory, solace of the wandering soul, before Thee my mouth is without speech, and my silence speaketh to Thee. How long will my Lord delay to come unto me? Let Him come unto me, His poor and humble one, and make me glad. … “Behold, here I am! Behold, I come to thee, for thou didst call Me. Thy tears and the longing of thy soul, thy humbleness and contrition of heart have inclined Me, and brought Me to thee.”
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 21 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

At the end of the second week there is often an afternoon with a door in it. It might be a church, left open for the hour, with a smell of old stone and candle wax and the cool that hits you as you step in from the heat. It might be a chapel by a crossing, or a stone wall in the shade with nobody about. You have no particular reason to go in. But the legs slow down, and you find that you are sitting, with the pack beside you, and that you have nothing at all to say.

That is the state this passage describes. The words in it are the disciple's, and they are among the most tender in the whole book. Before You, he says, my mouth is without speech, and my silence speaks to You. The title he gives to God is *solace of the wandering soul*, and it is hard for a walker to read it without a small start. A wandering soul. A solace for the one who is on the move and has not arrived.

His silence is not an absence of prayer. It is a form of it. Most people know this from the other side. Anybody who has sat with a friend in grief, or in the hospital, or in a car outside a house they cannot bring themselves to enter, has felt that some silences say more than anything that could be said aloud. They are full. They hold the whole of what is there, without making it smaller by explaining it.

Then, in the next sentence, the silence breaks, and what comes out is an honest complaint. How long will my Lord delay to come to me? It is the kind of question prayer is allowed to ask. It is impatient and hurt and a little sulky, and it comes from the same man whose silence we have just admired. The old books of prayer are full of such moments. They do not clean up the longing into something polite. They say: I have been waiting. Where are You?

And then, in the reply, the passage turns. *Behold, here I am.* The voice does not argue or explain. It says, I am here, because you called. Your tears and the longing of your soul have drawn me. It is a short, warm answer, and it comes at once.

This may be the hard part. In this book the answer comes within a few lines, and in life it often does not. A great many people sit in a quiet church, or on a wall, or in a bunk, and hear nothing. Some hear a great deal that is not comforting. Thomas knows this too, and a good deal of his book is about the long dry stretches. But in this chapter he offers the moment of answer as a possibility, and we can take it as one without insisting that it has to be ours.

There is a second hard thing. For a person in real grief, silence may be the only honest speech, and it may not feel like a solace at all. It may feel like a room with the furniture taken out. If that is where you are, nothing here asks you to fill it. You are not required to feel consoled, or to feel anything. To sit in the room, and let it be as empty as it is, is already a kind of answer to the question the passage asks.

And there is plain doubt. You may sit in the shade and wonder whether anyone is listening at all. That is a fair thing to wonder, and the passage does not scold you for it. The disciple is wondering too.

For today, a few minutes with no words will do. You do not need a church, though a church, where one is open, will often give you the quiet for nothing. Sit, with your hands open on your knees. Let whatever is there be there. If a word comes, let it be a small one.

## For the walk

- If I had no words at all, what would my silence say? Do not answer yet. Carry it.
- Find a quiet place today, a church if there is one open, a wall in the shade if not, and sit for five minutes with your hands open on your knees. Say nothing. If a word comes, let it be a single small one: "here". When the five minutes are over, get up slowly, and carry a little of the quiet into the next hour.

## At the end of the day

You have reached the end of the second week, and the room is as it is. There may be a window, with the light going. There may be a few people already asleep, a few still talking quietly. Tonight, the day's last word may not need to be a word.

Look back at the day for its silences. Not the ones you chose, necessarily, but the ones that arrived. There may have been a long stretch of the morning when nobody spoke. There may have been a moment of stillness in a doorway or at a crossing, when something simply stopped. Perhaps you stood in front of a view and could not say what you felt. Perhaps you stood at a doorway and went on. Whatever these were, they were part of the day, and they may be the part that stays.

Then ask a harder question, if you are able to. What did you carry today that has no words? There is almost always something: a loss, a hope, a person, a question that has been with you since before you set out. It does not have to be named tonight. It can simply be held, as you might hold a stone in a pocket. The passage says that silence of this kind is not empty, and that it is heard.

If there is something you wish you could say to somebody, and have not, tonight is not too late to think about it, even if you cannot yet say it. Sometimes the first step is only to see that it is there.

Two weeks is the middle of things, whatever the length of your road. The first rush of newness has gone, and the end is not yet near enough to hurry you. Something has probably shifted in these fourteen days: the pace of your feet, the people you know by sight, the things you notice and the things you have stopped noticing. There is no need to measure the change tonight. It is enough to see that you are not the person who set out. If you are among others tonight, notice how each of them carries their own silence. Some wear it lightly, and some like a coat. It is a good thing to respect when you pass someone sitting alone in the common room.

Three questions for tonight:

- What did I carry today that had no words?
- When was I most alone, and did it feel empty or full?
- Is there something I would like to say to someone, even if I cannot say it yet?

Honestly, tiredness. At night silence is not a discipline, only a mercy. If you are very tired, do not try to make the quiet mean something. Let the room be dark, let your breathing slow, and let the day end without comment. If it is true, as the passage says, that silence speaks, then you have already said all that was needed.

## A short prayer

*I have no words tonight, and I will not look for any. Whatever I carry, I set it down in the quiet. If anyone is listening, let them hear what I cannot say. If no one is, let the dark be gentle anyway. I am here. Let that be enough, and let tomorrow's road begin gently. Let the quiet last until morning, and keep me company in it.*
