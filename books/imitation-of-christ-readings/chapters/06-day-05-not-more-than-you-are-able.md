# Day 5. Not more than you are able

*Is enthusiasm a good guide to how far you can go?*

## The passage

> Some who were presumptuous because of the grace of devotion within them, have destroyed themselves, because they would do more than they were able, not considering the measure of their own littleness, but rather following the impulse of the heart than the judgment of the reason. … They who are as yet new and unskilled in the way of the Lord, unless they rule themselves after the counsel of the wise, may easily be deceived and led away.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 7 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

The fifth day is one of the dangerous ones, and it does not feel dangerous at all. The first morning was nerves and the second was sore, but by now something has loosened. Your legs have found a rhythm. The pack is a fact. The villages come and go like pages. You feel strong, perhaps stronger than you have felt in years, and the thought arrives, quite reasonably, that you could do a few more kilometres today. Why stop at the planned place? Why not the next one? Other walkers are going on. The light is good.

Walkers who have done this before tend to say the same thing about the early days: they are when the trouble starts. A tendon complains, a shin tightens, a blister gets going under a sock that has been wet for an hour. The advice that is passed from one walker to another is dull and almost always the same. Start shorter than you think you need to. Build slowly. Stop while there is still something left.

Thomas à Kempis says it in his own way, and it is blunter than most. He is writing about people who had a surge of inward fervour, a warmth in their faith, and who rode it too hard. They wanted to do more than they were able, he says, "not considering the measure of their own littleness", and followed the impulse of the heart instead of the judgement of reason. They destroyed themselves, he says. It is a strong word, and he means it as a warning, not a prediction. The same mistake can be made with a knee.

There is nothing wrong with the impulse. It is fuel. The trouble is only that it is a poor map. The heart tells you how much you want to do, and it has no way of telling you how much the body can bear this week. For that you need reason, and reason is the plain, unglamorous business of looking at the actual distance, the actual heat, the actual state of your feet.

The second sentence goes a step further. Those who are new and unskilled in the way, he says, should take the counsel of the wise, or they are easily led astray. On a long walk the wise are not hard to find. They are the people who have walked before, who sit on the same bench at four in the afternoon, rub their ankles and tell you, without lecturing, what they wish they had done. A pharmacist, a host in an albergue, a fellow traveller whose pace you trust. What the wise say is rarely deep. Drink more water than you think you need. Eat before you are hungry. Stop a little earlier than you wish. None of it is clever, and all of it works. To take advice from such a person is not weakness. It is a good use of the time they have already spent.

Where is this hard? First, in the phrase "the measure of their own littleness". It can sound like a put-down, and Thomas is not above putting people down. Read it as a measure and not a judgement. Every body has a pace, and so does every mind and every heart. Knowing yours is not smallness. It is the beginning of going far. Second, limits are hard to see from inside. How do you know that what stops you is the body, and not fear dressed up as prudence? There is no rule. But the body's signals are usually clear: a sharp pain, a pain that grows, a tightness that does not warm up. Fear tends to be vaguer and louder. If it is sharp, or if it gets worse, see a pharmacist or a doctor, and in a real emergency call 112. That is not heroism, and it is not defeat.

The word that fits this day is patience, and here it simply means pace. It means believing that a month of ordinary days will take you further than a week of heroic ones. Any day you finish with something in reserve is a day that has made tomorrow possible.

## For the walk

- If I were walking for a month and not for a day, would I walk like this?
- For the first hour, go a little slower than feels natural. Find a pace at which you could talk, or hum a tune, without gasping. Then notice, when the hour is up, how much easier the second hour feels. At each stop, give your feet ten seconds of honest attention: any new heat, any tightness that has not gone?

## At the end of the day

Take the boots off first, and then the socks, and sit for a moment with your bare feet on a cool floor. The next few minutes are a conversation with your body, and for once you are the one who should be listening.

Start at the feet and work upwards. Toes, heels, ankles, shins, knees, thighs, hips, back, shoulders. Not as a medical examination, only as an honest question at each stop: how are you? Some places will answer with a dull ache that means they have done their work. A few may say something sharper. If anything is sharp, or hot, or swollen, or worse than it was this morning, make a note to see a pharmacist or a doctor tomorrow before you go on. That is the wise counsel the passage speaks of, and it is available in most towns of any size.

Now think back over the pace of the day. Was there a stretch where the heart ran ahead of the body? Perhaps you went faster to keep up with someone, or to prove something, or because the morning was so fine. Perhaps you stayed behind when you might have walked on, and felt a small, odd shame. Neither is a failure. Both are only information.

Whom did you listen to today? Perhaps someone said something sensible and you nodded and ignored it. Perhaps no one said anything, and you wished they had. There is nearly always someone nearby who has walked this road before, and most of them like to be asked.

Look at tomorrow's stage with the same honesty. The sensible distance is the one you could manage on a bad day, not the one you managed on a good one. If there is a shorter place to stop, know where it is. And do not measure yourself against the strongest person in the room. They have their own feet to answer to. And if you decide on a short day, or a rest day, there is no need to announce it to yourself as a defeat. Some of the wisest decisions on a long walk are made at this hour, with the feet up and the map folded.

Three questions for tonight:

- Where did my enthusiasm run ahead of my body today?
- Whose advice did I hear, and what would it be wise to do about it?
- If tomorrow's walk had to be a little shorter than I planned, what would I gain?

An honest word about tiredness. Tired is not the same as hurt. Tiredness is the ordinary price of a good day, and it will mostly be gone by morning. But be honest with yourself about the difference. If you are not sure which of the two this is, treat it as the more careful one until you find out. A rest day is not a loss. Many people on long roads say that their best days came after they stopped trying to be strong.

## A short prayer

*Teach me the size of my own strength, and let me not be ashamed of it. Where I ran ahead today, forgive me, and let my body forgive me too. Give me patience to go a little slower, and the sense to listen to someone who knows. Let my feet rest tonight, and mend. Let the one who walks faster walk well, and the one who walks slower, and me.*
