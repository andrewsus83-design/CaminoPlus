# Day 17. Less rather than more

*What could you give away today, and what would giving free?*

## The passage

> “Strive, My Son, to do another’s will rather than thine own. Choose always to have less rather than more. Seek always after the lowest place, and to be subject to all. Wish always and pray that the will of God be fulfilled in thee. Behold, such a man as this entereth into the inheritance of peace and quietness.” … O my Lord, this Thy short discourse hath in itself much of perfectness. It is short in words but full of meaning, and abundant in fruit.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 23 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

Many hostels have a shelf by the door, or a box in a corner, where walkers leave what they no longer want to carry. It is a small museum of changed minds. A second pair of trousers. A paperback with a bookmark halfway through. A bottle of sun cream, a jar of olives, a pair of poles with the rubber worn smooth. Each item on it was once thought essential, and each was set down somewhere between the first week and the third. Someone else will pick it up. Nobody arranged it. It simply happens, in many hostels, most days.

That shelf is a very good commentary on the passage. The voice in this chapter, which is the one Thomas à Kempis gives to God in his third book, promises to teach the way of peace and of true liberty, and it does so in four short sentences. Do another's will rather than your own. Choose to have less rather than more. Seek the lowest place and be subject to all. Wish and pray that the will of God be done in you. The man who does this, it says, enters into an inheritance of peace and quietness.

Then the disciple replies, and his reply is lovely. It is a short discourse, he says, much in few words, and abundant in fruit. If he could keep it fully, he adds in the next line, disturbance would not arise within him so easily. It is the plain testimony of someone who has tried it and knows how far he falls short.

Lay the four sentences against a long walk. The first is about yielding: letting someone go first through the door, give the better seat, choose the other's restaurant. The second is the pack, which every walker eventually learns: you can get along on less than you set out with, and you feel lighter for it. The third is the oddest, to take the lowest place. It means, perhaps, to be at the end of the queue, to take the top bunk, to be last to be served. And the fourth is the largest: to hold your own plan loosely under something bigger.

The thread through all four is offering. You give something up, a preference, a thing, a place, and in the giving something unexpectedly lightens. A person who offers the lower bunk to someone with bad knees often sleeps well in the upper. A person who gives away half a loaf rarely goes hungry. It is one of the paradoxes of the road that the hands which open are the ones that are filled. A hand cannot receive while it is clenched. Thomas would not have put it so, but the shelf by the door puts it for him.

Where is it hard? Seriously hard, and it needs honesty. "Do another's will rather than thine own", *always*, is a dangerous sentence in some hands. There are people who give way all the time, who have never had the luxury of wanting anything for themselves, and to whom this advice would be one more weight. If you are someone who is already the first to yield, this passage is not aimed at you. For you the beginning of the lesson may be to ask what you want, and to say so. Thomas is writing to people who grasp, and a cloister full of the grasping is a very different place from a kitchen full of the over-giving.

In the same way, "the lowest place" is a hard phrase for anyone who has never been offered a high one. It is not an instruction to be walked over, and it does not ask you to put up with harm. It is a way of saying that you do not need to win the seat to be at peace.

There is a plain doubt, too. Is "less rather than more" always wise? A tired person who gives up the last of the water is not being holy. The passage asks for a certain freedom from grasping, not for foolishness. Giving that is real begins with enough to give.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? One small yielding. One small lightening. Let somebody go first. Offer the better seat. Look in your pack for a single thing you could do without, and put it on the shelf for the next person.

## For the walk

- What could I give away today, and what would I be freed by giving? The answer may be quite a small thing.
- Make one small yielding before the next stop: let someone go first, offer a seat, take the less comfortable place. And look in your pack, or in your head, for one thing you do not need, and decide to leave it behind. If it is a thing, put it where someone else can find it.

## At the end of the day

Look at the little pile of the day's possessions: boots by the bed, pack at the foot, a few things on a hook. It is not very much. Many walkers say, after a few weeks, that it is more than enough, and that they had never guessed how little a body needs to be warm, dry and fed.

Think back through the day for the moments of giving and taking. Where did you give way? Perhaps over a small thing: a place in a line, a seat at the table, the choice of where to eat, the decision about the next day's start. Was it freely given, or did it come out sideways, as resentment? Giving that is done with a sour face is not what the passage means. If it was freely done, let yourself enjoy it for a moment. It is a quiet pleasure, and it is allowed.

And where did you hold on? There is usually something. A view you insisted on, a bed you would not swap, a plan you kept to against the evidence. Ask what you were protecting. Often it is something small and tired, a fear of being overlooked, or of not having enough. It can be looked at kindly.

If there was a moment today when you found yourself giving too much, doing the whole of the kitchen, making all the choices, saying yes when you meant no, notice that too. The passage is not asking for that, and you are allowed to rest.

The pack is a good mirror. Which things did you reach for today, and which sat untouched at the bottom? You do not have to decide anything tonight, but it is worth noticing. And there is a heavier pack that you carry in your head: a grievance, a rehearsed argument, a comparison. These weigh nothing on a scale and a great deal on a hill. Naming one you could leave behind tomorrow is enough.

Three questions for tonight:

- Where did I give way today, and was it freely given?
- Where did I hold on to my own will, and what was I protecting?
- What could I leave behind tomorrow, in my pack or in my head?

An honest word about tiredness. Giving takes energy, and tonight you may have very little. You are not obliged to be generous at ten o'clock. A tired person's first and best gift is to be good company in the most ordinary way: a courteous word, a quiet room. Tomorrow will bring other chances. Tonight, it is enough not to take what is not yours. If you want a small gift to give before bed, give the person next to you a quiet room.

## A short prayer

*Teach me to hold things lightly: the seat, the plan, the pack, the point I wanted to win. Where I gave today, let it have been gladly. Where I grasped, forgive me. Let me not give so much that I vanish, nor keep so much that I cannot move. Let there be room in my hands for what comes tomorrow.*
