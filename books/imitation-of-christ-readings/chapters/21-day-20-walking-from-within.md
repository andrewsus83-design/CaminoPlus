# Day 20. Walking from within

*Can the road come home with you?*

## The passage

> He who knoweth things as they are and not as they are said or seem to be, he truly is wise, and is taught of God more than of men. … He who knoweth how to walk from within, and to set little value upon outward things, requireth not places nor waiteth for seasons, for holding his intercourse with God. The inward man quickly recollecteth himself, because he is never entirely given up to outward things. No outward labour and no necessary occupations stand in his way, but as events fall out, so doth he fit himself to them.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 2, chapter 1 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

The first thing that happens at home is the smell. Your own front hall, with its particular blend of damp coat, old wood and whatever was in the bin when you left. The second is the quiet. There is nobody snoring, no zip, no stranger's kettle. Then the post, and the fridge, and the small pile of tasks that has been waiting politely for three weeks. By evening the house has absorbed you. By the following week it is as though you had never left, and the road, which seemed so vivid, has begun to go soft at the edges like a dream in the morning.

This is the hardest day of the walk, though it is not really a day on the road at all. Many people who have walked far say that the coming home is harder than the leaving. What was clear on the road becomes unclear at the kitchen table. The self that walked, that stopped for a bench and noticed a lizard, gets crowded out by the self that answers emails.

Thomas à Kempis had a thought for this, and it is a bold one. A person who knows how to walk from within, he says, does not need particular places, or particular seasons, in order to be at home with God. Such a person quickly collects himself again, because he is never entirely given over to outward things. No outward labour and no necessary business stand in his way. As events fall out, he fits himself to them.

The phrase *walk from within* is the heart of it. It describes an inner way of moving, a steadiness that travels. The Camino, on this view, is not the only road. It is an unusually clear one, and it teaches you a way of walking that is not confined to it.

The first sentence of the passage gives you something to hold on to when you tell the story. The wise person, it says, knows things as they are, and not as they are said, or as they seem. It is a very good thing to remember when people ask how it was. There will be a story, and it will gather shape as you tell it. It will become a little tidier and a little grander each time. That is natural, and it is not a sin. But it is worth keeping, somewhere in yourself, the plain version: the heat, the boredom, the blisters, the two or three moments that actually changed you. Things as they were.

Now say where it is hard. The claim that a wise person needs no places and no seasons is a very high one. Most of us do need them. A particular bench, a time of day, a quiet corner: these are not weaknesses, but supports. The road helped partly because it was a place, with a rhythm built into it. At home the rhythm has to be made, and it is easy to lose in a fortnight.

"No outward labour stands in his way" is also easier said than done. Anyone with small children, a heavy job, a sick relative, knows that labour does stand in the way, and can crowd out the quiet altogether. Nor does "to set little value upon outward things" mean that you should be cool towards your family or your work. You can value people and tasks and still be steady inside them. The two are not opposed.

And there is a doubt it would be dishonest to leave out. Will it last? Not in the form it had. You will not stay as calm as you were on the third week of the walk. Some of it will go. But some of it, if you are lucky, and if you look after it, will not.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Choose one small anchor, one place and one time, and keep it for a week. Ten minutes on the doorstep with a cup of tea. A short walk with no phone. A moment, before bed, to look back at the day. It does not need to be much. It only needs to be regular, and to be yours.

## For the walk

- What did I do on the road that I could also do on an ordinary weekday?
- Choose one small anchor for the first week at home: a place and a time, perhaps ten minutes on the step with a cup of tea, or a short walk from your door without your phone. Do it once today. If you are still on the road, rehearse it, and keep it ready for the way home.

## At the end of the day

Your own bed, or something like it. After weeks of strangers' beds, it should feel luxurious, and it may feel merely strange. It has a different smell and a different noise, and the mattress is perhaps softer than your back remembers. Many people sleep badly the first night at home, and wonder why.

Look back on the day, which was probably crowded with small tasks, small conversations and the faint disorientation of being in a familiar place as someone slightly different. What looked different today? Perhaps it was the amount of stuff in the house. Perhaps it was the noise, or the speed of everything, or the sheer quantity of choices. Perhaps people looked different, or you looked different to them. Some of this will fade within days. It is worth noticing while it is still clear.

Think too about what you said. When people asked you how it was, what did you tell them? Was it the plain version or the tidy one? There is nothing wrong with the tidy one, in company. But ask yourself, quietly, which of the two you would like to keep for yourself. The passage speaks of knowing things as they are. It is a fine thing to keep at least one account that has not been improved.

And what did you do today that came from the road? A moment of looking out of a window for longer than you needed. A slower walk to the shop. A thank-you that was a little warmer than usual. Even one such thing is a sign that it has come home with you.

Unpacking is the last practical act of the walk: lay out, wash, put away. The pack goes into a cupboard. Some people keep one thing in sight, a shell, a stamped credencial, a stone from a pocket. Not as a trophy, only as a reminder. Choose what you would like to see tomorrow morning.

If somebody at home asked little about the walk, or asked and did not quite listen, that stings, and it is common. You are allowed to keep it for yourself for a while.

Three questions for tonight:

- What looked different in my own home today?
- What did I say about the walk, and was it the plain version?
- Which one small practice will I try to keep this week?

An honest word about tiredness. Coming home is tiring in a way that nobody warns you about. The body is relieved and the mind is overstimulated, and the result can be an odd, heavy fatigue. Do not judge your whole walk by how you feel this evening. Eat something simple, drink some water, and let the first night be a poor night if it has to be. The practice will still be there tomorrow.

## A short prayer

*I am home, and I am not the same, and the house does not quite know it. Let the road come in with me. Keep alive in me whatever was true out there, and let me find a little room for it here. Where I forget, let me begin again, and let the small things I carry home be enough.*
