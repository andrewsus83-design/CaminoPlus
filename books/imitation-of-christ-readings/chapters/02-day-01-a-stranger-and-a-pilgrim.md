# Day 1. A stranger and a pilgrim

*What do you carry when you are only passing through?*

## The passage

> If thou wilt stand fast and wilt profit as thou oughtest, hold thyself as an exile and a pilgrim upon the earth. … Keep thyself as a stranger and a pilgrim upon the earth, to whom the things of the world appertain not. Keep thine heart free, and lifted up towards God, for here have we no continuing city.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 1, chapters 17 and 23 (tr. William Benham)

## Reflection

There is a moment, somewhere between your own front door and the first yellow arrow, when you stop being someone who lives somewhere and become someone who is going. The pack is on your back. A shell, if you have one, swings from a strap. In a pocket is the *credencial*, the folded pilgrim passport that will collect a stamp in town after town. Nothing has happened yet. You are simply no longer where you were.

Thomas à Kempis had words for that state, and he did not keep them for people with rucksacks. He thought they described every reader. A stranger and a pilgrim, an exile and a pilgrim: someone away from home who has not yet arrived anywhere that stays.

The lines look severe at first. Keep yourself like a visitor, he says, to whom the things of the world do not belong. Keep the heart free and lifted up. For here, in his old phrase, we have no continuing city. No place lasts. Every town you walk through will carry on without you tomorrow, and so will the one you left behind.

Read on a road, it is gentler than it sounds. A walker already lives this way. You own what fits in the pack, and you chose it with care because you must carry it. You sleep in beds that are not yours and wake in rooms you will not see again. You are a guest in every village, and the best guests tidy up after themselves and say thank you. There is a lightness in it that anyone who has walked for a few days will know: the surprise of how little is needed, and how much of what weighed on you at home was never in the bag.

Leaving is its own small labour. On the first morning nearly everyone pats their pockets three times: passport, phone, money, water. The checking is natural, and it is also a tiny rehearsal of the clenched fist, the wish to make the day safe by holding on to everything that might go wrong. Somewhere around the second village the pockets stop being checked. Nothing fell out. The day went on being what it was.

Now the hard part. Thomas wrote for people in a cloister, and the line about the things of the world not belonging to you can be heard as coldness, as if the world and the people in it were to be held at arm's length. Most of us have a family, a job, a body that aches. We cannot treat them as scenery, and we should not try. The reading that serves a walker is narrower and kinder. It does not say love nothing. It says hold nothing in a clenched fist. You can love a house, a person, a plan, and still know that the hand holding it should be open. A heart that is free is free to move on because it was never nailed to the last place.

There is an honest doubt to set beside this. It is easy to feel like a stranger on the first morning, when home is a day behind you and one phone call away. The real test of "no continuing city" does not come on the first day. It comes on the afternoon when a place feels so good you would stay for ever, and on the evening when a place feels so bad you would give anything to leave. In both, the same sentence holds: this is not where you live. The good place will end, and so will the bad one. That is a comfort and a small grief together, and it is allowed to be both.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? Something modest. To arrive in each place as a guest does, noticing and thanking and claiming nothing. To leave the room a little tidier than you found it. To pass through the afternoon's irritation without moving in. And to remember that "pilgrim" is not a title you earn by distance. In Thomas's hands it is a way of holding yourself, and you can take it up this morning, before you have walked a single kilometre.

## For the walk

- What am I holding too tightly this morning: a plan, a worry, a picture of how this walk should feel? Name it, then picture the hand that holds it opening a little.
- For the first ten minutes, look at each place you pass as a guest would. Say a silent thank you to the first house, field or person you notice, and claim nothing from any of them. If it helps, choose one word for what you have left behind and let it stay behind.

## At the end of the day

You have arrived somewhere, or you are about to. The bed is unfamiliar, the pillow is the wrong shape, and somewhere along the room a stranger is zipping and unzipping a bag. Your feet are warm and complaining. For a minute, do nothing at all. The first evening of a long walk is always odd. Your body is tired in places it did not know it had, and your mind is still half at home.

Look back over the day without hurry. Some of it was hard. Perhaps there was a moment in the morning when the whole idea seemed faintly ridiculous. Perhaps there was a long middle stretch where the pack argued with your shoulders, or an hour spent working out what you had forgotten to bring. It may have been lonely, or too crowded. Say what was hard, in one sentence, to yourself. It does not need a solution tonight.

Some of it was good, even if you are too tired to feel it yet. A greeting from someone on the road. The first time you looked up and found you had walked a kilometre without thinking. Water that tasted of something. The smell of a bakery. Say that too.

Today you were a stranger and a pilgrim, both at once. In a new town nobody knows you, and nobody expects you to be anyone in particular. That can be frightening and it can be a relief. Tonight it may be both. If you are walking with someone you already know, notice how they look in this strange place. A friend often shows a new side on the road. If you are alone, notice whose face you were glad to see today, however briefly.

If today was easy, be careful of the opposite mistake. A day does not have to turn into a lesson before it can be allowed to be good. And if there are messages waiting from home, answer them in a sentence if you want to. You are allowed to be a stranger for a little while.

Three small questions for the dark:

- Where did I feel like a guest today, and where did I feel like an owner of something: a place in a queue, a bunk, a view, an opinion?
- What did I carry today that I did not need?
- What was given to me today that I did not earn?

An honest word about tiredness. You may not be able to answer any of this. The first day costs more than the distance suggests, because the body spends effort on newness alone. If the questions slide off, let them. They will keep until morning, and so will you. Putting the phone down and closing your eyes counts as the last practice of the day.

Down the room the lights go out, one by one. For a few breaths everybody in it is, just for tonight, exactly what Thomas said: a stranger, a pilgrim, a guest of the dark.

## A short prayer

*Tonight I am a guest in this bed, in this town, in this day. Thank you for the roof and for whoever lit the lamp. Let me hold lightly what I cannot keep, and receive without grabbing what is given. Forgive the day its clumsiness, and mine. Let my body rest and my heart stay open, ready to go on in the morning. Bless the people I left behind, and the ones I have not yet met.*
