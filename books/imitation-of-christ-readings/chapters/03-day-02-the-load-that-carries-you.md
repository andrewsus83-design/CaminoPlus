# Day 2. The load that carries you

*Is it the weight that hurts, or the quarrel with it?*

## The passage

> If thou willingly bear the Cross, it will bear thee, and will bring thee to the end which thou seekest, even where there shall be the end of suffering; though it shall not be here. If thou bear it unwillingly, thou makest a burden for thyself and greatly increaseth thy load, and yet thou must bear it. If thou cast away one cross, without doubt thou shalt find another and perchance a heavier.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 2, chapter 12 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

On the second morning the pack is no heavier than it was on the first. Nobody has added a stone. But yesterday's straps have left their marks, and you now know exactly where the weight sits. This is the day when walkers start to have opinions about it. They heft the pack and judge it. They look at the third paperback, the spare pair of trousers, the little tin that seemed essential in the shop, and they begin to bargain.

Thomas à Kempis was not talking about rucksacks. His word is the Cross, and for him it meant the trouble that comes to anyone who tries to live honestly: illness, loss, disappointment, the people who are hard to love, the days that go against you. His claim is plain. You will carry something. That part is not up to you. What is up to you is how.

Read the middle sentence slowly. If you bear it unwillingly, you make a burden for yourself and greatly increase your load. Most of us know this from the inside. There is the weight itself, and then there is the argument with the weight: why me, why today, why is it not fair. The argument has no mass you could put on a scale, and it still wears you out faster than the thing it is arguing about. Anyone who has walked uphill muttering knows that the muttering does not shorten the hill.

The first sentence has a lovely promise in it: carried willingly, the load will carry you. Walkers say much the same about a well-fitted pack. Done up properly, with the weight low on the hips, the strongest part of you takes it, and the shoulders are left free. The load becomes part of how you move. And a load can bring you to where you are going. The pack holds the bed, the rain jacket and the water. Without it there is no walk at all. The same is true of a day. A day carried willingly, even a hard one, takes you somewhere. A day carried grudgingly only takes you to the evening.

Now say where it is hard, because it is. "Willingly" is the word that snags. Some weights are unjust. Some are too heavy for one person. A person with real grief, or real pain, is not helped by being told to carry it cheerfully, and Thomas is not asking for cheerfulness. Elsewhere in his book he says things about suffering that this reading does not follow. Taken narrowly, the sentence is practical and not pious: what cannot be changed is made heavier by fighting it.

The last sentence deserves a careful look too. If you throw away one cross, you will find another, perhaps heavier. That is not always true. Some loads should be put down, and the wisdom is in telling which. A walker who sends a parcel home at the next post office has done something sensible. But there is a truth in it as well. Many people set out on a long walk hoping to leave something behind, and find it has travelled in the pack. A load thrown away in anger is often picked up again in another shape.

So what does it ask of an ordinary day? Perhaps three small things. First, a morning inventory: which of these weights are really mine, and which am I carrying out of habit, or because I never asked for help? Put down what can be put down. Second, for the weight that cannot be set down today, stop arguing with it, or at least argue more quietly. Say, "This is today's weight." Third, ask someone to take a share. Pilgrims do it all the time with bags, with blisters and with worries. It is a very ordinary habit of the road.

There is an honest doubt left over, and it is better not to hide it. Can anyone decide to be willing? Probably not, on command. But you can begin with the smallest honest step: admit that you are unwilling, loosen a strap, take a breath, and walk on. Willingness may come later, or it may not. A burden carried without a quarrel is already half as heavy.

## For the walk

- What am I carrying today that I could put down, and what must I carry whether I like it or not? Name each in a few plain words.
- Stop once in the first hour and adjust a single strap, or let your shoulders drop and roll back. As you do it, say quietly, "This is today's weight," and walk on without arguing with it. Notice, later, the first moment when you forget to argue.

## At the end of the day

The pack is off at last. That first moment of setting it down is one of the small private pleasures of the road: your shoulders float, your back straightens, and for a few minutes you feel strangely light, almost as if you might lift off the floor. Sit with that. It is the nearest thing to a picture of what Thomas is describing.

Look back over the day. What was heavy? There was the pack, certainly, and perhaps a stiff knee, or a hill that went on after it should have stopped. But heaviness comes in other forms, too. A phone call. A remark that is still turning over in your mind. A memory that walked beside you for an hour and would not go away. Name the heaviest thing. Was it the weight itself, or the argument you were having with it?

And what was light? Even a hard day has a few such moments: a downhill stretch, a bench in the shade, someone who offered to carry something, or simply a moment when the load and the walking were one thing and you forgot you were carrying anything at all.

Unpacking is a small ritual of its own. Lay out what you used today and what you never touched. Many walkers find that something has travelled all day without once being needed, and that what they reached for most was small: a warm layer, a pair of dry socks, a number written on paper. The pack tells you honestly what you actually carry.

Heavy days teach the body something too: how much it can bear, and how quickly it recovers once it is allowed to stop. What you could not manage this afternoon may be quite manageable after a night's sleep.

If you walked beside someone today, think about how the load was shared between you: who set the pace, who waited, who took the heavier share of the silence. Companions carry things for each other that never go on any scale.

Tonight, three gentle questions:

- What did I argue with today that I might have simply carried?
- Is there something in my pack, or in my head, that I could post home or leave behind tomorrow?
- Who offered me help today, and who could I ask tomorrow?

An honest word about tiredness. Weariness makes everything heavier, including thoughts. The worry that looked enormous at four o'clock often looks smaller after food and a wash, and smaller still after sleep. Do not try to settle the big questions of your life in a bunk bed. Put the pack where you can reach it in the morning, and let it be a pack again, and nothing more.

If any weight is too much to carry alone, say it to somebody tomorrow, in a plain sentence. That is not failure. It is how most loads on this road are carried.

## A short prayer

*Let me put down what was never mine to carry. Let me carry the rest without a quarrel. Where the load is too much, send a hand, and let me be glad to take it. Tonight the pack is by the bed and my shoulders are light. Let me not carry tomorrow's weight tonight. Let that be enough until morning, and let sleep come easily.*
