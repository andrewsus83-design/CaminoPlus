# How to use this book

This is a small book to be read slowly. It has thirty readings and each takes about five minutes. Read one a day, or one at the end of each stage, or open it anywhere and read what falls open. Nobody is counting.

Every reading has the same four parts.

- **The passage.** A few lines from *The Imitation of Christ*, a book of the inner life written in Latin about six hundred years ago. The words are quoted exactly as the translator, William Benham, gave them in 1886, *thee* and *thou* and all. Read the passage twice. The second time, read it aloud if you can, even under your breath. Old English slows the eye down, and that is half of its use.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Thomas's. It starts from something on the road, a bench, a bell, a blister, and lets the passage open it. It tries to say plainly what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. You will not need a notebook. Most of them are better done on your feet.
- **A short prayer.** Original and short. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular, and see how it sounds in your own mouth.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

*The Imitation of Christ* is a Christian book. It speaks to its reader as a believer, and in several places it speaks as Christ himself. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Thomas believed to recognise what he noticed about tiredness, pride, silence and the wish to begin again. Where he is hard to follow, we say so and do not pretend otherwise.

## The shape of the book

The readings do not follow the order of the original. They follow a walk, from setting out to coming home, in ten movements:

- Readings 1 to 4, *setting out*: a good beginning, a stranger's lightness, the limits of any guide, and not going faster than you can.
- Readings 5 to 7, *the weight we carry*: what we carry, what we could put down, and what makes a load lighter.
- Readings 8 to 10, *silence*: listening, the wandering mind, and a prayer with no words.
- Readings 11 to 13, *weariness*: small things that wear us down and what to do on a flat day.
- Readings 14 to 16, *companions*: the people we share a room and a road with.
- Readings 17 to 20, *doubt*: fear of the end, changing moods, tomorrow's worries and the long wait.
- Readings 21 and 22, *how we measure*: what we count, and what other people say.
- Readings 23 and 24, *gratitude*: small gifts and the road as a book.
- Readings 25 to 27, *arrival*: the shrine, the day after, and being at home.
- Readings 28 to 30, *return*: carrying the Way into an ordinary week.

A movement is only a few days' worth, but the book does not need thirty days. Skip, repeat, read backwards. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. The practices are deliberately small: a breath, a step count, a greeting. They are meant to be done without turning the walk into a task.

At home the book works the same way. Read one in the evening and carry the question through the next day, on the bus or at a desk. Pilgrims often say that the harder walk begins when they get back. The last movement of the book is for that.

A few words you will meet: *albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a communal kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly "have a good Way". Both are used here without fuss.

Read slowly. The classic is old, and it was written to be read slowly, one line at a time, by people with a long day ahead of them.
