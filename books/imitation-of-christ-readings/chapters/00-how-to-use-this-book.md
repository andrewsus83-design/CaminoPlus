# How to use this book

This is a small book to be read slowly, over three weeks. It is made to go with the Camino Francés. It has twenty-one days, one to read each day, whether you are walking or at home. On a road of about a month, you can repeat a favourite day, or read on rest days, and nobody will mind.

Every day has two parts. A morning reading of about five minutes, and an evening reflection of about three.

**In the morning** there are three things.

- **The passage.** A few lines from *The Imitation of Christ*, a book of the inner life written in Latin about six hundred years ago. The words are quoted exactly as the translator, William Benham, gave them in 1886, *thee* and *thou* and all. Read the passage twice. The second time, read it aloud if you can, even under your breath. Old English slows the eye down, and that is half of its use.
- **The reflection.** This part is ours, not Thomas's. It starts from something on the road, a bench, a bell, a blister, and lets the passage open it. It tries to say plainly what the passage says, where it is hard, and what it might ask of an ordinary day. It is allowed to doubt.
- **For the walk.** A question to carry for the next hour and one small practice that needs nothing but attention. You will not need a notebook. Most of them are better done on your feet.

**In the evening** there are two more.

- **At the end of the day.** A look back at the day you have just lived, with the morning's passage in mind. It names what was hard and what was good, asks two or three gentle questions, and is honest about tiredness. You do not need to write anything down. The questions are for lying down with.
- **A short prayer.** Original and short. If you pray, pray it. If you pray differently, change the words. If you do not pray, say it as a wish, to nobody in particular, and see how it sounds in your own mouth.

## A Christian book, and who it is for

*The Imitation of Christ* is a Christian book. It speaks to its reader as a believer, and in several places it speaks as Christ himself. We say so plainly so that nobody is surprised. People walk the Camino for many reasons: faith, sport, grief, curiosity, a rest from work. All of them are welcome here. You do not need to believe what Thomas believed to recognise what he noticed about tiredness, pride, silence and the wish to begin again. Where he is hard to follow, we say so and do not pretend otherwise.

## The shape of the three weeks

The readings do not follow the order of the original book. They follow a walk, in three weeks.

- **Week one, setting out (Days 1 to 7).** Leaving, the weight we carry, silence, beginning again, patience, weariness and a first rest.
- **Week two, the middle of the road (Days 8 to 14).** Companions, doubt, how we measure ourselves, consolation, small temptations, gratitude and the inner room.
- **Week three, arrival and return (Days 15 to 21).** Peace, trust, offering, the end of the road, the day after, coming home and going on.

The three weeks are a shape, not a timetable. Day 18 is about arriving and Day 20 about coming home, but the book cannot know on which day you reach the end of your own walk. Read the day that fits the day you are having. If you are tired, read the passage and the prayer and leave the rest for the morning.

## On the road and at home

Read the morning part on a bench, in a bunk, in a café while the soup arrives. Then put the phone away and take the question with you for an hour. The practices are deliberately small: a breath, a step count, a greeting. They are meant to be done without turning the walk into a task. Read the evening part last of all, when the boots are off.

At home the book works the same way. Read the morning part with your tea and carry the question through the day, on the bus or at a desk. Pilgrims often say that the harder walk begins when they get back. The last days of the book are for that.

A few words you will meet. *Albergue* is the simple hostel where pilgrims sleep, often in bunks, often with a communal kitchen. *Buen Camino* is what walkers say to one another on the road, roughly "have a good Way". Both are used here without fuss, and other words are explained where they first appear.

Read slowly. The classic is old, and it was written to be read slowly, one line at a time, by people with a long day ahead of them.
