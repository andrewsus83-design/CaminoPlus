# Day 13. The least benefit

*What is the smallest gift you have been given today?*

## The passage

> Be thankful, therefore, for the least benefit and thou shalt be worthy to receive greater. Let the least be unto thee even as the greatest, and let that which is of little account be unto thee as a special gift. If the majesty of the Giver be considered, nothing that is given shall seem small and of no worth, for that is not a small thing which is given by the Most High God.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 2, chapter 10 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

On a long road you learn very quickly what is large and what is small, and then you learn that you had it the wrong way round. A glass of cold water, after four hours without, is not a small thing. Neither is a bench in the shade at the exact moment you need to sit. Or a hot shower after a cold day, or a tap in a village square. A sandwich pressed on you by a stranger. A shaded stretch of path when the sun has become personal. At home these things are scenery. Out here they change the weather inside you.

Thomas à Kempis noticed the same thing six centuries ago, and drew a sensible conclusion. Be thankful for the least benefit, he says, and let the least be to you as the greatest. Let what seems of little account be received as a special gift. This is not asking you to pretend. It is asking you to do the opposite of pretending: to look straight at a small thing and see how big it is.

There is a second thought folded into the passage. If the majesty of the Giver is considered, he says, nothing that is given can seem small. In his view all gifts come, in the end, from God, and so even a crust of bread carries weight. A reader who does not share that belief can still take the shape of it. Look at where the small gift came from. Somebody put out the jug. Somebody planted the fig tree by the wall. Somebody painted that yellow arrow, years ago or last month. Somebody built the bench. The smallness of the gift is only on the surface. Behind it there is often a person, or a long chain of them, who thought about you without knowing your name.

Walkers sometimes say that the small gifts seem to multiply the longer the road goes on. They do not multiply. The eyes have simply learned to count them.

There is a moment in the passage that sounds a little transactional: be thankful for little, and you will be worthy to receive more. It can read like a trade, with gratitude as the price. A kinder way to take it is as a description of capacity. People who cannot notice small things cannot take in large ones. A mind that is always reaching for the next thing has no room to receive what is already in its hand. Gratitude is partly a skill of attention, and it grows with use.

There is a shadow on this subject, and honesty requires that it be named. "Be thankful" can be a cruel thing to say to someone who is in pain or loss. A few lines further on in the same chapter Thomas goes on to ask for thanks even for hardship, and this reading does not follow him there. Nobody should be told to be grateful for suffering. The passage we have here is about something much smaller and safer: noticing the good things that are actually good.

There is also the ordinary difficulty of attention. Most of the time, gratitude is simply not on the programme. The mind is on the next stage and the next meal. A person can go all day and be handed ten small gifts without noticing one. That is not a moral failing. It is only what happens to a mind that is busy.

And a plain doubt: some days you do not feel grateful, and no amount of looking will bring it on. If that is today, let it be. The practice is about noticing, not about producing a feeling. A feeling that has been forced is worth nothing. A gift noticed without much feeling is still a gift noticed.

The practice is to catch the small gifts in the act. Before each stop, name three that you have been given in the last hour. The temperature of the air. A kind word. The ground, still there under your feet. And once, to say thank you to a person for something small, and to mean it with your whole face.

## For the walk

- What is the smallest thing I have been given today, and who or what stands behind it? Try to find something you would usually pass without noticing.
- At each stop, name three small gifts of the last hour, silently or aloud. Before the end of the day, thank one person for one small thing, and let them see that you mean it. A plain "thank you" said slowly is enough.

## At the end of the day

It would be tempting at this hour to go through the day like an accounts clerk, making a list of things to be grateful for, and ticking them off with a sigh. Do not do that. Gratitude produced by obligation is stale. Instead, go slowly back over the day, as you might leaf through a photograph album, and see which small things come up on their own.

Perhaps it was the cold water. Perhaps a stranger's smile, or a stretch of path with a view you did not expect. Perhaps the plain fact of a mattress. Perhaps your own legs, which went on doing what you asked without being thanked, hour after hour. Aim for the very smallest things you can find. The point of the passage is the least, and the least is where most of the day's gifts actually live.

Then think about who stood behind them. Often it was someone you did not meet: whoever washed the sheets, cooked the soup, mended the path, kept the tap running. It is a strange and rather lovely thing to realise how many people have helped you today who will never know it. If there is a way, tomorrow, to pass something on to someone else, a small courtesy, a clean kitchen, a held gate, that is a kind of thank-you to them.

If there was a gift today that you did not notice at the time and see only now, say a word of thanks for that, too. It is not too late. Gifts can be received after the fact.

Here is a small exercise. Go through the day from morning to night and pick out the first gift and the last. The first may have been the coffee, or the quiet of the street. The last may be this very bed, or the sound of the room settling around you. Between them the day is full of others.

If you shared something today, food, water, a seat, a bit of tape, remember that giving is one way of saying thank you, and receiving gracefully is another. And if there is someone at home you have been thinking of while you walked, tonight is a good time to compose, in your head, the message you would send them.

Three questions for tonight:

- What was the smallest gift of today?
- Whom did I forget to thank?
- What did I treat as small that was really large?

A word about tiredness. Gratitude is not a duty at ten o'clock at night. If you are too tired to feel anything, you do not have to produce anything. A single thing named is enough, however flat it feels. "The bed" is a perfectly good answer. And if the day was hard, and nothing seems to deserve thanks, you are allowed to leave the question open until tomorrow, when the light will come back and the small things with it.

## A short prayer

*Thank you for the water and the shade, for the bench, the bread, and the stranger's smile. Thank you for the people I will never meet who made this day easier. Let me notice more tomorrow, and be quicker to say so. Where I have nothing to say tonight, let silence do for thanks. Let me sleep, and be glad.*
