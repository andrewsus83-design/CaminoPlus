# Day 12. A little puff of wind

*Why does the small thing undo us?*

## The passage

> I will confess to Thee, Lord, my infirmity. It is often a small thing which casteth me down and maketh me sad. I resolve that I will act bravely, but when a little temptation cometh, immediately I am in a great strait. Wonderfully small sometimes is the matter whence a grievous temptation cometh, and whilst I imagine myself safe for a little space; when I am not considering, I find myself often almost overcome by a little puff of wind.
>
> — Thomas à Kempis, *The Imitation of Christ*, Book 3, chapter 20 (tr. William Benham, 1886)

## Reflection

It is almost never the mountain. People cross high passes, endure a week of rain, walk on through a bad knee, and then lose their temper over a pebble. The pebble is in the boot, a very small hard thing, and it has been there for twenty minutes because stopping to deal with it would admit that the day was not going to plan. By the time you sit down on the verge, the pebble is the whole of the problem and the fault of everyone you have ever met.

Thomas à Kempis, writing as the disciple in his own third book, makes one of the most honest confessions in the whole Imitation. It is often, he says, a small thing that casts him down and makes him sad. He resolves to act bravely, and when a little temptation comes, immediately he is in a great strait. He imagines himself safe for a short while, and then, without noticing, he is almost overcome by a puff of wind.

A puff of wind. It is a lovely, humbling image. A strong man knocked sideways by a breeze. Anybody who has promised themselves a calm, patient, generous walk and then snapped at a stranger in a queue for the shower will know it. The resolutions were real, and so was the weakness. The wind needed only to be a little cold.

Notice two things in the passage. The first is that the matter is "wonderfully small". The trouble is usually not a great temptation but a trivial one: a remark, a delay, a smell, a sudden wish to stop. The second is the timing. He says that he was imagining himself safe, and was not considering, when the wind came. The dangerous moment is not in the middle of the struggle. It is just after a good stretch, when the guard is down. The arrival at the albergue after a fine day is often the very moment when something sharp is said.

"Temptation" is a heavy word, and in modern ears it can sound like a list of vices. A plainer way to read it is: anything that pulls you off your own intention. A taxi at the edge of the village. A shortcut along the road. A comment that begs for a clever reply. A decision to quit, made at four in the afternoon. Many of these are not wicked in themselves. Sometimes the taxi is the sensible thing, and to refuse it would be a kind of vanity. What Thomas is pointing at is the moment of being tugged, and the small gap in which you can choose.

Where is it hard? Perhaps in the tone. A confession of "my infirmity" can slide, in the hands of a person who is hard on himself, into self-contempt. Thomas is sometimes that person, and this book does not follow him there. The useful part of the passage is the honesty, not the shame. To say plainly "this is how I am knocked off course" is not a failure. It is the beginning of knowing where to put your guard.

There is also a plain honesty about the odds. The man in the passage resolves, falls, resolves again. He does not say that he has stopped falling. He says that this is what he is like, and he says it to the one he trusts most. That is a kind of wisdom: not to expect to become a person who is never moved by a breeze, but to become a person who notices sooner.

What does it ask of an ordinary day? A few small defences. Name the thing when it comes: "This is the puff of wind." A name takes some of the force out of it. Take three breaths before you answer or act. And deal with the actual pebble at once. Stop, sit down, take off the boot, shake it out. It takes thirty seconds, and it turns out to be a surprisingly good picture of the whole practice: do not walk on, out of stubbornness, with a small hard thing rubbing at you.

## For the walk

- What small thing is most likely to knock me off my course today, and what would a steadier answer look like?
- Take three breaths before you react to the next small irritation, and if you can, name it silently: "puff of wind." And if there is a stone in your boot, literally or otherwise, stop and deal with it now, even though it costs a minute. It is a small act that says you take the day seriously.

## At the end of the day

Tonight is a good time to be gentle with the person who walked today. Boots off. A cup of something warm. A moment, before you do anything else, in which nothing needs to be defended.

Think back for your puff of wind. Almost everyone had one. It may have been a sharp word, said or swallowed. It might have been a flash of envy, or a sudden decision to cut the day short. It might have been so small that you only noticed it afterwards: a slight meanness in the way you answered, a quick resentment over the last place in the queue. Do not be dramatic about it. Find it, look at it for a moment, and name it plainly.

What did you do next? That is the more interesting question, and it is often better than you fear. Perhaps you went on and stewed. Perhaps you recovered quickly and let it pass. Perhaps you apologised, or meant to. Whatever it was, you are not required to defend it tonight. And what would you have liked to do instead? A different word, a slower answer, a laugh? Holding the better version in mind, without scolding, is how it becomes easier next time.

If there is something that needs mending, an apology or a word of thanks, it can almost always wait for the morning, when you are rested and the other person is, too. Tired words made at night rarely repair anything.

Try also to remember where you were standing when it happened. Tired? Hungry? Too hot, or too cold? The pattern is often in the circumstances and not in the character, and knowing that gives you something practical to do about it, which is to eat, drink or sit down a little earlier.

And look for the places where you did not fall. A remark you let go by. A shortcut you declined. A sharp reply that you swallowed. These are easy to overlook, because nothing visibly happened, and they are exactly what the passage is hoping for. If you stayed kind in the face of your own irritation, count that too. It is the other half of the puff of wind: the small, steady hand that held the door.

Three questions for tonight:

- What small thing knocked me off course today?
- What did I do next, and what would I have liked to do?
- What pause, word or habit could I put in place tomorrow to meet it better?

An honest word about tiredness. The end of the day is exactly when the guard is lowest, and much of what we regret saying has been said tired. If you were short with someone this evening, it is quite likely that you were simply spent. That is not an excuse, only an explanation, and a reason to be kind to yourself. The point of the passage is not to make you ashamed of your weakness. It is to help you see it coming, and to give you an extra moment before the wind arrives.

## A short prayer

*Forgive the small unkindnesses of today, the ones I noticed and the ones I did not. Let the breeze that knocks me about be a little gentler tomorrow, and let me be a little steadier. Teach me to stop and shake out the stone. Where I fell, help me rise without shame. Let the wind drop tonight, and let the evening be calm. Let me wake gentler than I went to bed. Now let me rest.*
