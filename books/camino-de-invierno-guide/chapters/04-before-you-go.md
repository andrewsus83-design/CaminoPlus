# Before you go

## The credencial

The credencial is the pilgrim's passport, a folded card of stamps. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says it identifies the pilgrim, and that it has two practical uses: it gives access to the hostels of the Way, and it serves as the proof you show when you ask for the Compostela. The Office's page says it is issued by a parish church, a confraternity, a diocese, an association of friends of the Way, or another Christian institution connected with the pilgrimage. The Office's pages list confraternities and associations, in Spain and worldwide, so start there.

Stamps, the Office says, are normally collected where pilgrims sleep, but they can also be had at parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. On a road with few pilgrims, remember that list: a town hall will often do. The Office also mentions a digital credential app of its own. This book says nothing more about it, so read the Office's page before you rely on one.

## Getting to Ponferrada, and home from Santiago

Wikipedia says Ponferrada lies on the A-6 motorway, has intercity bus services, and has a railway station with trains to Madrid and Barcelona. The city has no airport of its own. The nearest, León, is ~106 km to the east, and the Santiago airport is ~197 km to the north-west. Timetables change, so check them in the month you travel.

Santiago's airport is ~12 km east of the city, and the city has a railway station. Do not plan a tight connection for the day you arrive: you will want to see the Pilgrim's Office and the cathedral first.

## Training

The Xunta's advice for pilgrims is to prepare physically, to plan the stages by what you can do, to pace your effort and to rest. It also says not to walk too fast in the first days, and to keep the same rhythm.

On this road that advice has teeth, because the days are long. A plan that suits a short road will not suit this one. In the weeks before you leave, practise walking ~20 km or more on two or three days in a row, with the pack you will carry, and include hills, because the road has a good many. Practise going downhill as well as up, because your knees and toes will feel the descents.

## What to carry

The Xunta's leaflet gives a plain list. Wear light, loose clothes in a light colour. Carry a light waterproof that also covers your pack, a hat or other head cover, sunglasses and sun cream. Carry a sleeping bag, a small multi-tool, a torch, a mobile phone and a small first-aid kit. Its suggested kit has antiseptic, gauze, tape, plasters, a needle and thread for blisters, small scissors, petroleum jelly, mosquito repellent, antihistamines and aspirin.

Add what the Confraternity of St James says about this road: expect mud, and cold and wet weather at any time of year. A warm layer and a dry bag for it matter here. Walking poles help on the steep stretches.

## Shoes and feet

The Xunta advises comfortable shoes that you have already worn, and says two pairs are a good idea. It advises well-fitting, breathable socks, clean and dry, and it says to wash your feet with soap and water at the end of the day and change your shoes. Mud is likely, and many walkers will want boots with a good grip. Break them in before you leave. Stop at the first hot spot and treat it.

## Money and language

Carry some cash as well as a card, and do not assume that a small place will take cards: ask.

The road is walked mostly in Spanish. The Confraternity recommends good basic Spanish. From the border of Galicia the signs and place names are in Galician, which is close to Spanish, and three words help: *rúa* is a street, *ponte* a bridge and *camiño* a way. A few Spanish phrases for directions, beds and food will serve you well.

## Maps, signal and booking ahead

Save an offline map before you leave home. The Confraternity says the waymarking has improved but may still be sparse or contradictory, and in the valleys a phone signal can fail. The CaminoPlus app is built to work without signal. It is still in development, and what it holds for this road is described at the end of this book.

Book your nights ahead. The app's own note for this route says to plan accommodation ahead, and the Castilla y León guide says services for pilgrims are not yet very abundant. The Xunta publishes a list of hostels as a PDF on its page for the road. This book does not repeat any list, because lists change. Ask in the app, and phone or message the place the day before.
