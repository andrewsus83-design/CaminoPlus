# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Winter Way starts in Ponferrada, the capital of the Bierzo, a region of León. The Xunta de Galicia calls it the entrance hall to Galicia. Here the Camino Francés goes straight on, and the pilgrim on the Winter Way turns left.

The first ~43 km are in León, by the figure of the Castilla y León tourism guide: out of Ponferrada, past the Roman gold workings of Las Médulas, to Puente de Domingo Flórez at the edge of Galicia. Then the road follows the River Sil through Valdeorras, in the province of Ourense, and crosses the south of Lugo, the Ribeira Sacra. It goes on through the Deza, in Pontevedra, and ends in Santiago, in A Coruña. The Xunta says the road crosses the four Galician provinces. Ourense has only a short share of the walking: the road is longer in Lugo and in Pontevedra.

At Lalín the Xunta says the road joins the Vía de la Plata. Its stage pages put the meeting at A Laxe, ~50 km from Santiago. The last two days follow the final stretch that the Winter Way shares with the Camino Sanabrés.

## How long is it?

The app's catalog gives ~260 km. The official sources do not agree, because they start counting in different places.

- The Xunta's route page gives ~239 km. That is the sum of its nine stages, and they begin at Las Médulas, not Ponferrada.
- The Xunta's leaflet, in Spanish, says about 260 km from Ponferrada, and its stage figures add up to ~262 km.
- The Castilla y León tourism guide measures ~271 km from Ponferrada.
- The Confraternity of St James says ~275 km, and so does Wikipedia.

Plan on ~260 to ~275 km. The ten days of this book add up to ~266 km.

## How many days?

The Xunta divides the road into nine stages, in both its pages and its leaflet. They are official units, not comfortable days: they run from ~14 to ~36 km, and three are longer than ~30 km. The CaminoPlus website lists the road as 10 to 12 stages. This book follows ten walking days named by the Xunta's stage towns, with Ponferrada to Las Médulas added at the start, and a chapter on the long days shows where each can be split.

## Why it is called the Winter Way

The name says the road was a winter alternative. What the sources say is more careful.

The Xunta writes that "it is thought" it may have arisen as a winter alternative to the hard climb to the snowy heights of O Cebreiro, where the Camino Francés enters Galicia, and perhaps to avoid the frequent floods of the rivers of the Valcarce valley. The Confraternity of St James says it is possible that the track was preferred in winter, and that there is "as yet no firm evidence" for the theory. The Castilla y León guide is bolder: it says pilgrims used the road until the eighteenth century, and that it faded once a comfortable way opened through the Valcarce in 1788. Wikipedia says pilgrims "traditionally" used it.

So the winter story is a plausible tradition, told with different degrees of confidence. The road's Roman roots are better documented.

## A short history

The Xunta says historians place the origin of the road in Roman times. A secondary Roman road is documented from the gold workings of Las Médulas towards the Via XVIII through Valdeorras, and the Roman tunnel at Montefurado is, it says, further evidence.

The stones remember pilgrims too. The Castilla y León guide says the Templar knights of the castle of Cornatel gave protection to pilgrims here, and that pilgrims had a hospital at Carucedo, beside Las Médulas. The Xunta's stage pages add the castle of Os Novaes, a Hospitaller residence, medieval hospitals at Belesar and San Pedro de Líncora, and a bridge near Rodeiro called A Ponte do Hospital, a name that recalls a pilgrims' hospital.

As a way marked and promoted for pilgrims, the road is young. The Xunta says it was made official in 2016 and calls it one of the newest Jacobean routes. The Confraternity says the Pilgrim's Office recognised it in 2015, and Wikipedia says 2016. These may be different events, and this book does not try to settle it.

## What it feels like

The Xunta describes a calm that "presides" over this alternative route, which few people use, and says that calm is one of its main attractions. The Confraternity calls it a solitary road for fit and confident walkers with good basic Spanish.

The landscape does the rest. You follow the Sil, wide and deep, between slate roofs and red clay. Vineyards climb the slopes in terraces, and later come oak and chestnut woods, granaries called *hórreos*, and old bridges, some of them Roman. The walking is hilly, the days are long, and the villages are small.
