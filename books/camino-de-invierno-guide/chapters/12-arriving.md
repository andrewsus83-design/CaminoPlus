# Arriving

## The last streets

The Winter Way brings you into Santiago from the south, over the River Sar and up through the Porta de Mazarelos, and the Xunta's last steps lead through the Praza das Praterías to the Praza do Obradoiro. Wikipedia says that construction of the present cathedral began in 1075 and that it was consecrated in 1211. It is a Romanesque church with Gothic and Baroque additions, built over the reputed tomb of St James. The Baroque façade on the Obradoiro square was completed by Fernando de Casas Novoa in 1740. Behind it stands the Romanesque Pórtico da Gloria of Master Mateo, whose completion in 1188 was carved on a stone. The crypt below the high altar is, says Wikipedia, where pilgrims of the ninth-century church ended their journey.

Pilgrims' Masses are held in the cathedral, and their times change, so ask on the day. The *botafumeiro*, the great swinging censer, is used on certain important religious holidays and, Wikipedia says, at all Pilgrims' Masses in a Holy Year. 2027 is a Holy Year, and the Holy Door is opened only in Holy Years.

## The Compostela

The Compostela is the certificate that the Pilgrim's Office in Santiago gives to those who meet its rules. The Office owns the rules, and they can change. This book says what the Office says, and sends you to the Office in any doubt.

- The Office gives it to pilgrims who follow the routes of the Way of St James on foot, by bicycle or on horseback, with the credencial stamped along the route.
- It asks for at least the last hundred kilometres on foot or on horseback, or the last two hundred kilometres by bicycle.
- On foot or on horseback, that means at least a hundred continuous kilometres on the same route to Santiago that the Office recognises. The final stage must include at least the last one leading into the cathedral.
- You may walk the Way in stages, if they follow in chronological and geographical order. A pilgrim who walks only the minimum distance must get the credencial stamped at the start and at the end of each stage, with the date.
- A pilgrim who has already walked part of a recognised route outside Spain needs no less than seventy kilometres in Spain.
- The Cabildo, the cathedral's governing council, reserves the right to grant the Compostela in special and exceptional cases.

*The Office awards the Compostela to those who walk for religious or spiritual reasons, and it describes the pilgrimage it has in mind as one made "with a Christian sentiment, even if it is only with an attitude of search". If that does not describe your reason for walking, ask the Pilgrim's Office what it can give you: it also offers a Certificate of Distance, which records the kilometres you walked from wherever you started.*

On its own pages the Office says the Certificate of Distance records the day and the starting point of your pilgrimage, the kilometres you covered, the day you arrived and the route, and that someone who has walked before can request it.

## This road and the rule

From Ponferrada you have ~260 km ahead of you, far more than the last hundred kilometres the Office asks for. A start further on is a matter of arithmetic, and the arithmetic here is uncertain.

- From Chantada, the Xunta's stage lengths add up to ~104 km, and its leaflet says ~103 km. That is close to the limit, and a short detour, or a different way of measuring, changes the answer.
- From Monforte de Lemos, the same stage lengths add up to ~135 km, and the leaflet says ~134 km. The Confraternity of St James gives Monforte as its suggested start for the minimum distance, at ~120 km from Santiago by its own figure. The sources differ by ~15 km.
- The Xunta says the Winter Way joins the Vía de la Plata, and its stage pages put the meeting at A Laxe, ~50 km from Santiago. The Office's rule speaks of the same route. This book does not say how the Office counts a walk that begins on one route and ends on another.

This book does not say whether any particular start counts. Ask the Pilgrim's Office before you go, say where you plan to start, and keep your credencial stamped along the way. The Office's pages on the Compostela and the credencial do not name the routes it recognises.

## At the Pilgrim's Office

The Office issues the Compostela, a document in Latin. You will need your stamped credencial. Procedures and hours change, so look at the Office's own website before you arrive, and do not plan a tight connection for the same day.

Whatever the Compostela means to you, it is a record of a road you walked, not a measure of it.

## The next day

Do not leave on the morning after you arrive. Santiago is a city built for the end of a pilgrimage, and it rewards a second day.

- **Rest.** After a road like this one, your legs have earned it.
- **See the cathedral without a queue behind you.** Sit in the Obradoiro square and watch the others arrive.
- **Think about what comes next.** If you want to keep walking, the Camino to Finisterre and Muxía, ~90 km, goes on from Santiago, and has its own book in this series.
- **Plan your way home** with the timetables in front of you, not at the last minute.
