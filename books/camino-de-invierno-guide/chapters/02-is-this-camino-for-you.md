# Is this Camino for you?

## What the official ratings say

The Xunta rates the Winter Way Medium-High overall. The app's catalog rates it 3 on a scale of 1 to 5, the same as the Camino Francés and one below the Camino Primitivo.

By stage, the Xunta's ratings run like this. The last day into Santiago is Low. Five stages are Medium. The stage from Quiroga to Monforte de Lemos is Medium-High. The stages from Monforte to Chantada and from Chantada to Rodeiro are High. Those two High days are consecutive.

## What makes it demanding

**The length of the days.** Seven of the Xunta's nine stages are between ~26 and ~36 km, and its estimates for the three longest are about seven hours of walking each. For the longest, ~36 km, that is a pace of ~5 km an hour with no stops, so plan on more.

**The hills.** The road is rarely flat for long. The Xunta describes a sharp ascent to the Alto da Serra on the way to Monforte, a hard climb of about 3 km after the bridge of Belesar, and a hard ascent of ~4.8 km on Monte Faro, to over ~1,100 m. Even the last day ends with a steep climb into Santiago.

**The quiet.** The Xunta says the road is little used, and the Confraternity of St James calls it solitary, with some stretches long and isolated. A Castilla y León tourism guide says services for pilgrims are not yet very abundant. The app's own note for this route says few pilgrims walk it and that you should plan accommodation ahead. A road with few walkers is a pleasure, but it means you cannot count on a stranger, a shop or a bed appearing when you need one.

**The waymarking.** The Confraternity says the waymarking has improved over recent years, but that signs may still be sparse or contradictory in places, and that you should be ready to ask local people for directions. That is easier with some Spanish.

## Who it suits

The Confraternity's words are the best summary: fit and confident walkers with good basic Spanish. It suits you if you have already walked several long days in a row, if you are comfortable with your own company, and if you are willing to plan each night. It suits a walker who has done the Francés and wants its opposite. It also suits anyone drawn to the Sil, the terraced vineyards, Roman remains and small stone villages more than to company on the road.

## Who might choose another

If this is your first Camino and you want company, consider the Camino Francés, which the Castilla y León guide calls the best known and most used of the Jacobean routes. It has its own book in this series, *The French Way*.

If you have only a week, the Camino Inglés is a walk of six days, and has its own book too, *The English Way*. If mountains are what you want, *The Original Way* covers the Primitivo.

If the idea of long hours alone worries you, try a few days of it first, or choose a busier road.

## Compared with its neighbours

From Ponferrada, the Camino Francés is ~209 km to Santiago, by the kilometre table of the Spanish Wikipedia. The Winter Way is ~260 km: ~50 km longer, and in return you leave the main stream of pilgrims behind.

The name promises a gentler road than the Francés, which crosses O Cebreiro at ~1,330 m. The Winter Way has its own high point on Monte Faro, and the French Wikipedia gives ~1,187 m as the highest point of the route. That is lower than O Cebreiro, but the Xunta itself offers a shorter variant around the climb of Monte Faro, which in its words avoids the snow in winter. The fair summary is that the Winter Way is the quieter road, hillier than its name suggests, with services that the Castilla y León guide calls not yet abundant.
