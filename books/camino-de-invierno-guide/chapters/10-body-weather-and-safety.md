# Body, weather and safety

This chapter is plain on purpose. The Winter Way is a quiet road with long days and few services, and a small problem is harder to solve when no one is near. Nothing here is a reason to stay at home. It is what a careful walker does.

## Feet and legs

Your feet decide how the walk goes. Stop at the first hot spot and dress it before it becomes a blister. Rest for a few minutes each hour, and eat and drink before you are hungry or thirsty.

Pace matters more than speed. The Xunta says not to walk too fast in the first days, and to keep the same rhythm. With stages of ~26 to ~36 km, the first day sets the tone for the rest.

The descents are as demanding as the climbs, and this road has long ones: from Pedrices after Las Médulas, and from Monte Faro down to the valley of A Ermida. Shorten your stride and use poles if you have them. If you feel a sharp or growing pain in a knee, a shin or the top of a foot, stop. Rest for a day, or take a shorter stage. A day lost to rest is better than a week lost to an injury.

## Water

The Xunta advises drinking at least two litres a day, and never drinking from streams, rivers, springs or fountains unless you are sure the water is safe. Where villages can be far apart, leave each place with enough for the next stretch, and more on a hot day.

## Heat

The valleys of the Sil and the Miño are the hot part of the road. Wikipedia describes the Ribeira Sacra as having long hot summers, says that temperatures above ~35 °C are not uncommon in Ponferrada in heatwaves, and gives ~41.5 °C as the record there, in July 2022. The Xunta advises light, loose clothes in a light colour, a head cover, sunglasses and sun cream, and avoiding the hottest hours. Start before the heat, carry more water than you think you need, and rest in the shade. If you are exhausted or have cramps, the Xunta says, rest somewhere cool and drink plenty. A headache, dizziness or nausea are signs to stop; if they do not pass, ask for help.

In dry summers, ask locally about fires and closed paths. *Check before you go.*

## Cold, rain and snow

The Confraternity of St James warns that the "Winter" road can have the same snow, sleet, fog and high winds as the mountains of the Camino Francés, that weather in the hills can be unpredictable, and that you should be ready for mud, cold and wet at any time of year. Wet clothes cool you fast, even in mild weather. Carry a waterproof, a warm layer, a hat and gloves, and keep one dry layer in a bag that cannot get wet. If you start to shiver, stop and get warm before you go on.

Two places call for particular care.

- **Monte Faro.** The climb is the highest on the road. The Xunta offers a shorter alternative from Penasillás that, in its words, avoids the snow in winter. If there is snow or cloud on the summit, take it. Look at the forecast the evening before and again in the morning.
- **The flat ground after Monforte.** The Xunta's Spanish text says flooding is frequent at the start of the stage to Chantada in the rainy season. After heavy rain, ask locally before you start.

## Fog, waymarking and roads

The Confraternity says the waymarking has improved but may still be sparse or contradictory in places. The Xunta's advice is never to leave the marked paths and to avoid walking when it gets dark. In fog, stay on the marked way. If you cannot see the next marker, stop, and do not guess: go back to the last one you saw. A map saved on your phone is the best backup.

Much of the road runs on tracks, and some of it runs on or beside roads. The Xunta's pages name stretches of the national road N-120 near Espandariz, the provincial road LU-933 after it, the C-533 from Rodeiro, and the national road N-525 at the start of the stage from Bendoiro, which the path then follows. Walk on the left margin of the road, facing the traffic, as the Xunta advises, and wear something bright. Never walk on a road in the dark or in thick fog.

Dogs on farms may bark as you pass. Walk on calmly, and do not stare at them or run.

## Walking alone

Many people on this road walk alone for hours. Tell someone where you will sleep each night and message them when you arrive. Keep your phone charged and your map saved on it, and know the name of the last village you passed. A phone signal can fail in the valleys, so do not rely on it.

## When to stop

Stop, and get help, for chest pain, a fever, severe dizziness, a fall with a blow to the head, a swollen joint, or anything that frightens you. Stop too if you are cold and cannot get warm, if you are lost in fog, or if you have run out of water. A doctor is the right person for a worry, and a plan can always change. The road will still be there.

**112** is the European emergency number, and it works in Spain. Say where you are as exactly as you can: the village, the stage, the last landmark you passed.

## Check before you go

Before each day, check the forecast, any local notice about the path, and the state of the ground: snow on Monte Faro, floods near Monforte, fires in a dry summer. Ask where you slept.
