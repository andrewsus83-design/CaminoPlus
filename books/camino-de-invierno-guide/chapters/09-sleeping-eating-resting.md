# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Where you will sleep

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: shared rooms, bunk beds, a shared bathroom and a kitchen in some, and a *hospitalero*, the host, who stamps your credencial. The Pilgrim's Office says the credencial gives access to the hostels of the Way, so have it with you.

The Xunta runs a public network of hostels on its Camino portal, and it publishes a list of hostels as a PDF on its page for the road. The Office says that hostels with no subsidy are kept going, in austerity, by pilgrims' contributions, so leave what you can and leave the place clean. It also asks groups organised with a support car, or by bicycle, to find other shelter than the pilgrim hostels.

This book names no hostel and says where none is. Hostels open and close, and a wrong line costs a tired person dearly. On this road the point is sharper than usual. The Confraternity of St James says there are hostels, or albergues not specific to pilgrims, at various points along the way, and a Castilla y León guide says services for pilgrims are not yet very abundant. So work out your nights first, then your days. Use the app and the Xunta's list, and confirm the night before.

The Confraternity adds that there is plenty of private accommodation in the towns on the way. A road with so few pilgrims makes the evening a time to plan the next day, not just to rest.

## Living with other people

Rooms are shared and the rules are simple.

- **Be quiet early and late.** Pack your bag the night before, and use a torch, not the ceiling light, when you leave in the dark.
- **Keep your boots and pack off the beds.** Some hostels ask for boots to be left at the door, so ask.
- **Check your bunk before you unpack.** If you find bites or bugs, tell the *hospitalero*, and if you can, wash and dry your clothes on a hot cycle.
- **Earplugs and a sleep mask** are the usual answer to snoring and early lights.

## Eating

A *menú del peregrino* is a set meal for pilgrims, offered in some places. This book gives no prices and names no restaurant: ask where you sleep.

Lalín holds a famous annual fair of *cocido*, the Galician stew. The Valdeorras and Ribeira Sacra regions are wine country, and the wines have denominations of origin. The Castilla y León guide singles out, for the villages of the Bierzo, roasted chestnuts, *empanada*, cabbage broth, a sausage called *androlla*, country bread and almond sweets.

The practical point is the long days. The Confraternity says some stretches are long and isolated, and the Xunta's advice is to eat energetic food on the way: nuts, figs, chocolate. Buy lunch before you set out, and do not trust a village on the map to have anything open when you arrive.

## Rest days

A rest day is part of a plan, not a failure of it. On this road two places suggest themselves. Monforte de Lemos comes at the end of the longest day, with its fortress, its monastery and its museums, and the Xunta's leaflet lists a good deal to see there. Santiago comes at the end, and the chapter on arriving says why to stay a second day.

If you need a rest in the middle of the road, build it into the plan, and ask in the app which of the larger places can host you.

## In the evening

Wash your feet, as the Xunta advises, and look at them before you sleep. Then spend ten minutes on tomorrow: how long it is, where you will eat lunch, where the water is, and where you will sleep. On a quiet road that is the best habit you can have.
