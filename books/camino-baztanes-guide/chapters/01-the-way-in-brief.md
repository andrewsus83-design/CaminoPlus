# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Camino Baztanés (in French *la voie du Baztan*) runs from Bayonne to Pamplona. The app's catalogue gives it those two ends, in France and Spain, and no length. The public sources do. Visit Navarra's blog splits the road into six stages totalling ~109 km, and the Camino pages of a Spanish consumer guide, Eroski Consumer, give the same six stages and the same total. Both put ~32 of those kilometres in the French Basque Country and ~77 in Navarre.

The road starts at the Gothic cathedral of Bayonne and follows the river Nive upstream. It climbs through the hills of Labourd, the French Basque province, to Ainhoa, and crosses the frontier at Dantxarinea, a district of the Navarrese village of Urdazubi/Urdax. Then it goes over the pass of Otsondo into the Baztán valley, follows the valley to Elizondo, climbs over the pass of Belate, and comes down the Ultzama valley to the edge of Pamplona. At the medieval bridge of the Trinity, in the village of Arre, it meets the Camino Francés. The last ~5 km, by Villava and Burlada, are on the French Way.

Bayonne is a crossroads. Its tourist office says it lies where two ways meet, the Camino del Norte and the Voie de Soulac, and that you can join the Camino Francés at Pamplona by continuing on the Baztán road from there. That is the road's place: a quiet way in to the Francés, or a week's walk in its own right.

## What kind of road it is

The French association of the Pyrénées-Atlantiques, the Amis du chemin de St-Jacques, calls it a little-known road through the green landscapes of the northern Basque Country and the equally green valleys of Navarre, and says it crosses the Pyrenees by the lowest pass, Belate. Visit Navarra says that pass is considerably lower than the entrances of the French and Aragonese Ways. Sources give its height differently: ~847 m (the French association, and French Wikipedia), about 900 m (the association's own description of the day), ~938 m (Visit Navarra's blog). Otsondo is ~670 m, says French Wikipedia. Whatever the height, the climbs to both are real.

The app's route notes describe the road as hilly, with forest paths, and say rain is common; they call it a small route where you need to plan your stops. Expect woods, field gates, stone farmhouses, a few small towns and long stretches with none.

## A road with a long memory

Visit Navarra calls it one of the oldest paths in Navarre. In the Middle Ages, its blog says, armies, merchants and kings used it to cross the Pyrenees, and from the ninth century European pilgrims did too, especially people from Aquitaine and Brittany. The proof, it says, is the pilgrims' hospitals and monasteries of the eleventh and twelfth centuries, known from their remains and from deeds of donation. It names the monasteries of Urdazubi/Urdax and Belate. A 1964 study by Padre Germán de Pamplona argued that the Bayonne to Pamplona road by Belate was far busier than people thought, and Eroski Consumer says the study has guided the road's recovery.

The recovery is recent. Eroski says the Association of Friends of the Camino de Santiago in Urdax-Baztán began in 2000 to recover the road, and a pilgrim-hostel directory, Albergues del Camino, says it was close to lost in places and that volunteers cleared paths with brushcutters. According to both, the little chapel of Santiago at Belate was reopened in 2016. The directory also reports the association's own account that documents about the road go back to the twelfth century. Treat that as a claim, not a proof.

## When the sources disagree

Lengths differ from source to source, and so do the stages. This book follows Visit Navarra's six stages, which Eroski repeats. The French association's own topo offers five stages totalling ~108 km, and the directory counts six stages from Bayonne to Arre, ~103 km. Visit Navarra's own route page shows ~86 km for the part from Urdazubi/Urdax to Arre, which does not match its blog's ~77 km in Navarre; this book uses the blog's figures. Each day's heading gives a rounded length, and the chapter on other ways to split the road shows the alternatives. Treat every figure as a good guide, not a measurement.
