# Before you go

## Your credencial

The *credencial* is the pilgrim's passport: a folded card with boxes for the stamps, or *sellos*, that you collect on the way. The Pilgrim's Office in Santiago says the stamps are normally collected where pilgrims sleep, but can also be had in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, town halls and other places. It also says where an official card comes from: parish churches, associations of friends of the Way, pilgrim hostels, confraternities and other authorised institutions, in Spain and abroad. The Confraternity of St James in Britain, for example, says its pilgrim credencial is accredited by Santiago Cathedral.

The simplest plan is to bring a card from home. If you would rather get one on the road, an Eroski Consumer note, of uncertain date, says that credencials and stamps could be had at Bayonne's cathedral, and the association of the Pyrénées-Atlantiques lists a pilgrims' refuge in Bayonne among its activities. None of this is a promise, so ask. On this road the card is your key to the pilgrims' hostels.

## Getting there, and getting back

Visit Navarra's blog describes two usual ways to begin. Most people who start in Navarre, it says, travel to Pamplona, take a bus to Elizondo and then a taxi to Urdazubi/Urdax. Others begin in Bayonne, reached by bus from San Sebastián. Starting at Urdax skips the French half, ~36 km, and begins with Day 3.

This book names no timetables, because they change, and none of the pages read for it gives a current timetable for either end of this road. Look before you leave home, ask at your hostel or the tourist office, and do not plan a train or a flight for the afternoon of your last walking day.

## Training and packing

The Xunta de Galicia, the regional government of Galicia, publishes general advice for pilgrims, and it applies here. Do not set off untrained. Take long walks in the weeks before you go, lengthen them gradually, and plan stages that fit your fitness. With a longest day of ~25 km and two long climbs, a practice walk of ~20 km with your loaded pack is a fair test.

Keep the pack light. The Xunta says a correctly packed rucksack should not exceed 10 kg, with padded straps, and that a waterproof garment belongs in the pack regardless of the time of year. On a wet mountain road, that advice is the heart of the list:

- **Rain gear that works.** A jacket, something for your legs or a poncho that also covers your pack, and a bag or liner so your dry clothes stay dry.
- **A warm layer** for the passes, where the sources speak of mist.
- **Shoes with grip.** The Xunta says footwear must be broken in, with hard soles for rough ground, and that new stiff shoes will hurt you. It recommends seamless socks, and drying your feet thoroughly, because damp feet invite fungus.
- **A head torch**, for the short days of the year.
- **A map you can use without signal.** The waymarks on the French side are small, and a pilgrim-hostel directory advises downloading the track before the day of Belate. The app's catalogue lists this road with its two ends and no stage-by-stage data, so carry a track from another source and make sure it works offline.

## Money, papers and language

The Xunta advises carrying your documents and cards rather than a lot of cash. Several stage ends are small villages, so keep a little cash for places that do not take cards. The Policía Foral, the regional police of Navarre, asks pilgrims to keep their passport or national identity card in a safe place. You are crossing an international frontier on foot, so carry yours.

The first two days are in France, and French helps there. After the frontier it is Spanish, and the official pages of Navarre give place names in Basque and Spanish together, such as Urdazubi/Urdax, Amaiur/Maya and Doneztebe/Santesteban. A few polite words in each language, *bonjour*, *merci*, *buenos días*, *gracias*, go a long way.
