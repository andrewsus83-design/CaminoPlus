# France: Bayonne to Urdax

## The character of these days

Two days in the French Basque Country: a river and a ridge. The first is flat and easy. The second is hilly and wooded, and it ends in Spain. The lengths in this book follow Visit Navarra's blog, which Eroski Consumer repeats; the chapter on other ways to split the road shows the alternatives.

These are the two days to watch the waymarks. The French association says the road is marked *voie du Baztan* all the way to the frontier, and that beyond it you follow the Spanish yellow arrows. Eroski says the French marks are small, and that you must pay more attention to the Camino here than you will later.

### Day 1: Bayonne to Ustaritz (~14 km)

Visit Navarra's blog says this stage has no difficulty and puts it at about three hours. It begins in front of Bayonne's Gothic cathedral, which the city's tourist office says UNESCO listed in 1998 among the monuments of the *Routes of Santiago de Compostela in France*. The French association also starts you from its pilgrims' refuge in the city. Either way you go down to the river Nive and keep it beside you.

Almost the whole day is the Nive's towpath, the *chemin de halage*, which Eroski explains was the path from which mules pulled the merchants' barges. It runs flat between orchards, fields and riverside trees, under a motorway and a railway bridge. The day ends at Ustaritz, where Eroski mentions the church of St Vincent, of the nineteenth century.

Use the easy day to learn what the Camino's mark looks like, because tomorrow asks more of you. For a longer first day, Eroski says the stage can be stretched to Souraïde or Espelette, ~25 km from Bayonne, which is also the French association's first stage.

### Day 2: Ustaritz to Urdazubi/Urdax (~21 km)

Visit Navarra's blog gives this day about five and a half hours, and says the first ~13.5 km are the leg-breakers, *rompepiernas*. After that it is gentler. The Spanish original of Eroski's page says the same: a day of two halves.

You leave Ustaritz by the old town hall square and climb through oak and chestnut woods, crossing small streams, and then follow gravel tracks between meadows. Eroski points to two places in the first ~4 km where it says many pilgrims lose the way, and advises following only the marks of the *voie du Baztan*, since other yellow marks lead to local routes. Espelette, famous for its pepper, is about a kilometre off the way, says Eroski.

Souraïde, ~10 km into the day, has a church dedicated to St James, says the French association. Above the village the way climbs to the Col de Pinodieta, where Eroski warns that it crosses another Camino, the one from Saint-Jean-Pied-de-Port to Irun. Cross the road and carry straight on.

Then comes the descent to Ainhoa. The Pyrénées-Atlantiques tourism site, Tourisme 64, says Ainhoa is a *bastide-rue*, a village laid out along one broad street, built in the twelfth century to welcome pilgrims on the voie du Baztan; its church of Notre-Dame de l'Assomption is of the thirteenth century and has tiers of galleries. The commune's own page says the village straddles the frontier with Spain, and that the pilgrims' road passes through it.

From Ainhoa small roads and low rises lead to the frontier, ~18 km into the day. On the French side the last district is Dantxaria, which belongs to Ainhoa; on the Spanish side it is Dantxarinea, a district of Urdazubi/Urdax. Eroski says Dantxarinea has lived on border trade for generations, and that you pass the old customs buildings and a small stream, the Lapitxuri, as you enter Spain. Then the yellow arrows begin, and a path beside the Ugarana river leads on to Urdazubi/Urdax.

Visit Navarra's blog says the monastery here had a pilgrims' hospital, and its route page says the caves of Urdazubi/Urdax, carved by the river Urtxuma and about 14,000 years old, can be visited on a guided tour. Ask locally when. Tomorrow starts with a climb.

**Care.** At Dantxarinea the way runs beside a national road, the N-121-B, and a little later it leaves the road at a Jacobean milestone, says Eroski. Watch for the turn, and watch the traffic. On this day the marks are small, so check your track at each junction in the first hour.
