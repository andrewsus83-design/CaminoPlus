# Body, weather and safety

## Feet, and the wet

On a road where rain is common, feet are the first thing to suffer. The Xunta's advice for pilgrims is to wear shoes that are broken in, to wash and dry your socks whenever you stop, to dry your feet thoroughly after any wetting, and to see a pharmacist or a doctor for anything that worries you. Do it early. A small blister on Day 2 is a large one by Day 5.

## Mist, mud and the two hard days

The two hard days are the days when the weather decides. The French association says Otsondo is easily wrapped in mist, and Eroski Consumer says the same of its woods. A pilgrim-hostel directory says that on Day 5 the weather weighs more than on any other day, and that after several days of rain or in fog the going can be much slower.

- **Look at the forecast the evening before, and again at breakfast,** and start early on Days 3 and 5. Both have a long climb, and both are better done with a margin of daylight.
- **On Day 5, know where you are.** Eroski says the road runs close to the path as far as the monastery of Belate, so help is near on the climb. From there to Lantz the path runs through forest, and Eroski calls it the loneliest part. If you cannot see the next mark, go back to the last one, and do not carry on in the hope that the cloud will lift.
- **In winter, snow.** Eroski says snow is easy to find at the pass of Belate, and its own advice then is to abandon the stage or to go on only with extreme caution, along the road from the inn on the climb. This book would add: ask locally before you start, and do not cross on snow you cannot judge. A road in snow has its own risks.
- **Mud and water.** Eroski notes mud beside the streams on Day 2, a path that turns muddy after a little rain between Enderiz and Olaiz on Day 6, and a stream on the way to Belate that runs with water in the rainy season. Poles or a staff can help on slippery ground; the Xunta says a staff is a great help on stony paths.

## Roads, gates and traffic

The Policía Foral, the regional police of Navarre, gives the standard advice for walkers on a road: walk on the left, and on the hard shoulder if there is one. In a group, walk in single file. At night or in poor visibility, use lights or reflective clothing.

Visit Navarra says this road keeps away from road traffic. Eroski's descriptions still mention crossings of main roads: beside the frontier at Dantxarinea, below the pass of Otsondo, and on the climb to Belate. Cross with care, and not where a bend hides the traffic. You will also pass field gates and cattle grids. Close gates behind you, and leave the fields as you found them.

## Heat and sun

This is not a hot road, but the summer sun is real, and the last day comes down into the Pamplona basin, where Meteo Navarra says July and August maxima average ~28 to ~30 °C. The Xunta advises a cap, high-protection sunscreen, and a water bottle you refill often. Meteo Navarra adds one thing worth knowing for the northern valleys: a south wind can bring a sharp rise in temperature, with warm, dry air that spreads fires quickly. If a fire warning is out, take it seriously, and ask locally.

## Your belongings

The Policía Foral's advice for pilgrims is simple: keep your things in sight, carry bags and rucksacks closed and fastened, take only the money you need, in an inner wallet, and keep your passport or identity card somewhere safe. If something is stolen, do not confront the thief; report it to the Policía Foral.

## When to stop, and 112

Know your own limits and respect them. If you feel unwell, if a pain grows, or if the weather turns on a hard day, stopping is the right plan, not a failure. For anything that worries you, see a doctor. **112 is the European emergency number**, and it works in France and in Spain. In the mountains, tell your host where you are going and when you expect to arrive.
