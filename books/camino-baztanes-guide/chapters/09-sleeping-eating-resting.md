# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door, and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Pilgrim's Office says that hostels without subsidies are kept going within austerity, with contributions from pilgrims.

On this road the hostels are small, and they are run by different hands: Eroski Consumer and a pilgrim-hostel directory describe an association of friends of the Way, parishes and town councils. Visit Navarra's blog counts a hostel about every ~11.5 km on average in Navarre, so a bed is not on every corner. The directory says several stage ends are small villages, and that some hostels work by prior notice or by collecting a key. Eroski says beds may be scarce in high season. This book lists none of them, because they change. Use the app and the phone, and book ahead, above all for the end of a hard day.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and pack your rucksack in the hall, not in the dark by your bed.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed. Earplugs go a long way, and if you are the one who snores, apologise at breakfast.

Bedbugs can turn up in any shared bed. Look at the seams of the mattress before you lie down, and if you are bitten tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. If you are ill or sleeping badly, take a private room.

## Eating

Visit Navarra's pages name what to look for: the curd of the Ultzama (*cuajada*), grilled beef chops (*chuletones*), the set meals of the cider houses, the chocolate with hazelnuts of Elizondo, and the corn flatbreads called *talos* from the mill at Amaiur. On the French side Eroski points to the Espelette pepper, which hangs drying on the houses. In Pamplona, Visit Navarra recommends the *pinchos*, small bites served in the bars of the old town.

Eating on the road takes planning. Eroski's notes mention long stretches with nothing: the climb of Otsondo, the forest beyond Belate down to Lantz, and the first ~14 km of the last day, as far as Sorauren. They also name places that close one day a week, a Monday or a Tuesday. Those details change, so treat them as a warning, not a list. Buy food the evening before a long day, and carry enough for a whole day on Days 3, 5 and 6.

The Xunta's health advice for pilgrims is to drink about two litres of water a day, and never to go beyond fifteen kilometres without drinking. Fill your bottle whenever you can, even when it rains.

## Resting

A short road is easy to treat as a sprint. Do not. **Elizondo**, the main town of the valley, has more services than the villages round it, says Visit Navarra's blog, and a day there before Belate is a sound use of a day if the weather has turned. **Pamplona** is a city to spend a day in, and the chapter on arriving says why.

On the road, listen to your body before your plan. If a pain grows instead of fading, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
