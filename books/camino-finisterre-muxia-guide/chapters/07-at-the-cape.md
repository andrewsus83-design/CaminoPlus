# At the cape: sunset, sea and boots

## The lighthouse and the zero

The Xunta ends its stage at the lighthouse and calls the tip of the cape kilometre zero of the Way of St James. The lighthouse was built in 1853, according to Spanish Wikipedia, after a decision taken in 1843. It is an octagonal granite tower, ~17 m high, on a promontory ~120 m above the sea, with a fog siren from 1888, a hint that fog is part of the cape's weather. Wikipedia's pictures of the cape show a bronze sculpture of a pilgrim's boot.

## Why it was the end of the earth

Finisterre is not the westernmost point of Spain or of Europe, and the sources say so. Wikipedia notes that Cape Touriñán, to the north, is ~124 m farther west, and that Cabo da Roca in Portugal lies ~16.5 km farther west than Finisterre. What made it the end of the earth was its look and its legend. Turismo de Galicia says that, according to legend, the Phoenicians raised an altar to the sun there, the Ara Solis, and that the Roman general Brutus took it for the place where the sun died at dusk. It also says that pilgrims who went on from Santiago followed the route the Milky Way marked out overhead, until they could go no further.

The Costa da Morte, the Coast of Death, is named for its shipwrecks. Wikipedia says the name first appeared in a newspaper article of 1904, after a run of wrecks. It mentions the loss of the torpedo cruiser HMS Serpent in 1890, with 173 of its 176 crew, and the sinking of the oil tanker Prestige in 2002, one of the worst oil spills in history. A local legend, Wikipedia adds, says that the wind here creates wild nightmares.

## The sunset

The Concello de Fisterra's page on the old rites names two. The first is the bath of purification, at Langosteira. The second is to watch the sunset, which the Concello describes as the death of the sun in the sea and its resurrection the next day, like the pilgrim's resurrection in the daily walking. Turismo de Galicia says it is not every day that you get to see the sun go down at world's end.

Plan for it, and do not make it a test. The chapter on when to go gives the times, from ~18:05 at midwinter to ~22:20 at midsummer, and they are approximate. Cloud may take the show away, and the walk was never for the show alone. If you can, give the cape a whole evening:

- **Go up with time to spare**, at least an hour before sunset, and carry a warm layer.
- **Know your way back before the light goes.** Carry a head torch and use it on the road.
- **Stay on the paths and behind the fences.** The lighthouse stands on a cliff, and the wind is strong. Never climb on wet rock to improve a photograph.

## The boots

Many pilgrims have taken up the custom of burning their boots or their clothes at the cape, as a way of leaving the old road behind. This book does not encourage it, and the town asks you not to.

The Concello's own page on the old rites does not mention burning at all. In October 2022 the news site Galicia Press quoted the Concello, which called burning a "false ritual" that turns a natural place into something like a dump and can even start forest fires. The same report said the town was launching a campaign, *Buen Camino, mejor final* ("A good Way, a better ending"), with a container at the zero kilometre for boots and belongings, to be handled in a clean and sustainable way.

In June 2024 the newspaper El Debate reported that the burning had been stamped out in 2022, and that a new habit had grown in its place: tying clothes and other objects to the fence at the lighthouse. According to the paper, the council had put notices in the hostels, appealed to people's civic sense, and put the local authorities to fining anyone caught leaving things. It judged these measures insufficient, and noted that in the strong wind many of the objects end up in the sea. The pilgrim news site Vive Camino adds that fires from bonfires have happened in recent years, and that burning some items releases toxic fumes.

This book could not read the text of any local rule, so it gives no legal wording and no figure for a fine. It says only what these sources say. The sense of it is plain: do not light a fire at the cape, and do not leave things behind.

There are quieter ways to do what the fire was meant to do:

- **Carry the boots home.** That is what the bag in your pack is for.
- **If you must part with them,** ask the tourist office or your hostel what the town asks pilgrims to do, and use the container if it is still there.
- **Keep the ritual and drop the flame.** Write down what you are leaving behind and keep the paper in your pocket. Read it again in a month.

The old rites the town names are the bath and the sunset. Those are enough.
