# The Way in brief

## Where it goes

The Xunta de Galicia describes the Fisterra and Muxía Way as a route that "originates in the city of Santiago" and has two goals: Cape Fisterra and the sanctuary of the Virxe da Barca in Muxía. It starts in the Praza do Obradoiro, crosses the Tambre at the bridge of Ponte Maceira, passes through Negreira and Olveiroa, and meets the sea at Cee and Corcubión. Fisterra is the first goal. Muxía lies to the north, on a peninsula of its own, and is the second.

The CaminoPlus catalog gives the road to Finisterre as ~90 km, and the sources keep close to it. The Xunta's four stages to the lighthouse add up to ~90 km, and its own text says ~89 km to Fisterra and ~87 km to Muxía. The Concello de Muxía, the town council, gives ~86.5 km from Santiago to the sanctuary, and the Concello de Fisterra's three stages add up to ~88 km. If you want both capes, the Xunta gives the whole road as ~118.8 km going to Fisterra first, and ~119.9 km going to Muxía first.

## The Way that leaves Santiago

Every other route in this series is a way *to* Santiago. This is a way *from* it. The Concello de Fisterra calls it the only Camino whose goal is not Santiago but its origin. The Xunta writes that after visiting the remains of the apostle many pilgrims decide to see "this end of the world", and calls the route the most faithful realisation of the old pilgrims' cry *Ultreia*, "farther beyond", answered by *Et suseia*, "and higher".

## What it feels like

The first two days are inland and green: oak groves, small rivers, stone bridges, hamlets, hórreos, gorse and pine and eucalyptus. The sea does not show itself until the third day, when, as the Xunta puts it, you first see Cape Fisterra from the Cruceiro da Armada, a viewpoint at ~247 m, "still far off". After that the road belongs to the Atlantic: the ría of Corcubión, the long beach of Langosteira, the lighthouse at the end of the cape, then the beaches and headlands on the way to Muxía.

## Why it exists

The name Finisterre comes from the Latin *finis terrae*, "end of the earth", and in Roman times, as Wikipedia puts it, the cape was believed to be an end of the known world. The Concello de Fisterra says the idea of a pilgrimage towards the westernmost lands of Europe was already present in Celtic beliefs, bound up with the sun's daily journey. Turismo de Galicia, the regional tourism board, tells how the Roman general Decimus Junius Brutus came to believe that this was where the sun died at dusk. Wikipedia's account of that general's campaign, in 138 to 136 BC, takes him no further than southern Gallaecia, so treat the story as a legend, and a good one.

The Christian pilgrimage is well documented only from the Middle Ages. A heritage report of 2013 on a Xunta website, written in Galician (the translations here are the book's own), finds the first unmistakable reference to Fisterra as a long-distance pilgrimage place in the bequest of a canon of Santiago in 1283, which lists it with Rocamadour and Roncesvalles. Its oldest news of Muxía is from 1446, when Sebastian Ilsung, a pilgrim from Augsburg, came to see the stone boat. Wikipedia sums up the position honestly: the origin of the pilgrimage to Finisterre is not certain.

The report adds something worth knowing before you leave. Most of the medieval accounts it quotes show travellers who were passive (they went because they had been told in Santiago that one must), curious (to see the end of the world) and even offhand. Domenico Laffi, writing in 1673, treated the trip as a whim. You do not have to be sure why you are going.

## A road with its own rules

This is not a Compostela route. The Compostela is the certificate of the Pilgrim's Office for the walk that arrives at the cathedral, and this walk begins where that one ends. The Office's pages read for this book say nothing about Fisterra or Muxía. The certificates of the coast, the Fisterrana and the Muxiana, are another matter, and the chapter on arriving says where the official sources place them.
