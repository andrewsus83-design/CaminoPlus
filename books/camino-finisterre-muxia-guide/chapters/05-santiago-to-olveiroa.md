# The inland days: Santiago to Olveiroa

## The character of these days

The first two days go west through country that has changed little in its outline: oak groves, stone bridges over the Sarela and the Tambre, hamlets of granaries and small fields. There is no sea, and the first day begins with a city to leave behind. The second is the long one.

The lengths in this book follow the Xunta's stage pages. Other sources differ by a kilometre or two, and the chapter on ways to split the days lists them. Treat each figure as a good guide, not a measurement.

### Day 1: Santiago to Negreira (~21 km)

The Xunta rates this stage high and estimates ~4 h 20 min. It starts as every Camino ends: in the Praza do Obradoiro. You leave by the line of the vanished Porta do Peregrino, the Pilgrim's Gate, also called the Porta da Trindade, down the Costa do Cristo and along the Rúa das Hortas, past the church of San Frutuoso. A document of 1536, according to a Xunta heritage report, already names this street as the way to Fisterra. Then comes the oak grove of San Lourenzo, with oaks the Xunta calls centenarian, and the countryside that the poet Rosalía de Castro wrote about in *Follas novas* (1880).

The way drops to the River Sarela, where the Xunta notes the ruins of old tanneries on the bank. After that a stony path climbs to the first houses of Sarela de Abaixo. The same heritage report says that from the top of this steep final climb you see the towers of the cathedral for the last time. Stop and look: it is your last sight of the city.

Then the road is country: Villestro, Roxos, Alto do Vento, a medieval bridge with a rounded arch at Augapesada, and a hard climb to the top of the Mar de Ovellas, with views over the valley of A Maía. The first ~8.9 km, to Alto do Vento, are rated medium to high by the Xunta, which fits its high rating for the whole day.

The day's great sight is the bridge at Ponte Maceira over the Tambre, which the Xunta calls the most outstanding bridge of this Camino. The heritage report says it was built at some point in the 15th century, and that people crossed by ferry at about the same spot before that. Spain's official tourism website calls the village one of the most beautiful in Spain, with an old mill, a dam and the chapel of San Blas. Cross it slowly.

Negreira comes soon after. The Xunta calls it the largest town, with over 2,000 inhabitants, that the pilgrim crosses before reaching the coast. At the entrance, the coat of arms of the Pazo de A Chancela shows a bridge: the one, according to Book III of the Codex Calixtinus, that was destroyed to stop the soldiers pursuing the disciples of St James. Hemingway mentions the town in *For Whom the Bell Tolls*, a fact the Xunta and Spain's tourism website both note. You pass the chapel of San Mauro and the Pazo do Cotón on the way through.

Go gently on the first day. It has a hard climb in it, and tomorrow is long.

### Day 2: Negreira to Olveiroa (~34 km)

This is the longest stage of the road. The Xunta rates it medium and estimates ~7 h 45 min. Spain's official tourism website agrees that it is the longest and calls the slopes gentle. It is not a day to start late.

Several stretches, the Xunta says, follow the old Camiño Real to Fisterra, and the place names show it: Camiño Real, Portocamiño. You cross the River Barcala on leaving Negreira and climb to the hamlet of Negreiroa, which the Xunta calls the original nucleus of Negreira. Then the road is countryside: gorse, cornfields, pine and eucalyptus, hórreos (the Xunta singles out those of As Maroñas) and Romanesque churches, such as that of Santa Mariña. After O Cornado you leave the municipality of Negreira for the region of Xallas.

Mount Aro, at ~556 m according to the Xunta, is the high point named in its text: a viewpoint over the Terra de Xallas, a country the Xunta says is known for basketry and for its women's straw hats. After the viewpoint of Corzón and the village of Mollón you reach the bridge over the River Xallas, the boundary between the municipalities of Mazaricos and Dumbría. Then A Ponte Olveira and the centre of Olveiroa, where the stage ends.

The heritage report lists the stops that were obligatory on the old royal road to Corcubión and Fisterra, and they are the places you pass: Augapesada, Ponte Maceira, As Maroñas, Ponte Olveira, Olveiroa. It also names travellers who left an account of this ground, such as Erich Lassota in the 1580s and Domenico Laffi in 1673.

Carry food and water for this day, and eat before you are hungry. Whether a place along the way is open is a question for the app and the phone. If the day is too long for you, the chapter on variants shows other ways to cut it.
