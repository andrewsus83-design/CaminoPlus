# To the sea: Olveiroa to Fisterra

## The character of these days

The third day brings the sea into view, and the fourth puts you beside it. The two days together are about as long as the long day behind you: ~20.7 km and ~14.2 km by the Xunta's figures, which add up to ~34.9 km. The Concello de Fisterra, whose own page counts three stages to the cape, runs the whole stretch from Olveiroa to Fisterra as one stage of ~33 km. This book follows the Xunta and takes it in two.

### Day 3: Olveiroa to Corcubión (~21 km)

The Xunta rates this stage medium to high and estimates ~4 h 25 min. You leave Olveiroa with the Xallas flowing below you, hemmed in by trees, cross the River Hospital, pass through O Logoso and reach the hamlet of Hospital. It once had a modest pilgrims' hospital, which has gone. Shortly after, at a roundabout, the road forks. This is the fork of the ways to Fisterra and to Muxía, in the Xunta's words. If you take the Fisterra branch you walk to the sea first, and this chapter follows it. The ways to take the other branch are in the chapter on variants.

After the fork the route leads towards the sanctuary of Nosa Señora das Neves, Mount Ousado and the hermitage of San Pedro Mártir, in the parish of A Pereiriña. Then, on the heights of the Cruceiro da Armada, at ~247 m, the Xunta says you can see Cape Fisterra for the first time, still far off. Spend a minute there. It is the first sight of where you are going.

A steep descent over the ría of Corcubión brings you to Cee. The Xunta calls it the first town of the region that the pilgrim reaches, and the largest municipality on the Costa da Morte, with notable commercial activity. Corcubión is next door. Its old quarter has been declared a historic-artistic complex, and the Xunta points to the church of San Marcos, in the Maritime Gothic style, from the end of the 15th century. It holds an image of the patron of the town that was made in northern Italy in the late 15th or early 16th century.

The heritage report calls Corcubión the most important town and port of that stretch of coast, and says the royal road from Santiago ran to it. From Corcubión to Fisterra, then an insignificant port in the report's words, there seems to have been no royal road at all.

### Day 4: Corcubión to Fisterra (~14 km)

The Xunta calls this a short stage with a high level of difficulty, ending at the lighthouse, and estimates ~3 h. It starts with a steep climb from the Campo do Rollo in Corcubión to the Campo de San Roque and the hamlet of O Vilar, passes through A Amarela and Estorde, and runs along stretches of the old Camiño Real at Sardiñeiro.

Then the coast takes over. The Xunta says that you cross a region with one of the most beautiful shorelines of the Iberian Peninsula: wide, calm sands cushioning a rocky shore, and a very rough sea that has left hundreds of stories and legends of shipwrecks. You skirt the Cala do Talón, pass Calcova and come to the long beach of Langosteira, with the town of Fisterra rising in the distance like an island. The marked path runs between the dunes, and the Xunta notes that many pilgrims choose to finish the stage on the beach. If you do, look at the tide first. The Concello de Fisterra publishes a tide page on its website.

Langosteira has a place in the old customs. The Concello's page on the old rites says the bath of purification was taken on this beach, two kilometres before the village, so that the pilgrim shook off the dust of the whole road. Spain's official tourism website calls it a good place for a swim, weather permitting. The sea here is rough, and the chapter on safety says what that means.

You enter Fisterra by the district of San Roque, pass the cruceiro of Baixar, and follow the Rúa Santa Catarina and the Rúa Real. Fisterra is an old port and fishing town, according to Wikipedia, and the Xunta says the road up to the lighthouse passes the church of Santa María das Areas, where the Santo Cristo of Fisterra is kept. The next chapter takes you up.

You may arrive with time to spare, because this is the short day. That is the point: the best light of the walk is at the end of it.
