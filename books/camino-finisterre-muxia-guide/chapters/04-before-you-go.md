# Before you go

## Your credencial

The *credencial* is the pilgrim's passport: a folded card with boxes for the stamps, or *sellos*, that you collect on the way. The Pilgrim's Office says the stamps are normally collected where pilgrims sleep, but can also be had in parish churches, monasteries, cathedrals, hotels, town halls and other places. It also says where to get an official card: from the confraternities of St James and from authorised associations, in Spain and abroad.

On this road the credencial matters for one reason. The Concello de Fisterra says the route you have walked is shown by the credencial, with the stamps or signatures of parishes, hostels and other places on the road. The pages read for this book do not say how many stamps are expected, or whether the stamps from your walk to Santiago count. Ask where you plan to collect the certificate, and carry a card with room on it. If you have just walked to Santiago, check how many boxes are left. A stamp from Santiago on the morning you leave is a good first one.

## Getting there, and getting back

The road starts in the Praza do Obradoiro, in front of the cathedral, as the Xunta says. The airport, Santiago-Rosalía de Castro, is ~12 km east of the city, according to Wikipedia.

Getting back needs thought. This book names no timetables, because they change. The Xunta runs a public transport website, bus.gal, where it says passengers can look up the times of its bus lines, and an older item in its news archive announces a line between Santiago, Cee and Fisterra. No source read for this book gives a current timetable for Fisterra or Muxía. Check before you leave Santiago, ask at your hostel or the tourist office the evening before you travel, and use the app. Do not plan your flight home for the afternoon of your last walking day.

## Training and packing

The Xunta's advice for every Camino applies: do not set off untrained. Take long walks in the weeks before you go, lengthen them gradually, and plan stages that match your fitness. With a longest day of ~34 km, a practice walk of ~20 km with your loaded pack, and two long days in a row, are a sensible test.

Keep the pack light. The Xunta says a correctly packed rucksack should not exceed 10 kg, with padded straps to prevent chafing. Its basics are a water bottle, footwear for the evening, high-protection sunscreen, a small first-aid kit, a phone with a charger, and your documents. It adds that a waterproof garment belongs in the pack at any time of year, and a cap is useful all year, for sun and for cold. The coast adds three things:

- **A windproof layer**, and a warm one for the cape, where the wind off the sea cools you fast, especially at dusk.
- **A head torch**, because the way down from the lighthouse can be after dark.
- **A bag for your boots, if you want to carry them home.** A later chapter says why.

## Shoes and feet

The Xunta is clear that footwear must be comfortable and already broken in, because new stiff shoes will hurt you. It recommends seamless socks, dry feet, and washing and drying socks at every stop. For anything that worries you, see a pharmacist or a doctor, and do it early.

## Money and language

The Xunta advises carrying your documents and cards rather than a lot of cash. Much of the road runs through hamlets, so carry a little cash for places that do not take cards.

Galicia has two official languages, Galician and Spanish. The Xunta's texts use the Galician forms of names, and so do the town councils' pages: Fisterra for Finisterre, Muxía for Mugía. You will also meet Galician words such as *rúa* (street), *praza* (square), *ponte* (bridge), *camiño* (path, way) and *hórreo* (the granary on stone legs). This book uses the Galician names and gives the Spanish ones where they help.
