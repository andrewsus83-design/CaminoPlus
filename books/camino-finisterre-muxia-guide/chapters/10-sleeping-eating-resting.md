# Sleeping, eating, resting

## Albergue life

An *albergue* is a pilgrims' hostel: a dormitory with bunk beds, a shared bathroom and often a place to wash your clothes. You show your credencial at the door and the host, the *hospitalero*, stamps it and tells you the house rules. The Xunta publishes a network of public hostels on its Camino portal, and the Concello de Fisterra refers to the municipal pilgrims' hostel in its town. Which places on this road have a hostel, and which are open this month, changes with the season, so this book lists none. The Xunta's stage pages also list hotels, guesthouses and rural lodgings. Use the app and the phone, and call ahead in the busy months.

The etiquette of a dormitory is short and kind.

- Arrive, claim a bed, shower, wash and hang out your clothes. The evening is for resting.
- Keep your voice low after the lights go out, and pack your rucksack in the hall, not in the dark by your bed.
- Use a head torch, not the main light. Silence your phone.
- Keep your pack and shoes off the bed.
- Earplugs are a small comfort that goes a long way. If you are the one who snores, apologise at breakfast.

Bedbugs can turn up in any shared bed. Look at the seams of the mattress before you lie down, keep your pack off the bed, and if you are bitten tell the host and wash and dry everything hot. If you are ill, tired or sleeping badly, take a private room. A good night is part of the walk.

## Eating

Many places along a Camino offer a *menú del peregrino*, a set meal for pilgrims. Ask what it includes before you sit down. For the long days, carry food: the stretch from Negreira to Olveiroa, the Xunta's longest, runs through country.

The sea shapes the table here, and the official sources say what to look for. Turismo de Galicia sends you to Fisterra's harbour first, to watch the fishing boats and, if you like, to eat razor clams or other local seafood. Spain's official tourism website names the fish and shellfish stews called *caldeiradas*, *empanadas*, local beef, *polbo á feira* (Galician octopus) and the *tarta de Santiago*, the Santiago tart.

The Xunta's health advice is to drink about two litres of water a day, and never to go beyond ~15 km without drinking. Fill your bottle whenever you can.

## Resting

A short road is easy to treat as a sprint. Do not. A day of rest is part of the walk, and this road has two natural places for one.

**Fisterra** is the first. Give the cape an evening, and the town a morning. The harbour, the church of Santa María das Areas and its Santo Cristo, the beach of Langosteira: all are worth a slow hour.

**Muxía** is the second. The sanctuary, the stones, Mount Corpiño at dusk and the fishing harbour make it a good place to stay an extra night and let the walk settle before you go back to Santiago.

On the road, listen to your body before your plan. If a pain grows instead of fading, stop, rest, and let a doctor look at it. You are not walking to prove anything.
